We would love to say that we are writing these lines while sitting on a Chinese high-speed train, travelling from Fenghuang to Shanghai at 350 km/h and eating <a href="https://whattocooktoday.com/soup-dumplings-xiao-long-bao.html" target="_blank"> xiaolongbao </a> ordered on Alipay with just a few clicks.

The truth is that I (<a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/giacomo-maggiore-499994263/" target="_blank">Giacomo</a>) am on a very sad Milan-Zurich Intercity train with no xiaolongbao, while I (<a href="https://substack.com/@giuliaelsaseverina?utm_source=global-search" target="_blank">Giulia, you can find her Substack here</a>) am lying down on the sofa sipping an espresso: only a few days after returning from the <b>Land of the Dragon</b> have we found the clarity and time to put down a few thoughts.

A necessary premise, to avoid any misunderstandings: every thought that follows is <b>extremely influenced by our experience.</b> Every comparison is made against our Western _"standard"_ and should not be understood in absolute terms.

### <b>The Big Brother</b>

In China, everything works. 

You open Alipay, link your credit card with a few clicks, enter your details and a photo of your passport, and you are all set.<br/>With a single app, you can access a restaurant menu, pay the lady stir-frying noodles in the street, activate public transport cards, message your friends and scroll through a few reels if you are bored _(at least in our experience; after all, we used the app to 1% of its potential)._

Similarly, as a Western tourist, <b>your passport is an all-access pass:</b> there are no tickets for trains or museums, every admission is linked to your identity, you buy everything online by entering your details and, _magically,_ enter the station or the Forbidden City by scanning your passport (or your face), with no need to print tickets or barcodes.

And yet, behind all this functionality lies an equally efficient and granular system of control. The moment every action is uniquely associated with a passport number _(or, for a Chinese citizen, an identity card),_ it becomes possible to <b>track and reconstruct every movement</b> and interaction of each person.
<br/>Moreover, if you add the extensive large-scale network of cameras _(cars photographed at every intersection and poles with webcams hanging from them),_ what happens online can easily be mapped and connected to offline life.

The result is a <b>system of control</b> capable of producing staggering quantities of data and relationships, with two natural consequences:
- the ability to aggregate data and identify <b>trends and patterns</b> that would otherwise be difficult to detect;
- the ability to <b>identify individual potentially dangerous behaviors</b> and intervene early with preventive measures.

Having read <a href="https://www.amazon.it/1984-Orwell/dp/8807903814" target="_blank">1984</a> a few years ago now, I realize how much this represents a rather dystopian present, but <s>(playing devil's advocate)</s> I wonder how much this view is shaped by my ideals.

As a European citizen, I put freedom and privacy first and, _at least at first glance,_ condemn a model in which the government knows what time I entered the Great Wall and what I ate two hours later.
<br/>China, by contrast, <b>has prioritized control, efficiency, and convenience</b>, managing to <b>"engineer"</b> life, _at least in the big cities,_ while fostering technological and economic growth and making life easier for its citizens.

I cannot deny that, besides being <b>fascinated from an engineering perspective</b> by this machine for collecting and analyzing data, all of this inevitably calls my ideals as a European liberal into question.

### <b>请不要放辣 (Qǐng bùyào fàng là)</b>

Walking through central Shanghai or Chongqing, <b>it is easy to forget that you are in the Far East</b>: McDonald's, Starbucks, Nike and Apple Stores, Zara and Prada are everywhere; people rush to work in Western outfits while Miley Cyrus songs blast from the bars.

Nevertheless, _except in a handful of cases in Shanghai,_ the Chinese population does not speak a word of English. I am not referring only to the lady selling grasshopper skewers at the market, but to the vast majority of people, including the young. Curiously, even the flight attendants on the Milan-Shenzhen flight, operated by a Chinese airline, struggled to understand my request: _“black tea, please”._

By "does not speak a word of English," we do not mean that conversation is difficult, but that you have to translate into Chinese words that now seem to have entered every vocabulary _(thank you, no, toilet, food, etc…)_ and that even the “gestures” we use in everyday communication are rarely understood.

The sad consequence is that <b>interactions are extremely complicated</b> and therefore stripped to the bare minimum: you communicate through volleys of Google Translate, typically writing a simple sentence in English _(with no subordinate clauses or redundant terms!),_ shoving the phone in the other person's face and waiting for them to do the same, trying to make the best sense of the smartphone's poor translation.

On the one hand, then, you see a country that has undergone a profound economic and social transformation in recent decades and that, while preserving its own cultural and institutional characteristics,<b> has incorporated many elements of global culture:</b> international brands and consumption patterns that do not seem so different from those of major Western cities. 

On the other hand, our experience seems to suggest that <b>globalization does not necessarily produce the same effects everywhere.</b> Openness to economic and cultural models does not imply linguistic homogenization; China seems to have incorporated some aspects of the model while maintaining strong autonomy over its linguistic tradition.

### <b>When China wakes, the world will tremble (Napoleon, 1816)</b>

In the early twentieth century, <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L0yCvHCk93w" target="_blank">Chongqing was a city of traditional wooden houses clinging to the rock</a>, with a population of around 200,000.<br/>Between 1938 and 1943, _during the Second Sino-Japanese War,_ the city was subjected to one of the most intense aerial bombing campaigns and effectively razed to the ground.

In 2026, <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0bxrlCZBTJE" target="_blank"> Chongqing is an autonomous municipality of over 30 million people,</a> one of the largest urban concentrations in the world, and is portrayed through its spectacular aesthetic: <b>a vertical megalopolis</b>, crossed by bridges and elevated highways, lit up by LEDs and capable of producing the <b>8D cyberpunk</b> imagery that has now become part of its tourist identity.

It is probably this discontinuity that makes Chongqing so difficult for a European tourist to decipher and appreciate.

Although it would be wrong to think that all of China looks like Chongqing, the city still allows you to grasp, _in a particularly extreme form,_ <b>the speed of the urbanization and economic development process</b> experienced by the country. 

Since the 1980s, China's GDP has grown by more than <b>9% a year</b>, hundreds of millions of people have escaped extreme poverty and in 2010 it overtook Japan to become the world's second-largest economy by nominal GDP.  

Although all of this has, _at least in the median case,_ brought greater socioeconomic well-being to the population, the sense is that the change has been <b>very rapid and violent</b>.<br/>First and foremost, _at least in the big cities,_ you can feel the <b>hyper-engineering</b> of every aspect of life and the use of mobile phones to do absolutely anything: in most places, you order through a QR code, human interaction is often minimal or absent, and you constantly get the impression that people are on their phones 24/7, often consuming extremely low-quality entertainment content _(at least by our standards)._

_[Several times at the supermarket, _i.e. at one of the many <a href="https://www.7-eleven.com/" target="_blank">7-Eleven </a>or FamilyMart stores,_ the cashiers did not even look up from their reels as they scanned the products; or, _even more dystopian,_ people scrolled through some Chinese social media platform while riding their bikes]._

### <b>What does "authentic" mean?</b>

Many tourist attractions are thoroughly organized around the visitor experience: shops and restaurants cluster around the main points of interest, creating a landscape in which the tourist and commercial dimensions end up overlapping almost completely. 

More than the mere presence of these businesses, what stands out is their <b>pervasiveness:</b> every element of the place seems to be part of the tourist experience _(and therefore an opportunity for consumption)._ 

<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4oIOTpfD3YQ" target="_blank">Furong Town, in Xiangxi Prefecture, Hunan,</a> embodies this model well. The town retains architectural and scenic elements <b>straight out of Kung Fu Panda:</b> old wooden houses, stone alleyways and a 70-meter waterfall dividing the town. And yet, once inside, <b>the view does not match the image.</b> The alleyways are entirely occupied by souvenir shops and restaurants promising the best rice tofu, while the buildings are often outlined with LEDs to put on an evening show.<br/>The entire complex sits behind a paid entrance and, just outside, a vast expanse of concrete houses parking lots, taxis and KFC.

The result is disorienting. <b>Not because the town is no longer “authentic” in any absolute sense</b> _(a concept that risks being rather arbitrary when judged by a foreign visitor who had never set foot in Furong before)_ but because its tourist image seems to have become almost inseparable from the way it is organized to welcome and entertain visitors.

This transformation probably says a great deal about China's relationship with tourism: <b>heritage preservation and its commercialization </b>merge until what belongs to history becomes indistinguishable from what was built to make money.

_In the same way,_ nature in the big cities seems subordinate to urbanization and progress: parks are limited to make room for more functional buildings and contact with nature is almost never part of the plan. _Even in natural parks,_ it remains impossible to lose yourself in the woods: every path is still paved, often surrounded by cameras; and travel in the mountains happens only<b> via elevators, buses, and cable cars</b> _(and even there, do not worry, you will find a vast expanse of concrete housing parking lots, taxis, and KFC)._
