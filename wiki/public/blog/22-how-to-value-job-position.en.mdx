I remember perfectly when, _on a sunny spring day and with a <a href="https://maps.app.goo.gl/nv3aNKPDQUJaEEEC9" target="_blank">slice of focaccia in hand</a>,_ I was walking along the seafront in <a href="https://maps.app.goo.gl/gn2oGNN29CmCiqak6" target="_blank">Recco</a>, discussing with close friends how to choose a job.

At the time _(even though it was less than a year ago),_ I still felt <b>fully immersed in student life:</b> I was working 15 hours a week as a <a href="https://kof.ethz.ch/it/" target="_blank">research assistant at ETH</a> _(one of the most stimulating experiences of my life!)_ and a full-time corporate job felt very far away.

Now things are very different: I am no longer employed _(unfortunately!)_ at a university doing research without having to worry about profits, I work _(fortunately!)_ about 25 hours a week at<a href="https://www.zurich.ch/en?pat=71700000061439939&gclsrc=aw.ds&gad_source=1&gad_campaignid=8774527800&gclid=Cj0KCQiA_P_XBhDvARIsALHjSUVwKgvu6pC7SNzAoB1lV3Z6eTeHwUmTtSRiB-_a8QBp-duDibPlgDQaAlBCEALw_wcB" target="_blank"> a real company </a>that needs to make a profit, and I am finishing the last exams of my Master's, preparing to look _(with considerable effort)_ for a full-time job!

_As a result,_ what once looked like idle talk about a life that did not concern me now feels more relevant. Although I know that, _given the difficult job market in Zurich today,_ you take what you can get, I have often thought about what<b> my ideal job </b>might be.

## <b>A (3+1)-dimensional space</b>

In mathematics, _before studying something,_ you always define a <b>measure function</b>. When modeling uncertain events, this means defining a probability space. _Similarly, in finance,_ evaluating a cash flow requires defining a discount rate.

_Forced comparisons aside,_ before drawing conclusions or evaluating a job, you need to build a <b>framework</b>. The one I imagined has 3 independent axes _(it lives in $\mathbb{R}^3$!),_ plus one optional.

Specifically, the three axes should be examined independently by answering the following questions:
- how closely do the <b>values of the company</b> (or institution) I work for align with mine?
- how stimulating, entertaining, and pleasant is the <b>work environment</b>?
- how stimulating, entertaining, and pleasant is the <b>individual work</b>?

To these we can add a fourth _(optional)_ question:
- how adequate is the <b>financial compensation</b>?

## <b>"There is only one social responsibility of business: to use its resources to increase its profits" (Milton Friedman)</b>

_In an ideal scenario,_ we would all want the company _(or institution)_ we work for to align perfectly with our values, even if they are deeply rooted, changeable over time or influenced by the people around us.

In reality, unfortunately, _at least in most cases,_ this does not happen and, _as a result,_ the first question becomes <b>"how willing are we to put our principles second?"</b>

Besides there being no right answer, it is often difficult to identify a company's values. Leaving aside the outliers _(i.e. weapons manufacturers, casinos, non-profit organizations, etc.),_ assessing a company's values depends closely on our own. _Moreover,_ it is often necessary to distinguish <b>what a company communicates from what it does.</b>

Consider <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/" target="_blank">Anthropic</a> (november 2026). Its home page reads <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/" target="_blank">"AI research and products that put safety at the frontier".</a>

How many people think of safety as a <b>founding value </b> of the company behind <a href="https://claude.ai/" target="_blank">Claude</a>?<br/>How many think it has had a <b>positive net effect</b> on society?

_Again,_ there is no right answer, but it is clear how much this depends on<b> 1)</b> the perspective adopted <b>2)</b> which lines of business are considered <b>3)</b> whose voice is heard.

_Moreover,_ it is often difficult to identify a <b>single set of values</b> because of the many stakeholders, investors, suppliers and especially customers a company interacts with.

Still considering Anthropic and its flagship product _(Claude),_ one could be perfectly aligned with its values when it comes to using AI to advance medicine, but _less so_ when it comes to producing viruses, cyberattacks or simple cheating at school.

_As if that were not enough,_ the visions, incentives, and parties involved change over time <b>in relation to the socio-political landscape:</b> <a href="https://about.meta.com/company-info/" target="_blank">Facebook </a>started as a site for looking at pictures of the hottest girls in college, evolved into a platform connecting people around the world through the internet and is now an advertising marketplace... _yet_ none of these changes happened overnight: when did Facebook <b>stop being good</b> and become a money-printing machine?

_Lastly,_ when assessing a company's values... let's not forget that its <b>primary goal is to make a profit.</b> _That's it._

_Then, depending on stakeholder/shareholder theory,_ we can add other <b>responsibilities and moral obligations</b> but, without the incentive of profit and the allocation of capital, the much-hated/much-loved/imperfect/[...] castle of <b>capitalism would collapse instantly.</b>

_Returning to the original question:_ how willing we are to compromise our values depends closely on our stage of life/career, our material needs at the time and how we <b>see and experience a company.</b>

## <b>"Coming together is a beginning, staying together is progress, but working together is success" (Henry Ford)</b>

Having assessed a corporation from the broadest, most general and philosophical perspective possible, let's not forget that <b>companies are made of people</b> and, _with some degree of mediation and abstraction,_ values depend on people.

Those same people, _inevitably,_ also determine the work environment and influence each of our _intra-company_ and _inter-company_ relationships.

Starting from the assumption _(whether we like it or not)_ that the workplace is where we spend <s>all</s> a good part of the day, I think it makes sense to ask whether the environment in which we work has a <b>positive/negative impact</b> on short-term well-being and long-term happiness _(even for those who see a 9-to-6 job purely as a means of earning a living)_.

_Specifically,_ two main parameters come to mind for assessing the environment around us. <br/>_First,_ a good dose of <b>fun</b> has never killed anyone. _From my very brief work experience,_ even though I am a <s>moderately</s> unsociable person, I am convinced that having colleagues _(who do not necessarily have to become lifelong friends)_ with whom you can joke, chat about this and that, and above all have a <b>relationship beyond Outlook and MS Teams</b>, can make the day better.

_Second,_ being surrounded by <b>intelligent people </b>with passions that, _whether or not they overlap with your own,_ extend beyond the business model helps create an <b>intellectually stimulating environment</b> _(in a very broad sense)_ that adds value to your experience in the long run.

_In this case,_ I think that the less personal interests are correlated with the company's business model, the more this diversity creates a <b>"rich" environment</b> and helps prevent us from becoming "isolated" in our own bubble.<br/>_On the other hand,_ I cannot deny that being surrounded by people who <b>1)</b> probably have a similar education and mindset <b>2)</b> share the same distinctive interests can help you <b>specialize</b> even further _(whether that is good or bad remains to be seen)_.

On this point, I think the company itself _(now understood as a higher entity, i.e. in this case the board or management)_ can influence all of this by encouraging exchanges and activities that go beyond pure economic profit and that, _more or less explicitly,_ aim to create a pleasant environment in which to work and, consequently, <b>attract talent</b> _(again: in this case the company does not do it out of philanthropy, but out of the foresight to recognize a connection between a stimulating workplace and long-term economic success)_.

_Last but not least,_ and closely dependent on the type of work and sector, a necessary _(but not sufficient)_ condition for an attractive work environment is the opportunity to <b>learn, grow</b> and see your work <b>valued</b> and <b>respected</b> by others.

## <b>“Is life not a hundred times too short to be bored?” (Nietzsche)</b>

Closely related to the previous point, the more granular aspect to consider is <b>your own work</b>, understood as the "set of practical activities you carry out"; setting aside _(as far as possible, since most jobs involve constant interaction with others)_ company values, the work environment and colleagues.

The starting point here, _without wanting to sound classist,_ is that you can work at the best company in the world, with the best colleagues in the world and still have a <b>shit job</b> _(literally: cleaning toilets)_.

While being aligned with the company's principles and feeling comfortable with the team are important factors, it is even more important to be <b>"challenged", stimulated</b> and <b>"engaged"</b> in something that interests you personally, _in short:_ <b>not to be bored.</b>

_Depending on your inclinations,_ this means different things: for some it means teaching medieval history in middle school, for others designing rockets _(or tanks)_... for me it simply comes down to working with numbers and giving them meaning.

Although I am generally opposed to the idea <b>"find a job you love and you will never work a day"</b> _(I am more in the camp of: <a href="https://www.amazon.com/clp/1455509124?lv=shuf&channelId=500" target="_blanK">"find something you are good at and you will fall in love with it"</a>, pages could be written about this)_ I see a grain of <s>truth</s> reality in it: 8 hours a day is a lot, and the idea of spending them bored terrifies me.

I am aware, however, that merely being able to worry about this aspect _(and fantasize about an ideal job in general)_ puts me in a <b>privileged position</b> _(I do not think people who clean toilets chose to do so)_ and that, _although it may not seem so,_ figuring out what we like doing _(not in words, not for status, not for money)_ is <b>much harder</b> than one might think.

Most jobs are assessed according to <b>more "material"</b> parameters: salary, type of company, the status quo and the effort involved in adapting, geographical location, etc., etc.... and, _partly because it is difficult to know in advance,_ people are less likely to look in detail at what they will be doing on a daily basis.

_As if that were not enough,_ most options that fit one parameter well <b>risk conflicting</b> with others: you can easily have an intellectually stimulating job serving an unpleasant purpose, just as you can have your best friends at the next desk while doing an extremely boring job.

## <b>It is the wand that chooses the wizard, Mr. Potter. </b>

If, _on top of all this,_ we add the<b> salary</b> variable to the system, things become even more complicated.

I like to think of salary as something that goes beyond its purely economic value: receiving money for your effort/time/intellect can be <b>extremely satisfying</b>, _regardless of the utility we assign to the sum of money,_ because it measures _(taking into account all the inefficiencies and negative externalities)_ how much society <b>values your work.</b>

Here I am referring to society at large rather than an individual company because, _living in a world of limited resources and assuming an efficient and competitive labor market,_ the salary paid by a company is, _to prevent arbitrage,_ closely related to what <b>the rest of the market offers.</b>

_To sum things up...._ even setting aside the need to pay rent and put food on the table _(i.e. assuming we have no material needs),_ <b>finding a single optimal solution is often impossible</b> or, _in other words,_ the function $u(x,y,z,\theta)$ that quantifies the utility of a job, where each parameter quantifies the answer to a question about the dream job, <b>has no global maximum.</b>

Introducing all the <b>imperfections</b> and <b>inefficiencies</b> of the real world, however... the problem looks different.

We do not live in $\mathbb{R}^3$, but in a labor market that offers <b>few options</b> with <b>discrete parameters</b> that can therefore often be compared 1:1, _which is why_ making comparisons becomes much more feasible.

It is rare to have the opportunity to compare <b>all</b> possible jobs, in <b>all </b> possible configurations according to the <b>3+</b> parameters considered: the comparison is often <b>constrained by the available options</b> and influenced by clear cases where one option is superior or by material needs that, _unfortunately,_ make my <b>discussion excessively idealistic</b>.

_Similarly,_ on the other side, the same problem arises: <b>how does a company assess a person?</b> Can similar questions be asked? <br/>_Probably,_ but that is another story _(and one on which I have even less say!)_.

The fact remains, however, that the one holding the upper hand is _(often)_ not the candidate... but rather it is the <b>job that chooses the person and not the other way around</b> <s>Mr. Potter!</s>.
