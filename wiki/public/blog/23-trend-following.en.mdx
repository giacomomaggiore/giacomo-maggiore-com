<b>Trend following is very simple</b>: long exposure to assets that have performed well in the past and short exposure to those that have <s>sucked</s>performed less well.

_In an ideal market,_ its role in asset allocation is well documented in academic research and equally simple: further <b>diversification</b> away from stocks and bonds and a source of returns in <b>low-growth regimes.</b>

Once all the frictions of financial markets are introduced, however... things get more complicated. _First of all,_ retail investors _(depending on their country of residence)_ often have limited access to products that replicate the strategy at _relatively high_ costs and with little transparency about their underlying holdings.

There is no <b>single, unambiguous definition</b> of trend following: every fund has its own methodology, implementation, investment universe, cost structure and so on... _as a result,_ it has always been difficult to <b>1)</b> find a single benchmark and <b>2)</b> understand exactly what was happening at the _position level._

_The curiosity remains, however:_ how does <b>"buy what is doing well, short the opposite"</b> translate into actual trades?

To answer that, I simulated a strategy loosely inspired by Clenow's book <a href="https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Following+the+Trend%3A+Diversified+Managed+Futures+Trading%2C+2nd+Edition-p-9781119908982" target="_blank"> "Following the Trend: Diversified Managed Futures Trading"</a>, implementing the same long/short logic across a smaller universe of futures contracts and running the usual analyses.

## <b>Surfing in the USA</b>
The strategy itself combines two different signals to build long and short positions. 
_First,_ a <b>filter based on exponential moving averages </b> determines whether the instrument is in an upward or downward trend. 

_Next,_ in an upward trend, a <b>long position </b> is entered if the price reaches or exceeds the highest of the previous $l$ closing prices. _Conversely,_ in a downward trend, a <b>short</b> position is entered if the price reaches or falls below the local low over the same window. If there are no entry or exit signals, the current position is maintained _(note that the strategy can therefore remain uninvested)._ <br/>The exposure _(sizing)_ for each instrument is then determined by its historical realized volatility relative to the portfolio's target volatility.

In formulas, let $A_t$ be the <a href="https://www.quantconnect.com/docs/v2/writing-algorithms/universes/futures#12-Continous-Contracts" target="_blank">Panama-adjusted </a> closing price at time $t$. Let $\mathrm{EMA}_{m,t}$ denote the long exponential moving average and $\mathrm{EMA}_{n,t}$ the short one, with $m>n$. The trend direction is $T_t$:

$$
T_t = \begin{cases}
+1, & \mathrm{EMA}_{n,t} > \mathrm{EMA}_{m,t}, \\
-1, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

For a window of $w$ trading sessions ($w=l$ for entry, $w=s$ for exit), the high and low exclude the current close:

$$
H_t^{(w)} = \max_{1 \le k \le w} A_{t-k}, \qquad
L_t^{(w)} = \min_{1 \le k \le w} A_{t-k}.
$$

The position at the next available close is therefore:

$$
p_{t+1} = \begin{cases}
+1, & p_t = 0,\ T_t = +1,\ A_t \ge H_t^{(l)}, \\
-1, & p_t = 0,\ T_t = -1,\ A_t \le L_t^{(l)}, \\
0, & p_t = +1,\ A_t \le L_t^{(s)}, \\
0, & p_t = -1,\ A_t \ge H_t^{(s)}, \\
p_t, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$


_Next,_ for each contract $j$, the change in value $D_{j,t}$ is obtained by multiplying the change in the adjusted closing price by the point value expressed in USD _(for contracts in other currencies, the price is first converted to USD)_.

$$
D_{j,t} = (A_{j,t}-A_{j,t-1})\,\mathrm{PV}^{\mathrm{USD}}_{j,t}.
$$

At close $t$, the preliminary weight $u_{j,t}$ is the direction to be executed divided by $\sigma_{j,t-1}$ _(standard deviation estimated up to the previous close)_. 

The overall risk $\sigma_{B,t}$ of all contracts in the market _(before setting contract quantities)_ is then estimated by applying the current weights to the previous $v$ trading sessions:

$$
u_{j,t} = \frac{p_{j,t}}{\sigma_{j,t-1}}, \qquad
B_{\tau;t} = \sum_j u_{j,t} D_{j,\tau}, \qquad
\sigma_{B,t} = \operatorname{sd}(B_{t-v;t},\ldots,B_{t-1;t}).
$$

When risk is positive and the benchmark risk is available, the <b>target number of contracts</b> is then obtained as follows:

$$
N^*_{j,t} = u_{j,t}\,
\frac{\mathrm{NAV}_{t-1}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{benchmark},t-1}}{\sigma_{B,t}}.
$$

Here, $\sigma_{\mathrm{benchmark},t-1}$ is the standard deviation of the benchmark's daily returns _(MSCI World in this case)_ over the last $v$ sessions available at the previous close, multiplied by a scaling constant _($=1$ in the base case)_.

Details and a Python implementation of the strategy are available in the <a href ="https://github.com/giacomomaggiore/financial-analyses/blob/main/05_trend_following_futures.ipynb" target="_blank">project's GitHub repo.</a>

## <b>Can the flap of a butterfly's wings in Brazil cause a tornado in Texas?</b>


First, I ran a backtest using the following parameters:
- exponential moving averages of $m=100$ sessions (long) and $n=50$ sessions (short)
- local highs and lows over $l=100$ sessions for entry and $s=50$ sessions for exit
- target daily volatility equal to that of the MSCI World, estimated over $v=63$ sessions
- interest on cash equal to 100% of the effective federal funds rate
- drag on futures notional of 0.20% per year as a proxy for the cost of rolling at expiry
- uniform transaction costs of 5 bp of traded notional
- daily rebalancing and sizing based on daily volatility over 63 sessions

The <a href="https://app2.msci.com/products/index-data-search/" target="_blank">MSCI World Net Return USD</a> index was used as the benchmark _(dividends reinvested net of withholding taxes)_. Accordingly, management fees _(TER)_ are not modeled for either portfolio.

_Beyond the higher Sharpe and CAGR..._ the most interesting things lie elsewhere. 

_First,_ there is the <b>strong lack of correlation</b> with the equity market, especially when stocks _sucked:_ during the dot-com bubble, the GFC, and the US credit downgrade, the strategy held up. 

From 2016 to 2020, _by contrast,_ there was no particular outperformance; during the post-Covid equity rally it could not keep up at all and, _unsurprisingly, given the inclusion of commodity and alternative asset class futures,_ it posted extremely positive returns during the supply crisis following the Russia-Ukraine war.

On the other hand, note that the strategy experienced <b>longer-lasting drawdowns </b> on average than an equity portfolio _(and even more so than a diversified stock and bond portfolio),_ further supporting the idea that, _regardless of the specific implementation,_ the two strategies should not be analyzed as standalone, mutually exclusive portfolios but, _as research widely suggests,_ as exposures to <b>different risk factors </b> that can offset each other and work well together.

_A side note:_ reading around, it seems instead that holding systematic or entirely passive strategies _(invoking the fascinating <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Efficient-market_hypothesis">efficient market hypothesis... </a>which I strongly believe in!)_ has become a <b>matter of identity</b> and binary choices rather than rational reasoning based on numbers and historical data _(we could now discuss the explanatory value of backtests, but that enters dangerous territory between statistics, macroeconomics, and philosophy)._

![Drawdowns and rolling cumulative returns of trend following and the MSCI World Net Return USD index](/images/blog/trend-drawdowns-rolling-returns.png)

*Drawdowns from previous highs (top) and cumulative returns over rolling five-year windows (bottom).*

_As mentioned earlier,_ since there is no unambiguous definition of a trend, the strategy is highly<b> sensitive to the assumptions </b>made during the backtest and, _in live trading,_ to the <b>conditions and frictions </b> of financial markets. _Unlike a buy-and-hold strategy in stocks and bonds,_ there are many levers to play with here: even setting aside the length of the moving averages and the windows for highs and lows, many other parameters remain to be calibrated.

![Sensitivity of CAGR, volatility, and Sharpe to backtest parameters](/images/blog/trend-sensitivity.png)

*Sensitivity of CAGR, volatility, and Sharpe to roll cost, cash interest rate, target risk, and rebalancing frequency varying one parameter at a time.*

_First, and unsurprisingly,_ <b>target volatility</b> can significantly change the portfolio's risk/return profile, following the usual rule that risk $\implies$ return.
<br/>Rebalancing frequency, _by contrast,_ has <b>nonlinear effects on the performance</b> of the portfolio, especially when combined with nonzero transaction costs: _ceteris paribus,_ CAGR and Sharpe peak with approximately weekly rebalancing, while volatility remains fairly constant.<br/>
_Similarly,_ <b>transaction costs </b>and especially<b> annual drag </b> have a significant impact on performance, an unsurprising result but one that is particularly relevant to retail investors, given that they translate into "imperfect" market implementation and nonzero management costs.

## <b>Smells like a yen carry trade</b>

Breaking down performance by sector and long/short exposure also shows how different contracts contribute differently to the portfolio.

![Net contribution of futures by market and position direction](/images/blog/trend-market-attribution.png)

*Net contribution to cumulative return, in percentage points, by market and long or short direction.*

S&P 500 futures, _for example,_ do particularly well with long exposure because of their upward trajectory in recent years, as do, _in general,_ futures that experienced strong rallies the strategy was able to capture. _Conversely,_ short positions in the yen, natural gas, and the pound benefited from falling markets.

It is also interesting to see that, adding up short and long contributions across all futures contracts, long positions contribute _(net of transaction costs)_ more than half of the portfolio's total return, while short positions and interest earned play a<b> more marginal role</b>. 

_Nevertheless,_ these remain the baseline of the portfolio because <b>1)</b> they provide <b>diversification away from the equity market</b> and <b>2)</b> allow long positions to profit when the opportunity arises. <br/>Without short exposures, the strategy would collapse into<b> long-only market timing</b> with greater exposure to market beta, losing its ability to diversify away from stocks and bonds.

_To sum up..._ there are a thousand ways to improve the proposed strategy and make it more <b>robust</b>, for example by reducing asset turnover, estimating volatility more accurately, or simply calibrating signal parameters or position sizing; yet I think that, when implementing it directly, it is much more useful to first understand the <b>role</b> one wants trend following to play within the overall allocation.

Instead of modeling it as a standalone strategy, each change should be made with a <b>more holistic view of the portfolio</b>, _i.e._ optimizing the strategy by considering only its added value within an already diversified portfolio, rather than evaluating $\alpha$ and volatility solely for the trend component.

This remains difficult, however, because it is <b>extremely individual</b>: _at least in theory,_ it would require starting from one's personal, very long-term asset allocation and, _once risk aversion, needs, and wealth objectives have been determined,_ developing a trend strategy that includes stocks, bonds, trend, and _[asset classes of choice]_ in line with one's outlook.
