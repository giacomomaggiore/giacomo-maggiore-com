## <a id="introduction"></a>Introduction

Asset management is about investing in assets to achieve certain goals subject to restrictions.

Wealth management determines the goals and restrictions.

Risk management makes sure the restrictions are satisfied.

- SAA (Strategic Asset Allocation): allocation to asset classes that should be followed in the long term. Possible approaches include:
    - Mean-variance analysis
    - Mean-downside-risk analysis (Ortec), which focuses on an investment's expected return (the mean) relative to its potential losses, ignoring positive gains entirely.
- TAA (Tactical Asset Allocation): tactical deviations from the SAA on a short-term (monthly) basis. Possible approaches include:
    - Passive rebalancing on an event-driven or time-frequency basis
    - Quantitative analysis (fundamental monetary macroeconomic (FMM) view)
    - Quantitative analysis based on regression
    - Rank-based
- IAA (Investment Asset Allocation): implementation of the TAA through investments
- Hedging: an overlay on the SAA to satisfy restrictions and risk constraints.
    - FX hedging: the standard rule is to:
        - hedge nominal assets
        - partially hedge real assets.
        - Example: typically, FX hedging for a CHF investor reduces volatility while preserving the mean return.
    - Tail-risk hedging: used when the payoff function is asymmetric.

Different types of asset allocation have different explanatory power for observed returns:
- SAA explains 75% of observed returns
- TAA explains 20%
- IAA explains 5%

Furthermore, they differ in their quantitative/qualitative approaches: from SAA (the most qualitative), we move through TAA and IAA to pure trading (the most quantitative).

### Performance Measures

We define the arithmetic average of returns as:

$$
\bar{R}_{\mathrm{arith}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} R_t
$$

In contrast, the geometric average return is:

$$
\bar{R}_{\mathrm{geo}} = \left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t)\right)^{1/T} - 1
$$

---

The time-weighted return (TWR) measures the average return for a fixed amount $X$ over $t = 1, 2, \ldots, T$:

$$
\mathrm{TWR} = \left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t)\right)^{1/T} - 1
$$

In contrast, the money-weighted return (MWR), also known as the internal rate of return (IRR), measures the average return when variable amounts $X_t$ are invested over $t = 1, 2, \ldots, T$. It is computed by solving for the rate that satisfies:

$$
\sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{CF_{\mathrm{in},t}}{(1 + \mathrm{MWR})^t}
= \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{CF_{\mathrm{out},t}}{(1 + \mathrm{MWR})^t}
$$

## <a id="1"></a>Strategy Development

Finding ideas is the hard part of strategy development. Conceptually, one can see returns as coming from three sources:
- <b>asset classes</b>
- <b>strategy styles</b> (carry, value, trend, volatility)
- <b>underlying risk factors</b>

For example, here are three sources of returns for some common strategies:
- <b>constant-allocation strategies</b>: earn risk premia on asset classes over time. The allocation depends on the (unconditional) risk premium, the level of risk, and the timing of losses.
- <b>timing strategies</b>: rotate among asset classes, with position sizes depending on conditional risk premia and estimates.
- <b>selection strategies</b>: take long and short positions within an asset class. They usually _do not target_ risk premia, but returns provided by strategy styles.

Testing the strategy is an essential part of the process. Ideally, you should know what to test before downloading the data. Common sources of data are:
- <b>general data sources</b> (Bloomberg, Datastream, Yahoo Finance)
- <b>specialized sources</b> (CRSP, Compustat, OptionMetrics)

It is important to be aware of biases and to make sure that dividends and interest are accounted for.

### Returns

We distinguish ordinary returns $R_{n,t}$ and log returns $r_{n,t}$. If an asset $n$ has price $P_{n,t}$ at time $t$ and pays interest or dividends (net of tax, for simplicity) $D_{n,t}$, the <b>ordinary return</b> is:
$$
R_{n,t} = \frac{P_{n,t} + D_{n,t}}{P_{n,t-1}} - 1
$$

<b>Log returns</b> are instead given by:

$$
r_{n,t} = \ln\left(\frac{P_{n,t} + D_{n,t}}{P_{n,t-1}}\right) = \ln(1 + R_{n,t})
$$

Suppose we invest an amount $V_0$ at $t = 0$ and earn $R_t$ or $r_t$ each period. After $T$ periods, we have:

$$
V_T = V_0 \prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t) = V_0 \exp\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t\right)
$$

We can also write <b>cumulative returns</b> $R_{0,T}$ and $r_{0,T}$:

$$
R_{0,T} = \frac{V_T}{V_0} - 1 = \prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t) - 1 \neq \sum_{t=1}^{T} R_t
$$

$$
r_{0,T} = \ln\left(\frac{V_T}{V_0}\right) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t
$$

_(Note that log returns are time-additive, whereas ordinary returns are not.)_

Furthermore, for ordinary returns, the average return per period is the <b>geometric mean</b>:

$$
\bar{R}_{0,T} = \left(\frac{V_T}{V_0}\right)^{1/T} - 1 = \left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t)\right)^{1/T} - 1
$$

For log returns, the average return per period is the <b>arithmetic mean</b>:

$$
\bar{r}_{0,T} = \frac{1}{T} \ln\left(\frac{V_T}{V_0}\right)
$$

But when should we consider ordinary versus log returns?

- <b>For realized returns</b>:
    - If the <b>arithmetic average ordinary return</b> is zero, you lose money unless every return is zero.
    - If the <b>geometric average ordinary return</b> is zero, you break even.
    - If the <b>arithmetic average log return</b> is zero, you break even.

For expected returns (assuming independent returns):
- For <b>ordinary returns</b>, if the expected one-period return is zero, then:
    - over a single period, on average, you break even;
    - over multiple periods, on average, you break even.
- For <b>log returns</b>, if the expected one-period log return is zero, then:
    - over a single period, on average, you make money;
    - the same applies over multiple periods.

Consider now a series of monthly returns with $m$ periods in a year. The average annual return solves

$$
1 + \bar{R}_{0,T}^{\text{annual}} = \left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t)\right)^{m/T}
$$

$$
\implies \bar{R}_{0,T}^{\text{annual}} = \left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t)\right)^{m/T} - 1 = (1 + \bar{R}_{0,T})^m - 1
$$

The average annual log return is instead:

$$
\bar{r}_{0,T}^{\text{annual}} = \frac{m}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t = m\bar{r}_{0,T}
$$

Suppose now that we want to decompose the overall return of the portfolio into the returns of $N$ assets, $n = 1, \ldots, N$, with weight $w_{n,t}$ in the portfolio.

The portfolio returns in period $t + 1$ are then:

$$
R_{p,t+1} = \sum_{n=1}^{N} w_{n,t}R_{n,t+1} = w_t^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t+1}
$$

$$
r_{p,t+1} = \ln(1 + R_{p,t+1})
$$

_(Note that ordinary returns aggregate in the cross section, but log returns do not; therefore):_
- if you want to compute the one-period return of a portfolio of assets, $\implies$ it is easier to work with ordinary returns;
- if you want to compute total returns or average returns over multiple periods, $\implies$ use log returns.
