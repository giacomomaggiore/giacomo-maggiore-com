## <a id="introduction"></a>Introduction

Asset management is about investing in assets to achieve certain goals subject to restrictions.

Wealth management determines the goals and restrictions.

Risk management makes sure the restrictions are satisfied.

- SAA (Strategic Asset Allocation): allocation to asset classes that should be followed in the long term. Possible approaches include:
    - Mean-variance analysis
    - Mean-downside-risk analysis (Ortec), which focuses on an investment's expected return (the mean) relative to its potential losses, ignoring positive gains entirely.
- TAA (Tactical Asset Allocation): tactical deviations from the SAA on a short-term (monthly) basis. Possible approaches include:
    - Passive rebalancing on an event-driven or time-frequency basis
    - Quantitative analysis (fundamental monetary macroeconomic (FMM) view)
    - Quantitative analysis based on regression
    - Rank-based
- IAA (Investment Asset Allocation): implementation of the TAA through investments
- Hedging: an overlay on the SAA to satisfy restrictions and risk constraints.
    - FX hedging: the standard rule is to:
        - hedge nominal assets
        - partially hedge real assets.
        - Example: typically, FX hedging for a CHF investor reduces volatility while preserving the mean return.
    - Tail-risk hedging: used when the payoff function is asymmetric.

Different types of asset allocation have different explanatory power for observed returns:
- SAA explains 75% of observed returns
- TAA explains 20%
- IAA explains 5%

Furthermore, they differ in their quantitative/qualitative approaches: from SAA (the most qualitative), we move through TAA and IAA to pure trading (the most quantitative).

### Performance Measures

We define the arithmetic average of returns as:

$$
\bar{R}_{\mathrm{arith}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} R_t
$$

In contrast, the geometric average return is:

$$
\bar{R}_{\mathrm{geo}} = \left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t)\right)^{1/T} - 1
$$

---

The time-weighted return (TWR) measures the average return for a fixed amount $X$ over $t = 1, 2, \ldots, T$:

$$
\mathrm{TWR} = \left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t)\right)^{1/T} - 1
$$

In contrast, the money-weighted return (MWR), also known as the internal rate of return (IRR), measures the average return when variable amounts $X_t$ are invested over $t = 1, 2, \ldots, T$. It is computed by solving for the rate that satisfies:

$$
\sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{CF_{\mathrm{in},t}}{(1 + \mathrm{MWR})^t}
= \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{CF_{\mathrm{out},t}}{(1 + \mathrm{MWR})^t}
$$

## <a id="1"></a>Strategy Development

Finding ideas is the hard part of strategy development. Conceptually, one can see returns as coming from three sources:
- <b>asset classes</b>
- <b>strategy styles</b> (carry, value, trend, volatility)
- <b>underlying risk factors</b>

For example, here are three sources of returns for some common strategies:
- <b>constant-allocation strategies</b>: earn risk premia on asset classes over time. The allocation depends on the (unconditional) risk premium, the level of risk, and the timing of losses.
- <b>timing strategies</b>: rotate among asset classes, with position sizes depending on conditional risk premia and estimates.
- <b>selection strategies</b>: take long and short positions within an asset class. They usually _do not target_ risk premia, but returns provided by strategy styles.

Testing the strategy is an essential part of the process. Ideally, you should know what to test before downloading the data. Common sources of data are:
- <b>general data sources</b> (Bloomberg, Datastream, Yahoo Finance)
- <b>specialized sources</b> (CRSP, Compustat, OptionMetrics)

It is important to be aware of biases and to make sure that dividends and interest are accounted for.

### Returns

We distinguish ordinary returns $R_{n,t}$ and log returns $r_{n,t}$. If an asset $n$ has price $P_{n,t}$ at time $t$ and pays interest or dividends (net of tax, for simplicity) $D_{n,t}$, the <b>ordinary return</b> is:
$$
R_{n,t} = \frac{P_{n,t} + D_{n,t}}{P_{n,t-1}} - 1
$$

<b>Log returns</b> are instead given by:

$$
r_{n,t} = \ln\left(\frac{P_{n,t} + D_{n,t}}{P_{n,t-1}}\right) = \ln(1 + R_{n,t})
$$

Suppose we invest an amount $V_0$ at $t = 0$ and earn $R_t$ or $r_t$ each period. After $T$ periods, we have:

$$
V_T = V_0 \prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t) = V_0 \exp\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t\right)
$$

We can also write <b>cumulative returns</b> $R_{0,T}$ and $r_{0,T}$:

$$
R_{0,T} = \frac{V_T}{V_0} - 1 = \prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t) - 1 \neq \sum_{t=1}^{T} R_t
$$

$$
r_{0,T} = \ln\left(\frac{V_T}{V_0}\right) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t
$$

_(Note that log returns are time-additive, whereas ordinary returns are not.)_

Furthermore, for ordinary returns, the average return per period is the <b>geometric mean</b>:

$$
\bar{R}_{0,T} = \left(\frac{V_T}{V_0}\right)^{1/T} - 1 = \left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t)\right)^{1/T} - 1
$$

For log returns, the average return per period is the <b>arithmetic mean</b>:

$$
\bar{r}_{0,T} = \frac{1}{T} \ln\left(\frac{V_T}{V_0}\right)
$$

But when should we consider ordinary versus log returns?

- <b>For realized returns</b>:
    - If the <b>arithmetic average ordinary return</b> is zero, you lose money unless every return is zero.
    - If the <b>geometric average ordinary return</b> is zero, you break even.
    - If the <b>arithmetic average log return</b> is zero, you break even.

For expected returns (assuming independent returns):
- For <b>ordinary returns</b>, if the expected one-period return is zero, then:
    - over a single period, on average, you break even;
    - over multiple periods, on average, you break even.
- For <b>log returns</b>, if the expected one-period log return is zero, then:
    - over a single period, on average, you make money;
    - the same applies over multiple periods.

Consider now a series of monthly returns with $m$ periods in a year. The average annual return solves

$$
1 + \bar{R}_{0,T}^{\text{annual}} = \left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t)\right)^{m/T}
$$

$$
\implies \bar{R}_{0,T}^{\text{annual}} = \left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t)\right)^{m/T} - 1 = (1 + \bar{R}_{0,T})^m - 1
$$

The average annual log return is instead:

$$
\bar{r}_{0,T}^{\text{annual}} = \frac{m}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t = m\bar{r}_{0,T}
$$

Suppose now that we want to decompose the overall return of the portfolio into the returns of $N$ assets, $n = 1, \ldots, N$, with weight $w_{n,t}$ in the portfolio.

The portfolio returns in period $t + 1$ are then:

$$
R_{p,t+1} = \sum_{n=1}^{N} w_{n,t}R_{n,t+1} = w_t^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t+1}
$$

$$
r_{p,t+1} = \ln(1 + R_{p,t+1})
$$

_(Note that ordinary returns aggregate in the cross section, but log returns do not; therefore):_
- if you want to compute the one-period return of a portfolio of assets, $\implies$ it is easier to work with ordinary returns;
- if you want to compute total returns or average returns over multiple periods, $\implies$ use log returns.

Furthermore, many strategies involve <b>long-short portfolios</b>; e.g., a momentum strategy will buy assets that performed well in the past and short assets that performed poorly.

Recall that, when we short a security:
- you borrow the security;
- you sell the security on the market, so you are credited for that sale;
- you have a liability to return the security and pay the lender a lending fee, as well as the interest and dividends;
- at some $t$, you buy back the security.

So, if you initially have $100$ in cash and then short a stock for $50$ and buy one for $80$, the result is the following:

| Assets | Liabilities |
| --- | --- |
| Stocks long $80$ | Short stock $50$ |
| Cash $70$ | Equity $100$ |
| Total $150$ | Total $150$ |

Therefore, the total return on the portfolio will be:

$$
\text{return on the long stock} + \text{interest on cash} - \text{return on the short stock} - \text{stock lending fees}
$$

If we denote by $w_t$ the share positions of assets _(positive for long, negative for short)_ and consider the riskless asset to be asset $0$, then we have:

$$
R_{p,t+1} = w_t^{\mathsf{T}} R_{t+1} + (1 - w_t^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{1}) R_{f,t+1} + \sum_{n=1}^{N} \min[w_{n,t}, 0] L_{n,t+1}
$$

where $L_{n,t+1}$ is the percentage lending fee from $t$ to $t+1$.

_Intuition: we apply the usual return formula but also take into account the <b>adjustment</b> for stock lending fees._

Note also that the adjustment may be hard to estimate precisely, so we can take a better approach and subtract an estimate of the average lending fee $\bar{L}$ multiplied by the overall fraction held short of the portfolio, that is:

$$
R_{p,t+1} = w_t^{\mathsf{T}} R_{t+1} + (1 - w_t^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{1}) R_{f,t+1} + \bar{L} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \min[w_{n,t}, 0]
$$

We could also keep track of the long and short legs separately with the constraint that weights sum to $1$; in this case, we think of the portfolio as a <b>zero-cost long-short portfolio</b>.

Consider $w_L \ge 0$ (long portfolio) and $w_S \ge 0$ (short portfolio); we then calculate:

$$
R_{p,t+1}^{L} = w_{L,t}^{\mathsf{T}} R_{t+1}
$$

$$
R_{p,t+1}^{S} = w_{S,t}^{\mathsf{T}} (R_{t+1} + L_{t+1})
$$

Then the net return on the zero-cost long-short portfolio is the difference:

$$
R_{p,t+1} = R_{p,t+1}^{L} - R_{p,t+1}^{S} = w_{L,t}^{\mathsf{T}} R_{t+1} - w_{S,t}^{\mathsf{T}} (R_{t+1} + L_{t+1})
$$

<b>Accounting for Dividends</b>

Recall that the return of asset $n$ with price $P_{n,t}$ at time $t$ that pays dividend $D_{n,t}$ is:

$$
R_{n,t} = \frac{P_{n,t} + D_{n,t}}{P_{n,t-1}} - 1
$$

_(When using Bloomberg to get data, you can use the `DPDF` function to make the necessary adjustments.)_

_Note also that <a href="https://www.unisg.ch/en/university/library/search-and-use/databases/crsp-center-for-research-in-security-prices-/-wrds/">CRSP</a> also provides returns that are adjusted for splits and dividends._

Additional adjustments are required if you are subject to withholding taxes on dividends or interest that are not creditable/refundable.

This can be particularly useful when a portfolio has short positions because:
- on long positions, you receive <b>dividends net of withholding tax</b>;
- on short positions, you have to pay <b>full dividends to the lender</b>.

Suppose the case when your long positions pay $x$ subject to tax $t\%$, and the short positions have dividends of $y$ (so you have to pay $y$). Without capital gains or losses, the return profile is:

$$
\text{Profit} = x - y - t x
$$

_Shortcut: to assess the impact of withholding taxes, simply subtract the dividend yield of long positions and withholding tax from your returns._

Furthermore, <b>cross-border investments</b> can create an additional complexity, since part of the foreign withholding tax can be refunded and part can be credited.

Example: US withholding tax on dividends.

If you are a non-US investor and own US stocks, the IRS generally imposes a 30% withholding tax on dividends paid by US companies.

However, most EU countries and Switzerland have tax treaties with the US: with a valid Form W-8BEN completed through your broker, the withholding tax is typically reduced from 30% to 15%.

For a <b>European investor</b> that wants to invest in US equities through ETFs, there are two alternatives:
- Holding a US ETF directly, with a reduced withholding tax of 15%
- Holding an Irish-domiciled ETF with the same withholding tax paid internally by the ETF.

While the solutions may look identical, the second way is much <b>more tax efficient</b>: there is no additional tax on the investor side, while with the first option, there may be additional tax-filing, estate-tax, or withholding-tax complications depending on the country.

On the other hand, _for the Swiss investor,_ the situation is different, since they can claim the 15% treaty withholding tax through the Swiss tax system and recover some or all of it, which is why a US-domiciled ETF can be more efficient, also because they sometimes come at lower costs and spreads.

We now illustrate the case where the domestic country <b>taxes income but not capital gains</b> (i.e., Switzerland).

Assume that capital gains $x = 900$, dividend income $y = 200$, dividends paid on shorts $z = -100$, and the domestic tax rate $t = 0.2$. Then we have the following:

| Case | Full crediting | Credit limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dividend income | 200 | 200 |
| Dividends paid on shorts | -100 | -100 |
| Capital gains | 900 | 900 |
| Pre-tax profit | 1000 | 1000 |
| Foreign withholding tax | -60 | -60 |
| Domestic income taxes | 20 | 0 |
| Profit after tax | 960 | 940 |

Note that domestic income taxes = $t \times (y + z)$.

Formally, let $V_t$ be the value of the portfolio before any taxes, $D_t$ the aggregate dividends, $\tau_d$ the domestic tax rate, and $\tau_f$ the non-refundable part of the foreign withholding tax. We have the following:

| Withholding tax on long dividends | $\tau_f D_t$ |
| --- | --- |
| Domestic dividend income tax | $\tau_d D_t$ |
| Tax credit | $-\min(\tau_f, \tau_d) D_t$ |
| Total tax cost | $\max(\tau_f, \tau_d) D_t$ |

_Intuition: you pay the higher of the two taxes, because the lower one can usually be credited against the higher one._


In terms of returns, the tax is:

$$
\max(\tau_f, \tau_d) \frac{D_t}{V_{t-1}}
$$

and so the <b>after-tax return</b> is:

$$
R_{p,t}^{\mathrm{net}} = R_{p,t} - \max(\tau_d, \tau_f) \frac{D_t}{V_{t-1}}
$$

Suppose now that the profits _(including capital gains)_ are subject to a domestic tax $\tau_d$, that is:

$$
R_{p,t}^{\mathrm{net}} = R_{p,t}(1 - \tau_d) - \max(\tau_f - \tau_d, 0) \frac{D_t}{V_{t-1}}
$$

Therefore, the drag on returns from taxation is the sum of:
- the product of the pre-tax return and the domestic tax rate $\tau_d$;
- the product of the dividend yield on long positions and the part of the tax that is not refundable.

<b>Portfolios with Futures Contracts</b>

We now consider the presence of <b>futures contracts</b>. Since they are <b>unfunded positions</b> (i.e., they do not require paying the full asset value upfront), the change in the futures price is settled in cash at the end of each trading day, so the value of the asset is zero and we _cannot really compute a return_.

Consider $R_{p,t+1}$, the portfolio return _excluding futures_, $F_t$, the futures price, and $N_f$, the <b>futures exposure</b> _(number of contracts times multiplier)_.<br/>In this case, the change in the total value of the portfolio is:

$$
V_{t+1} - V_t = V_t R_{p,t+1} + N_f(F_{t+1} - F_t)
$$

Therefore, the return with futures $R_{p,t+1}^{F}$ is:

$$
R_{p,t+1}^{F} = \frac{V_{t+1}}{V_t} - 1 = R_{p,t+1} + \frac{N_f F_t}{V_t}\frac{F_{t+1} - F_t}{F_t} = R_{p,t+1} + w_{F,t}R_{F,t+1}
$$

_(Note that the formula is similar to the previous one, but always remember that weights do NOT need to sum to 1.)_

However, when <b>futures positions are rolled</b> _(i.e., closing the current position before expiry and opening a new one with later expiry),_ we make sure to use a given futures contract and _not mix contracts_ with different maturities.

_Consider, for example, E-Mini S&P 500 Futures around the September 2026 expiry:_
- On September 17, we would use the September contract to compute returns.
- Starting from September 18, we would use the December contract.

One way to do this easily is to configure your code to determine which contract to use. With the _generic futures_ in Bloomberg, the contract is rolled automatically and, using the function <b>GDUT</b>, prices are adjusted automatically for rollover _(needed because futures prices differ across maturities and the price spread between maturities reflects the difference between the risk-free rate and the dividend yield)._

Formally, for an equity future, the <b>no-arbitrage</b> price is:

$$
F(T) = S_0 e^{(r-q)T}
$$

where $S_0$ is the stock's current price, $r$ is the continuously compounded risk-free rate, and $q$ is the dividend yield. The price difference between two maturities $T_1$ and $T_2$ is then:

$$
F(T_2) - F(T_1) = S_0\left(e^{(r-q)T_2} - e^{(r-q)T_1}\right)
$$

_However,_ note that futures returns will generally differ from the returns on price and total-return indices. In general, in the _absence of market frictions_, futures returns equal index returns _(including dividends)_ minus the risk-free rate:

$$
R_{\mathrm{futures}} \approx R_{\mathrm{total\ return\ index}} - r_f
$$

We now give a quick proof of this:

Suppose initially that the value of the underlying price index follows:

$$
dS_t = (\mu_t - q) S_t\,dt + \sigma_t S_t\,dB_t
$$

where $q$ is the dividend yield, $dB_t$ is <b>Brownian motion</b>, $\mu_t$ is the drift, and $\sigma_t$ is the volatility.

We also denote by $r$ the risk-free rate, so the price of futures maturing at $T$ is:

$$
F_t = S_t e^{(r-q)(T-t)}
$$

Using Itô's formula, we get:

$$
dF_t = dS_t e^{(r-q)(T-t)} - S_t (r-q) e^{(r-q)(T-t)}\,dt = \frac{dS_t}{S_t}F_t - (r-q)F_t\,dt
$$

$$
\frac{dF_t}{F_t} = \frac{dS_t}{S_t} - (r-q)\,dt = (\mu_t - r)\,dt + \sigma_t\,dB_t
$$

<b>Performance measurement</b>

The idea of performance measurement is to relate the portfolio's realized return to risk. Note that performance measures are usually based on <b>excess returns</b>, i.e., the return of the portfolio minus the return of the risk-free asset.

The <b>Sharpe ratio</b> gives the average return of the portfolio $\bar{R}_p$ in excess of the risk-free rate $\bar{R}_f$ per unit of total portfolio risk $\sigma_p$.

$$
\mathrm{SR}_p = \frac{\bar{R}_p - \bar{R}_f}{\sigma_p}
$$

It is an appropriate measure for an investor focused on the <b>full risk</b> of the portfolio. However, note that the Sharpe ratio:
- does not distinguish whether returns are generated by systematic or idiosyncratic risk;
- only considers the first two moments of the return distribution.
