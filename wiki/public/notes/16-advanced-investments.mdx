## <a id="1">Introduction to Asset Management: Asset Allocation, Hedging and Performance Measures</a>

Asset management is about investing in assets to achieve certain goals subject to restrictions.

Wealth management determines the goals and restrictions.

Risk management makes sure the restrictions are satisfied.

- SAA (Strategic Asset Allocation): allocation to asset classes that should be followed in the long term. Possible approaches include:
    - Mean-variance analysis
    - Mean-downside-risk analysis (Ortec), which focuses on an investment's expected return (the mean) relative to its potential losses, ignoring positive gains entirely.
- TAA (Tactical Asset Allocation): tactical deviations from the SAA on a short-term (monthly) basis. Possible approaches include:
    - Passive rebalancing on an event-driven or time-frequency basis
    - Quantitative analysis (fundamental monetary macroeconomic (FMM) view)
    - Quantitative analysis based on regression
    - Rank-based
- IAA (Investment Asset Allocation): implementation of the TAA through investments
- Hedging: an overlay on the SAA to satisfy restrictions and risk constraints.
    - FX hedging: the standard rule is to:
        - hedge nominal assets
        - partially hedge real assets.
        - Example: typically, FX hedging for a CHF investor reduces volatility while preserving the mean return.
    - Tail-risk hedging: used when the payoff function is asymmetric.

Different types of asset allocation have different explanatory power for observed returns:
- SAA explains 75% of observed returns
- TAA explains 20%
- IAA explains 5%

Furthermore, they differ in their quantitative/qualitative approaches: from SAA (the most qualitative), we move through TAA and IAA to pure trading (the most quantitative).

### Performance Measures

We define the arithmetic average of returns as:

$$
\bar{R}_{\mathrm{arith}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} R_t
$$

In contrast, the geometric average return is:

$$
\bar{R}_{\mathrm{geo}} = \left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t)\right)^{1/T} - 1
$$

---

The time-weighted return (TWR) measures the average return for a fixed amount $X$ over $t = 1, 2, \ldots, T$:

$$
\mathrm{TWR} = \left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t)\right)^{1/T} - 1
$$

In contrast, the money-weighted return (MWR), also known as the internal rate of return (IRR), measures the average return when variable amounts $X_t$ are invested over $t = 1, 2, \ldots, T$. It is computed by solving for the rate that satisfies:

$$
\sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{CF_{\mathrm{in},t}}{(1 + \mathrm{MWR})^t}
= \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{CF_{\mathrm{out},t}}{(1 + \mathrm{MWR})^t}
$$

## <a id="2">Strategy Development: Sources of Returns, Return Calculations, Performance Measurement and Timing Ability</a>

Finding ideas is the hard part of strategy development. Conceptually, one can see returns as coming from three sources:
- <b>asset classes</b>
- <b>strategy styles</b> (carry, value, trend, volatility)
- <b>underlying risk factors</b>

For example, here are three sources of returns for some common strategies:
- <b>constant-allocation strategies</b>: earn risk premia on asset classes over time. The allocation depends on the (unconditional) risk premium, the level of risk, and the timing of losses.
- <b>timing strategies</b>: rotate among asset classes, with position sizes depending on conditional risk premia and estimates.
- <b>selection strategies</b>: take long and short positions within an asset class. They usually _do not target_ risk premia, but returns provided by strategy styles.

Testing the strategy is an essential part of the process. Ideally, you should know what to test before downloading the data. Common sources of data are:
- <b>general data sources</b> (<a href="https://www.bloomberg.com/professional/" target="_blank">Bloomberg</a>, Datastream, <a href="https://finance.yahoo.com/" target="_blank">Yahoo Finance</a>)
- <b>specialized sources</b> (<a href="https://www.crsp.org/" target="_blank">CRSP</a>, Compustat, <a href="https://optionmetrics.com/" target="_blank">OptionMetrics</a>)

It is important to be aware of biases and to make sure that dividends and interest are accounted for.

### Returns

We distinguish ordinary returns $R_{n,t}$ and log returns $r_{n,t}$. If an asset $n$ has price $P_{n,t}$ at time $t$ and pays interest or dividends (net of tax, for simplicity) $D_{n,t}$, the <b>ordinary return</b> is:
$$
R_{n,t} = \frac{P_{n,t} + D_{n,t}}{P_{n,t-1}} - 1
$$

<b>Log returns</b> are instead given by:

$$
r_{n,t} = \ln\left(\frac{P_{n,t} + D_{n,t}}{P_{n,t-1}}\right) = \ln(1 + R_{n,t})
$$

Suppose we invest an amount $V_0$ at $t = 0$ and earn $R_t$ or $r_t$ each period. After $T$ periods, we have:

$$
V_T = V_0 \prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t) = V_0 \exp\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t\right)
$$

We can also write <b>cumulative returns</b> $R_{0,T}$ and $r_{0,T}$:

$$
R_{0,T} = \frac{V_T}{V_0} - 1 = \prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t) - 1 \neq \sum_{t=1}^{T} R_t
$$

$$
r_{0,T} = \ln\left(\frac{V_T}{V_0}\right) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t
$$

_(Note that log returns are time-additive, whereas ordinary returns are not.)_

Furthermore, for ordinary returns, the average return per period is the <b>geometric mean</b>:

$$
\bar{R}_{0,T} = \left(\frac{V_T}{V_0}\right)^{1/T} - 1 = \left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t)\right)^{1/T} - 1
$$

For log returns, the average return per period is the <b>arithmetic mean</b>:

$$
\bar{r}_{0,T} = \frac{1}{T} \ln\left(\frac{V_T}{V_0}\right)
$$

But when should we consider ordinary versus log returns?

- <b>For realized returns</b>:
    - If the <b>arithmetic average ordinary return</b> is zero, you lose money unless every return is zero.
    - If the <b>geometric average ordinary return</b> is zero, you break even.
    - If the <b>arithmetic average log return</b> is zero, you break even.

For expected returns (assuming independent returns):
- For <b>ordinary returns</b>, if the expected one-period return is zero, then:
    - over a single period, on average, you break even;
    - over multiple periods, on average, you break even.
- For <b>log returns</b>, if the expected one-period log return is zero, then:
    - over a single period, on average, you make money;
    - the same applies over multiple periods.

Consider now a series of monthly returns with $m$ periods in a year. The average annual return solves

$$
1 + \bar{R}_{0,T}^{\text{annual}} = \left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t)\right)^{m/T}
$$

$$
\implies \bar{R}_{0,T}^{\text{annual}} = \left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + R_t)\right)^{m/T} - 1 = (1 + \bar{R}_{0,T})^m - 1
$$

The average annual log return is instead:

$$
\bar{r}_{0,T}^{\text{annual}} = \frac{m}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t = m\bar{r}_{0,T}
$$

Suppose now that we want to decompose the overall return of the portfolio into the returns of $N$ assets, $n = 1, \ldots, N$, with weight $w_{n,t}$ in the portfolio.

The portfolio returns in period $t + 1$ are then:

$$
R_{p,t+1} = \sum_{n=1}^{N} w_{n,t}R_{n,t+1} = w_t^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t+1}
$$

$$
r_{p,t+1} = \ln(1 + R_{p,t+1})
$$

_(Note that ordinary returns aggregate in the cross section, but log returns do not; therefore):_
- if you want to compute the one-period return of a portfolio of assets, $\implies$ it is easier to work with ordinary returns;
- if you want to compute total returns or average returns over multiple periods, $\implies$ use log returns.

Furthermore, many strategies involve <b>long-short portfolios</b>; e.g., a momentum strategy will buy assets that performed well in the past and short assets that performed poorly.

Recall that, when we short a security:
- you borrow the security;
- you sell the security on the market, so you are credited for that sale;
- you have a liability to return the security and pay the lender a lending fee, as well as the interest and dividends;
- at some $t$, you buy back the security.

So, if you initially have $100$ in cash and then short a stock for $50$ and buy one for $80$, the result is the following:

| Assets | Liabilities |
| --- | --- |
| Stocks long $80$ | Short stock $50$ |
| Cash $70$ | Equity $100$ |
| Total $150$ | Total $150$ |

Therefore, the total return on the portfolio will be:

$$
\text{return on the long stock} + \text{interest on cash} - \text{return on the short stock} - \text{stock lending fees}
$$

If we denote by $w_t$ the share positions of assets _(positive for long, negative for short)_ and consider the riskless asset to be asset $0$, then we have:

$$
R_{p,t+1} = w_t^{\mathsf{T}} R_{t+1} + (1 - w_t^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{1}) R_{f,t+1} + \sum_{n=1}^{N} \min[w_{n,t}, 0] L_{n,t+1}
$$

where $L_{n,t+1}$ is the percentage lending fee from $t$ to $t+1$.

_Intuition: we apply the usual return formula but also take into account the <b>adjustment</b> for stock lending fees._

Note also that the adjustment may be hard to estimate precisely, so we can take a better approach and subtract an estimate of the average lending fee $\bar{L}$ multiplied by the overall fraction held short of the portfolio, that is:

$$
R_{p,t+1} = w_t^{\mathsf{T}} R_{t+1} + (1 - w_t^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{1}) R_{f,t+1} + \bar{L} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \min[w_{n,t}, 0]
$$

We could also keep track of the long and short legs separately with the constraint that weights sum to $1$; in this case, we think of the portfolio as a <b>zero-cost long-short portfolio</b>.

Consider $w_L \ge 0$ (long portfolio) and $w_S \ge 0$ (short portfolio); we then calculate:

$$
R_{p,t+1}^{L} = w_{L,t}^{\mathsf{T}} R_{t+1}
$$

$$
R_{p,t+1}^{S} = w_{S,t}^{\mathsf{T}} (R_{t+1} + L_{t+1})
$$

Then the net return on the zero-cost long-short portfolio is the difference:

$$
R_{p,t+1} = R_{p,t+1}^{L} - R_{p,t+1}^{S} = w_{L,t}^{\mathsf{T}} R_{t+1} - w_{S,t}^{\mathsf{T}} (R_{t+1} + L_{t+1})
$$

<b>Accounting for Dividends</b>

Recall that the return of asset $n$ with price $P_{n,t}$ at time $t$ that pays dividend $D_{n,t}$ is:

$$
R_{n,t} = \frac{P_{n,t} + D_{n,t}}{P_{n,t-1}} - 1
$$

_(When using <a href="https://www.bloomberg.com/professional/" target="_blank">Bloomberg</a> to get data, you can use the `DPDF` function to make the necessary adjustments.)_

_Note also that <a href="https://www.unisg.ch/en/university/library/search-and-use/databases/crsp-center-for-research-in-security-prices-/-wrds/">CRSP</a> also provides returns that are adjusted for splits and dividends._

Additional adjustments are required if you are subject to withholding taxes on dividends or interest that are not creditable/refundable.

This can be particularly useful when a portfolio has short positions because:
- on long positions, you receive <b>dividends net of withholding tax</b>;
- on short positions, you have to pay <b>full dividends to the lender</b>.

Suppose that your long positions pay $x$ subject to tax $t\%$, and the short positions have dividends of $y$ (so you have to pay $y$). Without capital gains or losses, the return profile is:

$$
\text{Profit} = x - y - t x
$$

_Shortcut: to assess the impact of withholding taxes, simply subtract the dividend yield of long positions and withholding tax from your returns._

Furthermore, <b>cross-border investments</b> can create an additional complexity, since part of the foreign withholding tax can be refunded and part can be credited.

Example: US withholding tax on dividends.

If you are a non-US investor and own US stocks, the IRS generally imposes a 30% withholding tax on dividends paid by US companies.

However, most EU countries and Switzerland have tax treaties with the US: with a valid <a href="https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-w-8-ben" target="_blank">Form W-8BEN</a> completed through your broker, the withholding tax is typically reduced from 30% to 15%.

For a <b>European investor</b> that wants to invest in US equities through ETFs, there are two alternatives:
- Holding a US ETF directly, with a reduced withholding tax of 15%
- Holding an Irish-domiciled ETF with the same withholding tax paid internally by the ETF.

While the solutions may look identical, the second way is much <b>more tax efficient</b>: there is no additional tax on the investor side, while with the first option, there may be additional tax-filing, estate-tax, or withholding-tax complications depending on the country.

On the other hand, _for the Swiss investor,_ the situation is different, since they can claim the 15% treaty withholding tax through the Swiss tax system and recover some or all of it, which is why a US-domiciled ETF can be more efficient, also because they sometimes come at lower costs and spreads.

We now illustrate the case where the domestic country <b>taxes income but not capital gains</b> (i.e., Switzerland).

Assume that capital gains $x = 900$, dividend income $y = 200$, dividends paid on shorts $z = -100$, and the domestic tax rate $t = 0.2$. Then we have the following:

| Case | Full crediting | Credit limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dividend income | 200 | 200 |
| Dividends paid on shorts | -100 | -100 |
| Capital gains | 900 | 900 |
| Pre-tax profit | 1000 | 1000 |
| Foreign withholding tax | -60 | -60 |
| Domestic income taxes | 20 | 0 |
| Profit after tax | 960 | 940 |

Note that domestic income taxes = $t \times (y + z)$.

Formally, let $V_t$ be the value of the portfolio before any taxes, $D_t$ the aggregate dividends, $\tau_d$ the domestic tax rate, and $\tau_f$ the non-refundable part of the foreign withholding tax. We have the following:

| Withholding tax on long dividends | $\tau_f D_t$ |
| --- | --- |
| Domestic dividend income tax | $\tau_d D_t$ |
| Tax credit | $-\min(\tau_f, \tau_d) D_t$ |
| Total tax cost | $\max(\tau_f, \tau_d) D_t$ |

_Intuition: you pay the higher of the two taxes, because the lower one can usually be credited against the higher one._


In terms of returns, the tax is:

$$
\max(\tau_f, \tau_d) \frac{D_t}{V_{t-1}}
$$

and so the <b>after-tax return</b> is:

$$
R_{p,t}^{\mathrm{net}} = R_{p,t} - \max(\tau_d, \tau_f) \frac{D_t}{V_{t-1}}
$$

Suppose now that the profits _(including capital gains)_ are subject to a domestic tax $\tau_d$, that is:

$$
R_{p,t}^{\mathrm{net}} = R_{p,t}(1 - \tau_d) - \max(\tau_f - \tau_d, 0) \frac{D_t}{V_{t-1}}
$$

Therefore, the drag on returns from taxation is the sum of:
- the product of the pre-tax return and the domestic tax rate $\tau_d$;
- the product of the dividend yield on long positions and the part of the tax that is not refundable.

<b>Portfolios with Futures Contracts</b>

We now consider the presence of <b>futures contracts</b>. Since they are <b>unfunded positions</b> (i.e., they do not require paying the full asset value upfront), the change in the futures price is settled in cash at the end of each trading day, so the value of the asset is zero and we _cannot really compute a return_.

Consider $R_{p,t+1}$, the portfolio return _excluding futures_, $F_t$, the futures price, and $N_f$, the <b>futures exposure</b> _(number of contracts times multiplier)_.<br/>In this case, the change in the total value of the portfolio is:

$$
V_{t+1} - V_t = V_t R_{p,t+1} + N_f(F_{t+1} - F_t)
$$

Therefore, the return with futures $R_{p,t+1}^{F}$ is:

$$
R_{p,t+1}^{F} = \frac{V_{t+1}}{V_t} - 1 = R_{p,t+1} + \frac{N_f F_t}{V_t}\frac{F_{t+1} - F_t}{F_t} = R_{p,t+1} + w_{F,t}R_{F,t+1}
$$

_(Note that the formula is similar to the previous one, but always remember that weights do NOT need to sum to 1.)_

However, when <b>futures positions are rolled</b> _(i.e., closing the current position before expiry and opening a new one with later expiry),_ we make sure to use a given futures contract and _not mix contracts_ with different maturities.

_Consider, for example, <a href="https://www.cmegroup.com/markets/equities/sp/e-mini-sandp500.html" target="_blank">E-Mini S&P 500 Futures</a> around the September 2026 expiry:_
- On September 17, we would use the September contract to compute returns.
- Starting from September 18, we would use the December contract.

One way to do this easily is to configure your code to determine which contract to use. With the _generic futures_ in Bloomberg, the contract is rolled automatically and, using the function <b>GDUT</b>, prices are adjusted automatically for rollover _(needed because futures prices differ across maturities and the price spread between maturities reflects the difference between the risk-free rate and the dividend yield)._

Formally, for an equity future, the <b>no-arbitrage</b> price is:

$$
F(T) = S_0 e^{(r-q)T}
$$

where $S_0$ is the stock's current price, $r$ is the continuously compounded risk-free rate, and $q$ is the dividend yield. The price difference between two maturities $T_1$ and $T_2$ is then:

$$
F(T_2) - F(T_1) = S_0\left(e^{(r-q)T_2} - e^{(r-q)T_1}\right)
$$

_However,_ note that futures returns will generally differ from the returns on price and total-return indices. In general, in the _absence of market frictions_, futures returns equal index returns _(including dividends)_ minus the risk-free rate:

$$
R_{\mathrm{futures}} \approx R_{\mathrm{total\ return\ index}} - r_f
$$

We now give a quick proof of this:

Suppose initially that the value of the underlying price index follows:

$$
dS_t = (\mu_t - q) S_t\,dt + \sigma_t S_t\,dB_t
$$

where $q$ is the dividend yield, $dB_t$ is <b>Brownian motion</b>, $\mu_t$ is the drift, and $\sigma_t$ is the volatility.

We also denote by $r$ the risk-free rate, so the price of futures maturing at $T$ is:

$$
F_t = S_t e^{(r-q)(T-t)}
$$

Using Itô's formula, we get:

$$
dF_t = dS_t e^{(r-q)(T-t)} - S_t (r-q) e^{(r-q)(T-t)}\,dt = \frac{dS_t}{S_t}F_t - (r-q)F_t\,dt
$$

$$
\frac{dF_t}{F_t} = \frac{dS_t}{S_t} - (r-q)\,dt = (\mu_t - r)\,dt + \sigma_t\,dB_t
$$

<b>Performance measurement</b>

The idea of performance measurement is to relate the portfolio's realized return to risk. Note that performance measures are usually based on <b>excess returns</b>, i.e., the return of the portfolio minus the return of the risk-free asset.

The <b>Sharpe ratio</b> gives the average return of the portfolio $\bar{R}_p$ in excess of the risk-free rate $\bar{R}_f$ per unit of total portfolio risk $\sigma_p$.

$$
\mathrm{SR}_p = \frac{\bar{R}_p - \bar{R}_f}{\sigma_p}
$$

It is an appropriate measure for an investor focused on the <b>full risk</b> of the portfolio. However, note that the Sharpe ratio:
- does not distinguish whether returns are generated by systematic or idiosyncratic risk;
- only considers the first two moments of the return distribution.

Another very common performance measure is <b>alpha</b>, which represents the <b>excess return</b> on the portfolio over the return that would have been achieved by investing in a benchmark portfolio with the <b>same systematic risk</b>.<br/>It can be estimated, together with its significance, by regressing the portfolio excess return $R_{p,t}-R_{f,t}$ on the excess returns of the systematic risk factors $R_{j,t}$, i.e. by finding $\alpha_p$, $\beta_j$, and $\epsilon_{p,t}$ such that:

$$
R_{p,t}-R_{f,t}=\alpha_p+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\beta_jR_{j,t}+\epsilon_{p,t}.
$$

Note that alpha can be computed using one or more benchmark models. For example, we can consider the <b>market model</b>:

$$
R_{p,t}-R_{f,t}=\alpha_p+\beta_p(R_{M,t}-R_{f,t})+\epsilon_{p,t}.
$$

_Similarly,_ we can extend this regression to the <b><a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(93)90023-5" target="_blank">Fama–French three-factor model</a></b> _(market factor, SMB size factor, and HML value factor)_:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_{p,t}-R_{f,t}={}&\alpha_p+\beta_{M,p}(R_{M,t}-R_{f,t})\\
&+\beta_{SMB,p}(R_{S,t}-R_{B,t})
+\beta_{HML,p}(R_{H,t}-R_{L,t})+\epsilon_{p,t}.
\end{aligned}
$$

By adding the momentum factor, we obtain the <b><a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1997.tb03808.x" target="_blank">Fama–French–Carhart four-factor model</a></b>:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_{p,t}-R_{f,t}={}&\alpha_p+\beta_{M,p}(R_{M,t}-R_{f,t})\\
&+\beta_{SMB,p}(R_{S,t}-R_{B,t})
+\beta_{HML,p}(R_{H,t}-R_{L,t})\\
&+\beta_{UMD,p}(R_{U,t}-R_{D,t})+\epsilon_{p,t}.
\end{aligned}
$$

_Lastly,_ the <b>Fung–Hsieh model</b>, _mostly used to evaluate hedge funds and CTAs (commodity trading advisors)_, takes into account that active strategies may produce <b>nonlinear payoff profiles</b> through the use of leverage, short selling, and derivatives. The model uses seven factors, divided into equity factors (market and size-spread factors), bond factors _(bond-market and credit-spread factors)_, and trend-following factors _(bond, currency, and commodity trends)_. An additional emerging-market factor can eventually be added.

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_{p,t}-R_{f,t}={}&\alpha_p+\beta_{M,p}(R_{M,t}-R_{f,t})+\beta_{SMB,p}(R_{S,t}-R_{B,t})\\
&+\beta_{Term,p}(R_{10yTreasury,t}-R_{f,t})+\beta_{Default,p}(R_{BAA,t}-R_{10yTreasury,t})\\
&+\beta_{FX,p}(R_{FXStraddle,t}-R_{f,t})+\beta_{Cdty,p}(R_{CdtyStraddle,t}-R_{f,t})\\
&+\beta_{Bond,p}(R_{BondStraddle,t}-R_{f,t})+\epsilon_{p,t}.
\end{aligned}
$$

More on this regression can be found <a href="https://www.trendfollowing.com/whitepaper/hsieh_fung_final_paper.pdf" target="_blank">here in the famous paper "The Risk in Hedge Fund Strategies: Theory and Evidence from Trend Followers" by William Fung and David Hsieh</a>.

Furthermore, we can define the <b>information ratio (appraisal ratio)</b>, which gives the average return achieved by a portfolio in excess of a benchmark divided by the tracking error _(i.e. the standard deviation of the return differential)_. In a formula, we write:

$$
\mathrm{IR}=\frac{\bar{R}_p-\bar{R}_b}{\sigma_{p-b}}.
$$

_Note that in this case_ the benchmark can be specified exogenously or estimated using a regression. In that case, the information ratio can be computed from the regression results using $\alpha$ and $\epsilon$ (the regression residual), where the residual standard deviation represents the tracking error:

$$
\mathrm{IR}=\frac{\bar{R}_p-\bar{R}_b}{\sigma_{p-b}}=\frac{\alpha}{\sigma_\epsilon}.
$$

<b>Measuring timing ability</b>

We usually say that _"time in the market beats timing the market..."_; however, we now discuss and measure the timing ability of a portfolio. We can do this in two ways:
- by plotting $\beta$ against the market return;
- by comparing portfolio returns and market returns.

_Practically speaking_, this analysis is particularly relevant when considering a portfolio that rotates between stocks and bonds _(the same analysis can be extended to other asset classes by using the market return as the return on the asset class and $\beta$ as the exposure to the asset class)_.

We can first state that if a strategy is <b>successful at timing the market</b>, there should be a positive correlation between the portfolio's $\beta$ and the market return _(or between $\beta$ and the difference between the market return and the bond return, i.e. the excess return to bonds)_.

_Secondly_, if a strategy is <b>successful at timing the market</b>, the portfolio's exposure to fluctuations in the market is greater when the market goes up and smaller when it goes down, yielding a <b>convex</b> relationship between market returns and portfolio returns _(intuition: a portfolio should seek to invest when assets go up and stay liquid when they go down)_.


<div align = "center">
![Bias variance tradeoff](/images/notes/third-semester/timing-2.png)
</div>

Therefore, we can detect timing ability by testing for <b>nonlinearity</b> in the relationship between market returns and portfolio returns. One common approach is the <b><a href="https://hbr.org/1966/07/can-mutual-funds-outguess-the-market" target="_blank">Treynor–Mazuy procedure</a></b>, which fits a quadratic curve to the returns using the following _(linear, despite the quadratic curve!)_ regression:

$$
R_{p,t}-R_{f,t}=\alpha_p+\beta_p(R_{M,t}-R_{f,t})+\gamma_p(R_{M,t}-R_{f,t})^2+\epsilon_{p,t}.
$$

In the case of good timing ability, we expect a positive and statistically significant $\gamma_p$ coefficient.

Furthermore, the <b><a href="https://doi.org/10.1086/296131" target="_blank">Merton–Henriksson procedure</a></b> tests whether the portfolio return resembles the <b>payoff diagram of a long market position plus a long call</b> on the market by running the regression:

$$
R_{p,t}-R_{f,t}=\alpha_p+\beta_p(R_{M,t}-R_{f,t})+\gamma_p\max(R_{M,t}-R_{f,t},0)+\epsilon_{p,t}.
$$

_Again,_ in the case of positive timing evidence, we expect a positive $\gamma_p$. Unfortunately, the explanatory power of these tests is still low.

One last point: note that $\alpha_p$ and $\beta_p$ will be <b>distorted</b> if the strategy involves timing but timing is not modeled explicitly when trying to measure performance, i.e. if the analysis assumes constant exposure.

_For example_, suppose a strategy:
- holds mostly equities during bull markets;
- moves to cash in the opposite case.

The returns may look strong, and the regression may attribute these returns to $\alpha$ _(skill)_ and $\beta$ _(sensitivity to the market)_. However, part of the performance may simply derive from <b>when</b> the strategy is invested, rather than from stock selection itself, creating a <b>timing bias</b> _(in the sense of model misspecification)_. A more accurate analysis would model time-varying exposure _(i.e. $\beta_t$)_ using:

$$
R_t=\alpha_t+\beta_tR_{m,t}+\epsilon_t.
$$

## <a id="3">Principles of Strategy Development: Statistical Significance, Overfitting, Biases and Transaction Costs</a>

When developing a strategy, it is essential to keep the following in mind:
- always worry about <b>statistical significance</b>
- be aware of <b>overfitting and data mining</b> _("if you stress numbers enough, they can tell you anything")_
- be aware of <b>biases!</b> _(backfilling bias, selection bias, look-ahead bias, etc., etc.)_
- account for <b>transaction costs!</b>

<b>Statistical significance</b>

_Generally speaking, unless a relation/effect is statistically significant, it should not be used as a starting point for an investment strategy._

_Firstly,_ consider that many asset returns are <b>not normally distributed</b> _(e.g. stock returns in the short period have fat tails, options have large skewness...),_ and a consequence of this is <b>non-normality of the residuals</b> in return regressions.

_Secondly,_ consider that <b>volatility is time-varying</b>, so asset returns are not independent and identically distributed.

Therefore, when running a regression, we have that:
- by the <b>Gauss-Markov theorem</b>, the OLS estimator is BLUE _(<a href="https://giacomomaggiore.com/notes/15-econometrics" target="_blank">Best Linear Unbiased Estimator; I've written more about it here in my econometrics notes!</a>) if the errors are independent and identically distributed (IID), so note that <b>normality is not required!</b>
- When the residuals are not IID _(i.e. if they are <b>correlated</b> or there is heteroskedasticity, so error variance changes with $x$):_
    - the OLS is still unbiased but it is <b>inefficient</b>, and GLS _(Generalized Least Squares)_ is BLUE.
    - the OLS standard errors are incorrect, and we should instead use <b>robust standard errors</b>.

<b>Overfitting and Biases</b>

We start by supposing we are looking for a strategy with a high Sharpe ratio. We can then say that the larger the number of trials made when developing the strategy, the longer the length of the backtest required for the <b>expected out-of-sample</b> Sharpe ratio to be positive.

<div align = "center">
![Bias variance tradeoff](/images/notes/third-semester/sharpe-ratio-trials.png)
</div>

Therefore, we list some approaches that can be used to avoid overfitting:
- keep track of the number of trials and keep this number _low!_
- keep the strategy _relatively simple_
- run out-of-sample tests by splitting data into training/testing datasets
- investigate the robustness of the strategy to changes in parameters

Furthermore, beyond overfitting, particular focus should be placed on biases since they can arise in several places and times, in the way assets/data are collected, considered, and analyzed.

In particular, we define <b>look-ahead bias</b> as the tendency to use information that was not _actually_ available at the time the investment decision had to be made. Note that this bias is particularly evident in backtesting and signal detection.

_For example,_ suppose that we are trying to predict returns using a regression $R_{t+1} = \alpha + \beta x_t + \epsilon_{t+1}$.

To avoid look-ahead biases, we have to make sure that $x_t$ was _really known_ at time $t$ _(i.e. that $x_t$ is $\mathcal{F}_t$-adapted... but this is another story: if you want to know a bit more about stochastic equations and filtrations, well, prepare to suck... <a href="https://giacomomaggiore.com/notes/20-numerical-solution-sde#1" target="_blank">maybe my notes here can be a starting point!</a>)_. <br/>Note that this problem is particularly relevant in finance since accounting/earnings data are often published with a delay, some financial market data _(interest, mutual fund NAV)_ are published with a lag of one trading day, there are always different time zones in place and, _finally,_ data are often revised after publication!

_On the other hand_, <b>selection bias</b> arises when the sample is not randomly drawn from the underlying population, causing assets with particular features to be <b>over-/under-represented in the sample</b>. The most reliable fix in this case is always the simplest, yet most difficult to implement in practice: _include the full population!_

<b>Survivorship bias</b>, _in particular,_ is a form of selection bias that arises when the sample used in the analysis only includes assets that are still traded at the end of the sample period. For example, evaluating mutual fund performance using only funds that still exist excludes all the funds that either closed or merged, so the surviving sample appears to have stronger historical performance than the full initial sample. _On the other hand_, almost off-topic in finance, studying only successful startups and concluding that founders who drop out are still very successful... well, this is obvious... you don't see dropout students that are both without money and a degree.

_In general,_ survivorship bias can be avoided by using the population of all assets in a given category, regardless of their current status, _(but this could be very hard to do, especially when looking for public data)_ or by using the constituents of an index from a period previous to each investment decision as the starting point/sample.

<b>Backfilling / instant history bias</b> arises instead when historical data are backfilled into a database when an asset is added to it. _For example,_ this happens when a hedge fund database adds a fund and includes returns before the inclusion date _(it can be fixed by dropping data for assets before their inclusion date)_.

<b>Transaction costs</b>

Assessing the impact of transaction costs requires a priori estimates of costs and, more difficult, estimates of the strategy's turnover. The case of <b>proportional transaction costs</b> is easiest to deal with. <br/>If we denote by $c$ the trading cost per currency unit _(including commissions, bid-ask spreads, and any other transaction taxes or costs)_, a quick estimate of the effect of transaction costs on the return per period is _just_ the product of $c$ and the strategy's average turnover _(assuming all assets have the same cost, but this can be viewed differently simply by taking individual costs and turnovers)_.

If we want more granularity, we can subtract the product of costs and turnovers at the end of each period. To do that, consider $w$ as the vector of portfolio weights and $|\ldots|$ as its component-wise absolute value. In this case, the total turnover in period $t$ can be approximated as:

$$
z_t = |w_t - w_{t-1}|^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{1}
$$

but note that this approximation does not account for the change in portfolio weights induced by differences in returns across assets. To include this, we can compute the portfolio's weights $\hat{w}_t$ at the end of the period and calculate the turnover:

$$
\hat{w}_{n,t} = w_{n,t-1} \frac{1 + R_{n,t}}{1 + R_{p,t}}, \qquad z_t = |w_t - \hat{w}_t|^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{1}
$$

## <a id="4">Constant-Allocation, Timing and Selection Strategies: Risk Premium Estimation and Predictive Models</a>

<b>Risk Premium Estimation</b>

We start by saying that the simplest strategies are the ones targeting a <b>constant allocation</b> to a number of asset classes, so the key decisions are:
- which asset classes should we consider?
- how often should the portfolio be rebalanced toward target weights?

Indeed, _note that_ for those strategies, the return depends on the risk premia of the asset classes over time, and the size of the allocation depends on the <b>(unconditional)</b> risk premium per unit of risk, the level of risk, and the timing of the losses of each asset class.

<b>Unconditional risk premia</b> on different asset classes can be estimated as the difference between the average return on the risky asset and the average return on the riskless asset. For example, we can say that:
- <b>equity risk premium</b>: average total return on stocks
- <b>bond risk premium</b>: average total return on long-term debt
- <b>credit risk premium</b>: average total return on risky debt
- <b>real estate risk premium</b>: average total return on real estate
- <b>commodity risk premium</b>: average total return on commodities ... _and so on..._

_Deep dive:_ if you want to know more about risk premia, well, it is better to spend some time on <a href="https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/" target="_blank">Aswath Damodaran's</a> personal/official page, where he writes about asset pricing and personal finance and, _on a periodic basis,_ publishes estimates of equity risk premia around the world. Enjoy!

However, notice that average return estimates are _noisy_, so we must rely on <b>long sample periods</b>. Furthermore, _note that_ <b>risk premia are not constant over time</b> and therefore estimating <b>conditional risk premia</b> is much harder than obtaining unconditional estimates. (Recall that the unconditional risk premium is the long-run average return without conditioning on/knowing the current market information, while the conditional risk premium is the expected excess return given the information available today, such as valuation, interest rates, volatility, and market conditions.)

In this context, the <b><a href="https://doi.org/10.1086/260061" target="_blank">Fama–MacBeth method</a></b> is a common approach to estimate risk premia. Suppose we have $N$ assets with excess return on asset $n$ given by:

$$
R_{n,t} - R_{f,t} = a_n + \beta_n^{\mathsf{T}} f_t + \epsilon_{n,t}
$$

where $f_t$ denotes the factors driving the returns and $a_n$ is an <b>asset-specific constant</b> _(note that we write $a_n$ and not $\alpha_n$ so as not to confuse it with alpha!)_.

Given this, the method relies on the following steps:
- run $N$ time-series regressions, one for each asset $n$, and obtain all the $\beta_n$ values _(i.e. we estimate its factor exposures $\beta_n$: how much an asset $n$ moves with each risk factor)_;
- for each time period, run a <b>cross-sectional regression</b> of excess returns on the $\beta$ values to estimate the returns on the factors during a period, i.e. _(note that we regress on $\beta$ values; $\gamma$ and $\lambda$ are our coefficient estimates):_

$$
R_{n,t} - R_{f,t} = \gamma_t + \beta_n^{\mathsf{T}} \lambda_t + \alpha_{n,t}
$$

- we then obtain estimates of $\alpha_{n,t}$ _(the asset-specific residual)_ and the $\lambda_t$ values _(the factor risk premia)_. We can then consider their time-series average from all the cross-sectional regressions:

$$
\hat{\lambda} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \hat{\lambda}_t, \qquad \hat{\alpha}_n = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \hat{\alpha}_{n,t}
$$

Furthermore, to test the <b>significance of risk premia and asset-specific residuals</b>, we can compute the covariance matrices as:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{\lambda}) = \frac{1}{T^2}\sum_{t=1}^{T} (\hat{\lambda}_t - \hat{\lambda})(\hat{\lambda}_t - \hat{\lambda})^{\mathsf{T}}
$$

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{\alpha}) = \frac{1}{T^2}\sum_{t=1}^{T} (\hat{\alpha}_t - \hat{\alpha})(\hat{\alpha}_t - \hat{\alpha})^{\mathsf{T}}
$$

Note that the <b>resulting standard errors</b> are not perfect, since they do _not_ account for the fact that the $\beta$s are estimates, not real data. We could account for this by running a single cross-sectional regression for the sample average excess return and using formulas for the standard errors, in this case by running:

$$
\bar{R}_n - \bar{R}_f = \gamma + \beta_n^{\mathsf{T}} \lambda + \alpha_n
$$

<b>Timing Models</b>

The goal of timing models is to find indicators for which the <b>conditional return</b> distribution differs from the <b>unconditional return</b> distribution, relying on the predictability of assets.

As a consequence, this goal translates into identifying a set of variables that forecast the future return distribution in order to identify periods in which risky-asset returns are more attractive.

Consider now $x_t$, the predictive variable being considered, and $R_{t+h}$, the future excess return with a forecasting period of $h$. To test if $x_t$ has predictive power, we can <b>split the sample</b> based on the value of $x_t$ and compute summary statistics for $R_{t+h}$ for the subsamples, then run a test for identical distributions by estimating the <b>predictive regression</b>:

$$
R_{t+h} = \alpha_h + \beta_h x_t + \epsilon_{t+h}
$$

$$
R_{t+h}^2 = \alpha_h + \beta_h x_t + \epsilon_{t+h}
$$

where the first tells us about returns, the second about variance. <br/>_Furthermore_, we <b>regress using dummy variables</b> $D_{\{x_t > \tilde{x}\}}$ instead of $x_t$:

$$
R_{t+h} = \alpha_h + \beta_h D_{\{x_t > \tilde{x}\}} + \epsilon_{t+h}
$$

$$
R_{t+h}^2 = \alpha_h + \beta_h D_{\{x_t > \tilde{x}\}} + \epsilon_{t+h}
$$

_Note that_ if the critical values and the regression coefficients are estimated with rolling or expanding windows, then you are working out-of-sample. Of course, the forecast should be based on information available within the window used for the estimation to avoid look-ahead bias.

Common predictors are:
- <b>valuation ratios</b>
    - dividend/price ratio
    - earnings/price ratio
    - book-to-market ratio
    - consumption/wealth ratio
- <b>interest-rate variables</b>: _(reflecting monetary policy and market expectations about the business cycle)_
    - T-bill rate and the T-bill rate minus its 12-month average
    - term spread (TS) _(long-term yield minus short-term interest rate; it reflects business-cycle movements and anticipates changes in monetary policy)_
    - credit spread (CS) _(BBB bond yields minus AAA yields; represents the market assessment of future default risk)_
- <b>volatility variables</b>
    - realized volatility
    - implied volatility _(example: <a href="https://www.cboe.com/tradable_products/vix/" target="_blank">VIX</a>, which represents perceived risk)_
- <b>macro variables</b>
    - GDP
    - inflation
- <b>news sentiment</b>

<br/>

<b>Selection models</b> try to generate returns by selecting assets from a <b>cross-section based on some criteria</b>, _usually from the same asset class. Some examples are below:_
- <b>carry</b>: select currencies based on their interest rate
- <b>value</b>: select stocks based on their _book-to-market ratio_
- <b>momentum</b>: select stocks based on their past return

_Usually,_ we proceed as follows:
- <b>sort the assets into groups</b> based on the value of the variable of interest. The number of groups is chosen to find a balance between stronger effects at the extremes and reduced idiosyncratic risk (which requires many assets per group).
- <b>build portfolios of the assets in the groups</b>
- <b>investigate return properties of the portfolios</b>

_However,_ note that in many cases we want to incorporate <b>several variables</b>; in this case, <b>bivariate (or three-way) sorts</b> can be valid approaches for sorting over selected variables in either of two ways:
- <b>with an independent sort</b>, where we sort on different variables _independently,_ so the resulting portfolio will be the intersection of portfolios built on individual variables, and the number of assets in each portfolio will _strongly differ_ if the two variables are related;
- <b>dependent sort</b>, where we first sort on a first variable and then, within each quantile of the first variable, sort based on a second variable. In this case, the number of assets in each group is similar, but the results depend on the order chosen.

Furthermore, we can use the <b>scoring method</b> by attributing scores to each asset for each variable considered and then grouping them by the overall score.
- For each considered variable, we attribute a score to each asset based on its quantile, _making sure that higher means better for all_.
- compute an overall score by summing, averaging, or weighting _(be aware of overfitting!)_

The last approach involves <b>regression</b> to estimate the expected returns.
- we use the <b>Fama–MacBeth method</b> or <b>cross-sectional regression</b> to estimate the exposure of the assets to the factors and their risk premia to estimate $\hat{\gamma}$ and $\hat{\lambda}$, as done before
- we compute the expected excess return on each asset based on the factor exposure

$$
\mathbb{E}[R_{n,t} - R_{f,t}] = \hat{\gamma} + \beta_n^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\lambda}
$$

Once we have selected a selection method, the resulting portfolio can be implemented in three ways:
- <b>long-only portfolios</b>
- <b>long-short portfolio</b>, so the return for the period is equal to:
    + the return of the long portfolio
    + the risk-free rate
    - the cost of the portfolio held short
    - the lending fees of the portfolio
- <b>long portfolio with a hedge for market risk</b>, where the hedge is implemented using futures, so the return of each period is given by:
    + the return of the long portfolio
    - the hedge ratio times the percentage price change of futures

## <a id="5">Combining Strategies and Implementation: Portfolio Weights, Leverage, Short Selling and Levered ETFs</a>

<b>Combining strategies</b>

Individual strategies do not work all the time, so it can be useful to manage assets using multiple strategies. In this case, _instead of running portfolio optimization to allocate funds, it is often more useful to have heuristics since they are usually more robust._

When combining strategies, alpha and the factor exposure for the overall portfolio are the weighted averages of the underlying strategies. However, for the Sharpe ratio, we cannot say the same.

Suppose _for example_ we want to run a <b>mean-variance portfolio optimization</b>; let $\mu$ be the vector of expected returns and $\Sigma$ the covariance matrix. Then the <b>tangency portfolio</b> is:

$$
q = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - R_f \mathbf{1})}{\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - R_f \mathbf{1})}
  = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - R_f \mathbf{1})}{B - A R_f}
$$

with $A = \mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}$ and $B = \mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma^{-1}\mu$.

In this case, the Sharpe ratio will be:

$$
\mathrm{SR} = \frac{\mu_p - R_f}{\sigma_p}
  = \sqrt{(\mu - R_f \mathbf{1})^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - R_f \mathbf{1})}
$$

_However,_ suppose now that we do NOT trust the estimates of expected returns. Then the candidate portfolio would be the <b>risky-asset-only global minimum variance</b>, defined by:

$$
w = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}
  = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}{A}
$$

$$
\mu_p = w^{\mathsf{T}}\mu = \frac{B}{A}
$$

$$
\sigma_p^2 = w^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma w = \frac{1}{A}
$$

$$
\mathrm{SR}_p = \frac{B - A R_f}{\sqrt{A}}
$$

We also introduce the <b>risk parity</b> portfolio, with weights according to the inverse of their volatility, so we have:

$$
w_i = \frac{1/\sigma_i}{\sum_{n=1}^{N} 1/\sigma_n}
$$

If we let $\rho_{mn}$ be the <b>pairwise correlation</b> between $m$ and $n$, expected returns and variance are:

$$
\mu_p = \frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N} \mu_n/\sigma_n}{\sum_{n=1}^{N} 1/\sigma_n}
$$

$$
\sigma_p^2
  = \frac{N + \sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m\ne n}\rho_{mn}}
  {\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N} 1/\sigma_n\right)^2}
  = \frac{N + N(N-1)\bar{\rho}}
  {\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N} 1/\sigma_n\right)^2}
$$

which results in the Sharpe ratio:

$$
\mathrm{SR}_p
  = \sqrt{\frac{N}{1 + (N-1)\bar{\rho}}}
  \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\mu_n - R_f}{\sigma_n}
$$

_Lastly,_ for the <b>equally weighted portfolio</b>, $w = 1/N$, and we have the following:

$$
\mu_p = \bar{\mu}
$$

$$
\sigma_p^2 = \frac{\bar{\sigma}^2}{N}
  + \frac{N-1}{N}\bar{\rho}\,\bar{\sigma}^2
$$

where $\bar{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sigma_n^2$.

$$
\mathrm{SR}_p
  \approx \sqrt{\frac{N}{1 + (N-1)\bar{\rho}}}
  \frac{\bar{\mu} - R_f}{\bar{\sigma}}
$$

From these four expressions, we can compute the portfolios and the Sharpe ratio from different approaches:
- if the Sharpe ratios do _NOT_ differ, it is advisable to go for an <b>equally weighted</b> portfolio;
- if they do, <b>tilting the portfolio</b> toward weights recommended by other approaches can be justified;
- _empirically,_ <b>risk-parity</b> portfolios tend to be more robust
- if the tangency portfolio recommends shorting a strategy, we should <b>drop it</b> _(this typically happens if it is highly correlated with another one but has a lower expected return per unit of risk)._

_(Note that we could also look at skewness and worst return, as the Sharpe ratio does not cover everything.)_

<b>Implementation Issues</b>

Some common aspects to consider for implementation:
- are the instruments <b>accessible</b>?
- is there any minimum number of transactions required, what are the trading hours, in which currencies do assets trade, and are they OTC?
- are the positions subject to counterparty risk?
- commissions, spreads, taxes
- are instruments always available or substitutable?
- when should we buy? at open, close, or during the market session?

_In general,_ with strategies with lower turnover, the exact time of the transaction should not be critical. _On the other hand,_ for high-turnover strategies, it is important to know when you trade, _especially_ because many exchanges have special rules for trading at the open/close time, and prices at these times are usually more volatile.

<b>Leverage Constraints and Problems with Short Positions</b>

If the strategy uses <b>leverage</b>, it is important to make sure that the strategy can obtain it and <b>account for its cost</b> _(there's no free lunch!)_

The amount of leverage is usually driven by regulation, standards, and the broker, but usually we have:
- for stock/bond positions, you usually get up to 2:1 total
- for futures positions, it depends on the margin requirements and is usually larger
- for long option positions, it is generally hard to get leverage on top
- for short option positions, margin requirements may exceed the contract's value _(especially for OTM options)_
- for OTC derivatives, each bank/broker has its own rules

Particular issues arise if the strategy uses <b>short positions</b> in securities, since in order to short a security and hold it overnight, we need to borrow it:
- usually, banks/brokers have inventory that they lend for a fee, which is larger if many people want to short at the same time;
- whenever you borrow a security, the lender can request it to be returned. In this case, the bank/broker has to borrow it from someone else, but if they cannot, they can also recall your security, and this will force you to close the position;
- additional risk arises when regulators impose <b>short-selling bans</b> when a security drops sharply in value over a short period. _In this case,_ existing short positions are usually _grandfathered_, but you cannot open new ones.

Because of these problems, when we analyze a long-short strategy, we may:
- investigate the profitability of the short and long legs separately;
- consider alternatives to shorting.

_Conceptually,_ the short leg of a strategy can improve performance in two ways:
- selection of securities that we expect to drop in value;
- reduction of market risk.

_In particular,_ a well-functioning approach works as follows:
- we compute the performance statistics of:
    - the full long-short strategy;
    - a strategy involving only the long leg;
    - a strategy involving only the short leg;
    - a strategy involving the long leg and shorting the index futures.
- if the long-only strategy has the best performance, $\implies$ we ignore shorting;
- if the strategy with index futures is close in performance to the full long-only strategy, $\implies$ we use the strategy with futures;
- if the strategy using index futures is _significantly_ worse than the full long-only strategy, the full long-only strategy is then preferred, as long as it has a realistic implementation:
    - in this case, you may try to get a sense of whether you could encounter problems borrowing;
    - if this could be a problem, we can synthesize shorting in different ways:
        - by using options (long put, short call), but be aware of liquidity and transaction costs;
        - by selling futures and buying all the index members that we do not want to short;
        - by shorting ETFs or buying short ETFs in which the stocks we want to short have a large weight.

We now go back to the graph showing the returns on three ETFs around the 2007-2008 financial crisis:

- UYG (Ultra Financials, 2X long)
- SKF (Ultrashort Financials, 2X short)

We _immediately notice_ that over the 2-year period considered, IYF resulted in a 50% loss, and SKF didn't reach 100% again, so it was still showing a loss. _As we can see then,_ both the leveraged long and the levered short ETFs were worth <b>less than the underlying</b> at the end of the period. The root cause is that the ETFs are <b>rebalanced dynamically on a daily basis</b>, so, _at the end of the day, when the underlying index price went up,_
- the levered ETF has to increase its position to keep the leverage ratio constant;
- the short ETF reduces its short position.

_So,_ levered and inverse ETFs tend to do well in <b>trending markets</b> and do <b>poorly</b> in markets with reversals.


Assume, for example, a 2x-levered ETF _(rebalanced daily)_ investing in an index, and assume that the ETFs and the underlying index are both worth 100 at $t = 0$. Then assume that at $t = 1$ the index goes up by 10%, and then, at $t = 2$, it either rises another 10% or reverts to 100.

| Period | $t = 0$ | $t = 1$ | \multicolumn\{2\}\{c\}\{$t = 2$\} |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
|  |  |  | Continuation | Reversal |
| **Index** | 100 | 110 | 121 | 100 |
| **ETF Index Position** | 200 | 220 | 264 | 218.2 |
| **ETF Value** | 100 | 120 | 144 | 98.2 |
| **Required ETF Index Position** | 200 | 240 | 288 | 196.4 |
| **Required ETF Trade** | — | Buy 20 | Buy 24 | Sell 21.8 |

In general, leveraged ETFs perform poorly when volatility is high because of <b>volatility drag.</b> We can approximate the annualized continuously compounded return of a leveraged ETF with leverage 

$$
g_L \approx L\mu - \frac{1}{2}L^2\sigma^2,
$$

where $\mu$ is the underlying asset's average return and $\sigma^2$ is the variance of its returns. _Therefore,_ the approximate annualized simple return is:

$$
R_L \approx \exp\left(L\mu - \frac{1}{2}L^2\sigma^2\right) - 1.
$$

For small returns:

$$
\log(1 + L R_t) \approx L R_t - \frac{1}{2}L^2R_t^2.
$$

Taking averages gives:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\log(1 + L R_t)\right]
\approx L\mu - \frac{1}{2}L^2\sigma^2.
$$

The second term is the volatility drag. It is negative and increases with the square of the leverage.

_Hence,_ a levered or short ETF requires adjusting the size of the ETF positions used to undo the effect of the dynamic rebalancing inside the ETF _(but of course you don't need to rebalance if you backtest the strategy already while using a levered/short ETF.)_

## <a id="6">Equity Risk Premium and Return Predictability: Valuation Ratios, Dividend Yield and the Term Premium</a>

The <b>equity risk premium (ERP)</b> is the excess return of the equity market over that of a low-risk asset.
It is usually measured against Treasury bill or Treasury bond returns.

We can then distinguish between:
- <b>historical ERP</b>: the extra return earned in the past
- <b>expected ERP</b>: the average extra return from investing in
equities rather than in T-Bills over the next h months, quarters, or years:

$$
E_t[\mathrm{ERP}_{t\to t+h}]
= E_t[R_{M,t,t+h}] - R_{f,t}.
$$

_Note_ that if you borrow capital and invest it in the equity market, you earn ERP.

_Note also_ that we can write the optimal portfolio formula for a mean-variance investor with risk aversion $a$ as:

$$
w = \frac{1}{a}\frac{\mathbb{E}_t[R_{M,t+1}] - R_{f,t+1}}{\operatorname{Var}_t[R_{M,t+1}]} = \frac{1}{a}\frac{\mathbb{E}_t[\mathrm{ERP}_{t+1}]}{\operatorname{Var}_t[R_{M,t+1}]}
$$

The economic idea is that the _ex ante_ ERP is the compensation required by investors to allocate capital to risky assets rather than to the risk-free asset, and the equilibrium reasoning predicts a <b>positive</b> ex-ante ERP because stocks do poorly in bad times.

To estimate the <b>historical ERP</b>, we can compare historical average returns of the stock market with those of the risk-free asset by adopting a <b>long sample period</b>, since stock returns are volatile _(note that we can distinguish between nominal and real returns)_.

_On the other hand,_ estimating the <b>expected ERP</b> is much more difficult. We cannot simply use the historical one because there is a <b>negative relation</b> between changes in future and historical ERPs _(if investors require the future ERP to decrease, realized equity returns are positive, and so the historical ERP increases)_.

To understand this relation, we can decompose stock returns with price $P_t$ and dividend $D_t$ as follows:

$$
1 + R_t = \frac{P_t + D_t}{P_{t-1}} = \left(1 + \frac{D_t}{P_t}\right) \times \frac{D_t}{D_{t-1}} \times \frac{P_t / D_t}{P_{t-1} / D_{t-1}}
$$

where the first term includes the <b>dividend yield</b>, the second the <b>dividend growth</b>, and the last one the <b>multiple expansion</b>, which drives the changes in the expected ERP over the short term while, _in the long run,_ it accounts for a smaller part of the ERP.

<div align = "center">
![Bias variance tradeoff](/images/notes/third-semester/erp-5.png)
</div>

We can then use this decomposition to get an estimate of the ERP by assessing each component of $R_t$ and subtracting the real risk-free rate _(for the risk-free rate, we can consider the return of short-term T-bills in the US)_.

<b>Equity Return Predictability</b>

We first recall that timing models are based on predictability, which studies patterns in the return distribution over time to identify sets of variables that forecast the future return distribution. We previously saw that predictability is about finding indicators for which an asset's conditional return distribution differs from its unconditional distribution.

We also saw that, in order to assess whether a variable $x_t$ has predictive ability, we have to:
- <b>split the sample</b> based on the value of $x_t$, compute statistics of $R_{t+h}$ for the subsamples, and run a test for identical distributions
- estimate the <b>predictive regressions</b>:

$$
R_{t+h} = \alpha_h + \beta_h x_t + \epsilon_{t+h}
$$

$$
R_{t+h}^2 = \alpha_h + \beta_h x_t + \epsilon_{t+h}
$$

We then assess the significance of the betas _(the first tells us about expected returns; the second, about variance)_.
- run a <b>predictive regression using dummy variables</b> $D_{\{x_t > \tilde{x}\}}$ instead of $x_t$:

$$
R_{t+h} = \alpha_h + \beta_h D_{\{x_t > \tilde{x}\}} + \epsilon_{t+h}
$$

$$
R_{t+h}^2 = \alpha_h + \beta_h D_{\{x_t > \tilde{x}\}} + \epsilon_{t+h}
$$

<b>Price/Dividend Ratio Predictor</b>

We've seen that dividend yield is a key component of long-term equity returns; its inverse is the price/dividend ratio. However, we can notice that the P/D ratio varies strongly over time.

To understand how we can estimate the ERP from the price/dividend ratio, consider a firm that pays a dividend $D_t$ and is expected to pay a terminal dividend $D_{t+1}$.

Consider $P_t$, the current price. Then the gross return on an investment in the firm is:

$$
R_{t+1} = \frac{D_{t+1}}{P_t} \implies \mathbb{E}_t[R_{t+1}] = \frac{\mathbb{E}_t[D_{t+1}]}{P_t}
$$

If we solve for $P_t/D_t$, we get:

$$
\frac{P_t}{D_t} = \frac{\mathbb{E}_t[D_{t+1}/D_t]}{\mathbb{E}_t[R_{t+1}]}
$$

Therefore, the P/D ratio moves due to changes in <b>expected dividend growth</b> or changes in <b>expected returns</b>.

There are two views on the information contained in the P/D ratio:
- <b>traditional view</b>: expected returns are _more or less_ constant, and the P/D ratio is driven by changes in expected dividend growth. Therefore, a high P/D ratio should predict high future dividend growth. For a forecasting horizon $k$, this can be tested with:

$$
\frac{D_{t+k}}{D_t} = a_k + b_k \frac{D_t}{P_t} + \epsilon_{t+k}
$$

This view was challenged by Shiller (1981) in the paper <a href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/1802789" target="_blank">Do Stock Prices Move Too Much to be Justified by Subsequent Changes in Dividends?</a> _(American Economic Review)_, which shows that prices move far too much to be explained by subsequent changes in dividends alone.

- <b>modern view</b>: the P/D ratio is driven by changes in expected returns. Therefore, a high P/D ratio should predict <b>low future returns</b>. This can be tested with:

$$
R_{M,t\to t+k} - R_{f,t\to t+k} = a_k + b_k \frac{D_t}{P_t} + \epsilon_{t+k}
$$

Fama and French (1988) ran this regression in the paper <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(88)90020-7" target="_blank">Dividend Yields and Expected Stock Returns</a> _(Journal of Financial Economics)_, finding that the dividend yield predicts returns, especially at long horizons. Cochrane (2008), in <a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhm046" target="_blank">The Dog That Did Not Bark: A Defense of Return Predictability</a> _(Review of Financial Studies)_, adds that dividend growth is <b>not</b> predictable, so the P/D ratio must move because of expected returns. Goyal and Welch (2008) are more skeptical in <a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhm014" target="_blank">A Comprehensive Look at the Empirical Performance of Equity Premium Prediction</a> _(Review of Financial Studies)_, showing that this predictability is weak out of sample. For an overview of the discount-rate view see Cochrane (2011), <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2011.01671.x" target="_blank">Presidential Address: Discount Rates</a> _(Journal of Finance)_.

We can analyze this view further by rewriting the return definition and taking logs, as Campbell and Shiller (1988) do in the paper <a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/1.3.195" target="_blank">The Dividend-Price Ratio and Expectations of Future Dividends and Discount Factors</a> _(Review of Financial Studies)_.

$$
\frac{P_t}{D_t} = R_{t+1}^{-1}\frac{D_{t+1}}{D_t}
$$

$$
p_t - d_t = \Delta d_{t+1} - r_{t+1}
$$

If we take expectations, we obtain:

$$
p_t - d_t = \mathbb{E}_t[\Delta d_{t+1}] - \mathbb{E}_t[r_{t+1}]
$$

- If the <b>expected returns are constant</b> at $\bar r$, then this becomes:

$$
\mathbb{E}_t[\Delta d_{t+1}] = \bar r + p_t - d_t
$$

If expectations are rational _(unbiased)_, then we have $\Delta d_{t+1} = \mathbb{E}_t[\Delta d_{t+1}] + \epsilon_{t+1}$, with $\mathbb{E}_t[\epsilon_{t+1}] = 0$, so we obtain:

$$
\Delta d_{t+1} = \bar r + (p_t - d_t) + \epsilon_{t+1}
$$

- If the <b>expected dividend growth is constant</b> at $\mathbb{E}_t[\Delta d_{t+1}] = \bar d$, then we have:

$$
\mathbb{E}_t[r_{t+1}] = \bar d - (p_t - d_t)
$$

Under rational expectations, $r_{t+1} = \mathbb{E}_t[r_{t+1}] + \epsilon_{t+1}$, with $\mathbb{E}_t[\epsilon_{t+1}] = 0$, so we have:

$$
r_{t+1} = \bar d - (p_t - d_t) + \epsilon_{t+1}
$$

For example, between 1927 and 2005, the D/P indicator predicted future returns rather than dividend growth.

![Bias variance tradeoff](/images/notes/third-semester/p-d-ratio.png)
</div>

However, the out-of-sample predictive power is still poor, making it difficult to use the ratio to improve portfolio performance.

<div align = "center">
![Bias variance tradeoff](/images/notes/third-semester/p-d-ratio-2.png)
</div>

_Note that_ the one-period model can be extended to multiple periods, as Campbell and Shiller did in 1988. In this case, we start from the one-period return.

$$
R_{t+1} = \frac{P_{t+1}+D_{t+1}}{P_t}
$$

$$
\frac{P_t}{D_t} = \frac{1}{R_{t+1}}\frac{P_{t+1}+D_{t+1}}{D_t}
= R_{t+1}^{-1}\frac{D_{t+1}}{D_t}\left(1 + \frac{P_{t+1}}{D_{t+1}}\right)
$$

If we substitute forward one period, $$\frac{P_{t+1}}{D_{t+1}} = R_{t+2}^{-1}\,\frac{D_{t+2}}{D_{t+1}}\left(1 + \frac{P_{t+2}}{D_{t+2}}\right)$$, we have:

$$
\frac{P_t}{D_t}
= R_{t+1}^{-1}\frac{D_{t+1}}{D_t}
+ R_{t+1}^{-1}\frac{D_{t+1}}{D_t}R_{t+2}^{-1}\frac{D_{t+2}}{D_{t+1}}
\left(1 + \frac{P_{t+2}}{D_{t+2}}\right)
$$

If we iterate forward by imposing a limiting condition that P/D does not explode faster than returns, we can take expectations and write:

$$
\frac{P_t}{D_t}
= E_t\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}
\left(\prod_{j=1}^{i}R_{t+j}^{-1}\right)\frac{D_{t+i}}{D_t}\right]
$$

_Intuition: today's P/D ratio is high if people expect <b>high future dividend growth</b> or <b>low future return</b>.

Note that, due to its complexity, we often use the approximation suggested by, _again,_ Campbell and Shiller using logs, and we obtain:

$$
p_t - d_t = -r_{t+1} + \Delta d_{t+1}
+ \ln\left(1 + \exp(p_{t+1} - d_{t+1})\right)
$$

that, _if we do a Taylor approximation around the log at a point $P/D = e^{p-d}$,_ i.e.

[formula of taylor]

we get:

$$
p_t - d_t = -r_{t+1} + \Delta d_{t+1}
+ c + \rho(p_{t+1} - d_{t+1})
$$

where $c = \ln(1 + P/D) - \rho(p - d)$ and $\rho = P/(P + D)$ is always less than, but close to, one.

If we iterate forward and impose the limiting condition, we take expectations:

$$
p_t - d_t = \text{const}
+ E_t\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\rho^{i-1}
(\Delta d_{t+i} - r_{t+i})\right]
$$

However, in its famous paper <a href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/1802789" target="_blank">Do stock prices move too much to be justified by subsequent changes in dividends?</a>, Shiller immediately noticed that, _since equity prices are quite volatile, this variation in price was not justified by changes in dividends or cash flow._ It turns out that <b>excess volatility</b> is the same as return predictability, i.e. if prices are _"too high"_, subsequent returns will be low as prices move back to their _"correct_" level (mean reversion).

So from the multi-period price-dividend equation, we can compute the variance of the P/D ratio as:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(p_t - d_t)
= \operatorname{Cov}\left(p_t - d_t,
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\rho^{i-1}\Delta d_{t+i}\right)
- \operatorname{Cov}\left(p_t - d_t,
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\rho^{i-1}r_{t+i}\right)
$$

We can then see that the dividend yield can only vary if it forecasts changes in future dividend growth or returns. This implies that P/D empirically predicts returns rather than dividend growth, and that variation in the P/D ratio comes from movements in expected returns.

_Note that_ this analysis was conducted at the aggregate level _(focusing on the index)_. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/1540-6261.00417" target="_blank">Vuolteenaho in 2002</a> showed that most of the variation in <b>single-stock returns</b> comes from cash-flow news rather than expected return news.

Indeed, at the single-stock level, D/P is driven by changes in cash flows, while at the aggregate level it is driven by changes in expected returns.

This happens because cash flow news about single stocks is highly idiosyncratic and is removed by diversification when stocks are combined into portfolios; movements in the aggregate portfolio/index mostly reflect changes in expected returns, which are driven by macro conditions.

<b>D/P and the term premium</b>

Fama and French, in their 1988 paper <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(88)90020-7" target="_blank">"Dividend Yields and Expected Stock Returns,"</a> predict returns on stocks and long-term corporate bonds using the D/P and the <b>term premium</b> (TP), defined as:
$$
TP = AAA Bond Yield - 1-month T-bill yield
$$

_Firstly,_ they discovered that D/P and TP predict returns in both markets, so this suggests there is a <b>common variation</b> in expected returns across markets.

Furthermore, one year later, in their 1989 paper <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(89)90095-0" target="_blank">Business Conditions and Expected Returns on Stocks and Bonds</a>, they found that expected returns on stocks and bonds are linked and share a common <b>counter-cyclical component</b>.

_In particular,_ they ask whether the same variables that track the business cycles also predict returns on stocks and bonds by regressing returns on three predictors, at horizons from one month to four years:
- dividend yield on the market portfolio _(high in bad times since prices are low)_
- default spread, BAA yield minus the AA yield _(widens in recessions and stays wide afterward)_
- TS _(low near the cycle peak; it moves faster than D/P and tracks shorter cycles)_

The main finding is that all the regressors have predictive power for both asset classes, with $R^2$ growing with the horizon. _In particular,_ the pattern is <b>counter-cyclical</b>: expected returns are highest at business cycle lows and lowest at the peaks.

_Furthermore,_ different variables capture different parts of the cycle: D/P and the default spread focus on the long-term persistent component, while the TP focuses on the short-term cycle and relates more to bond returns. _Lastly,_ the variation over time in the risk premium related to D/P is stronger for low-grade bonds than for high-grade bonds and stronger for stocks than for bonds.

## <a id="7">Volatility, News Sentiment and Seasonality: Further Predictors of Equity Returns</a>

<b>Volatility</b>

We recall that the optimal exposure to stocks depends on the risk-return trade-off, not just on the ERP; i.e., we have that:

$$
w_t = \frac{1}{a}\frac{\mathbb{E}_t[R_{t+1}] - R_{f,t+1}}{\operatorname{Var}_t[R_{t+1}]}
= \frac{1}{a}\frac{\mathbb{E}_t[\mathrm{ERP}_{t+1}]}{\operatorname{Var}_t[R_{t+1}]}.
$$

Moreira and Muir, in their 2017 paper <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12513" target="_blank">"Volatility-Managed Portfolios,"</a> show that <b>return variance</b> is highly forecastable, since it is persistent and tends to cluster. _In particular,_ a highly volatile month is followed by another turbulent month, and autocorrelation is high.

_However,_ they also show that <b>expected returns</b> do not move much with variance: after a high-variance month, the market's expected return does not necessarily change.

The intuition in a mean-variance framework says that investors allocate $w_t \approx \mu_t / \gamma\sigma^2$ to the risky assets (stocks), so they should scale exposure to stocks after volatile periods. However, the authors argue that most investors do not do this, as they are usually _buy-and-hold_ investors and target constant exposure, so their risk changes a lot during crises and their Sharpe ratio drops.

_On the other hand,_ a <b>volatility-scaled</b> portfolio with exposure to stocks $w_t = c / \operatorname{RV}_t$, where $c$ is the average exposure to the strategy _(chosen to match the unconditional standard deviation of the buy-and-hold portfolio)_ and $\operatorname{RV}_t$ is the realized variance of the previous month, can improve the Sharpe ratio.

---
<br/>
<b>News Sentiment</b>

<b>Sentiment analysis</b> uses computer software to understand the tone of news stories and is then used to time exposure to the equity market.

A good example is represented by the work of <a href="https://doi.org/10.3905/jpm.2015.41.2.100" target="_blank">Uhl et al. in 2015</a>, who performed the following analysis:
- they obtained news sentiment analysis from <a href="https://www.lseg.com/" target="_blank">Thomson Reuters News Analytics</a>, where each story is linked to a sentiment that can be positive, neutral, or negative (1, 0, or -1);
- they aggregated news at the company and macro levels and created the <b>sentiment momentum</b>, defined as the recent change in sentiment;
- they smoothed the variable to make the signal less noisy;
- they invested in <a href="https://www.msci.com/our-solutions/indexes/developed-markets" target="_blank">MSCI World</a> equities if aggregate equity sentiment (momentum) was positive and in an investment-grade 3–5-year bond index otherwise, achieving a Sharpe ratio of 1.47.


The main findings, beyond the high Sharpe ratio, were:
- news diffuses slowly _(prices don't fully absorb the information when it appears, and investors have limited attention)_;
- the selection of the smoothing parameter (strength) affects performance;
- change matters more than level, as sentiment levels carry persistent bias and some assets are always covered with more negative sentiment than others.

## <a id="8">The Cross-Section of Expected Returns</a>
