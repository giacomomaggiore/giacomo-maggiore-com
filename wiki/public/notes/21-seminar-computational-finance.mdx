## <b><a id="1"></a>Introduction: Transportation Marketplace Rate Forecast Using Signature Transform</b>

We begin by approaching the paper <a href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.04857" target="_blank">Transportation Marketplace Rate Forecast Using Signature Transform</a>, written by <a href="https://www.math.ucla.edu/people/visiting/haotian.gu" target="_blank">Haotian Gu</a>, <a href="https://ieor.berkeley.edu/people/xin-guo/" target="_blank">Xin Guo</a>, <a href="https://www.informs.org/Recognizing-Excellence/Award-Recipients/Timothy-L.-Jacobs" target="_blank">Timothy L. Jacobs</a>, <a href="https://kaminsky.ieor.berkeley.edu/" target="_blank">Philip Kaminsky</a>, and <a href="https://www.maths.ox.ac.uk/people/xinyu.li" target="_blank">Xinyu Li</a> in December 2024.

In the following section, I try to provide a better understanding and summary of the paper, while still reviewing theoretical foundations.

<b><a id="1.1"></a>Introduction: overview of freight marketplace rates and challenges in forecasting</b>

The starting point of the paper is Amazon's key problem in forecasting transportation freight marketplace rates, driven by a lot of factors _(networks of thousands of drivers, shippers of all sizes, seasonal and regular demands, traditional and digital exchanges, local, regional, and national economic factors, etc.)_ and often going through fundamental shifts _(because of wars, pandemics, fuel prices, or shifts in international patterns)_.

In this context, traditional time-series techniques may fail to predict transportation freight rates. <br/><b>Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA)</b> and <b>Exponential Smoothing</b> assume that time series are stationary, but this is not the case. _Furthermore,_ long-term trends and seasonality are not well captured by ARIMA. _Lastly,_ these methods rely only on historical data from the time series and are not able to capture the causal relationship between economic factors and the target prediction variable _(i.e., the transportation freight rates)_.

Indeed, a big challenge in analyzing time-series data is their changing statistical properties caused by changes in the business and economic cycle, shifts in policy, or changes in market conditions, _for example,_ as we have recently seen in terms of volatility, demand, and supply during COVID-19 or the war in Ukraine.

To address these problems, we can approach <b>statistical learning</b>, which relies on finding a feature map that maps the input data _(in our case: samples of time series)_ into a multidimensional feature space, with the following two important requirements:
- <b>universality</b>: nonlinear functions of the input data are approximated by linear functionals in the feature space, so the forecasting problem is translated into a linear regression;
- <b>characteristicness</b>: the expected value of the feature map characterizes the law of the random variable.

Therefore, the <b>signature transform</b> satisfies these two properties in duality.

Furthermore, the signature kernel allows for computationally efficient comparison of similarities between time-series data, which is particularly useful for identifying different "signature feature maps" _(i.e., the statistical counterpart of identifying different distributions for time-series data in the linearized feature space of the signature transform)_.

We recall that the signature transform _(path signature, signature)_ was introduced and initially studied in algebraic topology by Kuo-Tsai Chen in <a href="https://doi.org/10.1112/plms/s3-4.1.502" target="_blank">Iterated Integrals and Exponential Homomorphisms</a> (Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society) and <a href="https://doi.org/10.2307/1969671" target="_blank">Integration of Paths, Geometric Invariants and a Generalization of the Baker–Hausdorff Formula</a> (Annals of Mathematics), and then applied to the financial markets and machine learning.

_Generally speaking,_ given any continuous or discrete time series, its signature transform produces a vector of <b>real-valued features</b> that extract information such as order and area, and then _explicitly_ considers combinations of different channels to characterize the time series in a _computationally efficient way_.

_Furthermore,_ every continuous function of a time series can be asymptotically approximated by a linear functional of its signature, so, _in other words,_ the signature transform <b>linearizes the complicated feature space</b> and can be used as a powerful tool for pattern identification and forecasting in machine learning.

_Intuition:_ consider each market period as a path with rates, supply and demand, etc. The signature turns the path into a _"fingerprint"_ that captures the values and how they changed. Then the signature kernel compares fingerprints by $k(a,b) = \langle\Phi(a), \Phi(b)\rangle$ to understand how similar they are. During forecasting, historical periods more similar to the current one get more weight in the regression and vice versa, helping the model to adapt to seasonality and market regimes.

---
<br/>
<b><a id="1.2"></a>Methodology</b>

The proposed approach starts by collecting data _(market supply and demand factors)_ and running correlation tests between the marketplace rate and the factors in order to remove <b>nonsignificant</b> factors and identify factors that are collinear. <br />Then, the authors use the <b>universal nonlinearity property</b> of the signature transform to build signature features as candidates for the "internal features" using a feature map that transforms time-series inputs into a vector of features. Note that the map $\Phi(x)$ does not capture only the values, but also their order and interactions.

_Recall that_ collinearity means that predictors overlap, and, in regression, this makes it hard to tell which predictor matters and can make the coefficient estimates unstable.

Furthermore, to avoid overfitting and collinearity between signature features and external factors, they adopted the <b>two-step LASSO</b> for the regression _(helping to shrink coefficients and select a small set of features)_, enhanced with adaptive weights using a signature kernel that allows it to capture changes in regimes or seasonality.

## <b><a id="2"></a>Technical Background</b>

<b><a id="2.1"></a>Tensors</b>

A tensor is a mathematical object that generalizes vectors and matrices. <br/>
_Formally,_ let $V$ be a vector space over a field $\mathbb{F}$. A tensor of type $(r,s)$ on $V$ is an element of $T^r_s(V) = V^{\otimes r} \otimes (V^*)^{\otimes s}$, where $V^*$ is the dual space.

_Equivalently,_ it can be seen as a <b>multilinear map</b>:

$$
\underbrace{V^* \times \cdots \times V^*}_{r\text{ inputs}} \times \underbrace{V \times \cdots \times V}_{s\text{ inputs}} \longrightarrow \mathbb{F}.
$$

_As we said,_ tensors can be seen as a generalization of lower-dimensional data structures:
- rank-0 tensor: scalar
- rank-1 tensor: vector _(requires 1 index)_
- rank-2 tensor: matrix _(requires 2 indices)_
- rank-3 or higher tensor: higher-dimensional "cubes" or "hypercubes" of data _(example: a batch of color images in DL is a 4D structure with shape `[Batch Size, Channels, Height, Width]`)_


Furthermore, we recall that the <b>outer product</b> for $u \in \mathbb{R}^m$ and $v \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the tensor $u \otimes v \in \mathbb{R}^m \otimes \mathbb{R}^n$, with components $(u \otimes v)_{ij} = u_i v_j$. <br/>This translates to the following $m \times n$ matrix, which has all the pairwise component combinations:

$$
u \otimes v =
\begin{bmatrix}
u_1v_1 & \cdots & u_1v_n \\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
u_mv_1 & \cdots & u_mv_n
\end{bmatrix}
$$

---
<br/>

<b><a id="2.2"></a>Signatures of a Continuous Path</b>

Let $\mathbb{R}^{d_1} \otimes \mathbb{R}^{d_2} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathbb{R}^{d_n}$ denote the space of all <b>real tensors</b> with shape $(d_1, \ldots, d_n)$. <br/>
We define a binary operation called <b>tensor product</b>, denoted by $\otimes$, that maps a tensor of shape $(d_1, \ldots, d_n)$ and a tensor of shape $(e_1, \ldots, e_m)$ to a tensor of shape $(d_1, \ldots, d_n, e_1, \ldots, e_m)$ via

$$
(A \otimes B)_{i_1, \ldots, i_n, j_1, \ldots, j_m} = A_{i_1, \ldots, i_n} B_{j_1, \ldots, j_m}.
$$

When applied to two vectors, this is the <b>outer product</b>.
<br/>

Let $(\mathbb{R}^d)^{\otimes k} = \mathbb{R}^d \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathbb{R}^d$ and $v^{\otimes k} = v \otimes \cdots \otimes v$ for $v \in \mathbb{R}^d$, in each case with $k - 1$ tensor product symbols.

_Intuition:_ the tensor product combines two tensors by multiplying their elements _without summing_. _Example:_ multiplying a 2-vector by a 3-vector produces a $2 \times 3$ matrix. If we repeat the operation, we have $v^{\otimes k}$, where each entry is a product of $k$ components, $v_{i_1} \cdots v_{i_k}$.

<b>Definition: signature of $X$ as a collection of iterated integrals</b>

Let $a < b$ and let $X = (X^1, \ldots, X^d): [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}^d$ be a continuous, piecewise-smooth path. <br/>The signature of $X$ is then defined as the <b>collection of iterated integrals</b>:

$$
\operatorname{Sig}(X) = \left(S^k(X)\right)_{k \ge 0}
$$

$$
S^k(X) = \int_{a < t_1 < \cdots < t_k < b} dX_{t_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes dX_{t_k}
$$

$$
S^k(X)_{i_1, \ldots, i_k} = \int_{a < t_1 < \cdots < t_k < b} dX_{t_1}^{i_1} \cdots dX_{t_k}^{i_k}, \qquad 1 \le i_1, \ldots, i_k \le d.
$$

Here, $\otimes$ is the tensor product, $dX_t = \frac{dX_t}{dt}\,dt$, and the $k=0$ term is taken to be $1 \in \mathbb{R}$.

Then, the <b>truncated signature of depth $N$</b> of $X$ is defined as:

$$
\operatorname{Sig}^N(X) = \left(S^k(X)\right)_{0 \le k \le N}.
$$

_The idea is that_ we have a continuous, piecewise-smooth path $X$ ($X_t$ denotes its position at time $t$); the signature records a sequence of features called <b>levels</b>, i.e., level $k$ collects all the $k$-fold iterated integrals of the path:

$$
\operatorname{Sig}(X) = (1, S^1(X), S^2(X), S^3(X), \ldots).
$$

_Specifically,_ for path $X$, its level $k$ is:

$$
S^k(X)_{i_1, \ldots, i_k} = \int_{a < t_1 < \cdots < t_k < b} dX_{t_1}^{i_1} \cdots dX_{t_k}^{i_k}.
$$

_Similarly,_ a 2-fold iterated integral is:

$$
\int_a^b \left(\int_a^{t_2} dX_{t_1}^i\right) dX_{t_2}^j.
$$

The inner integral collects changes in coordinate $i$ up to time $t_2$; the outer integral then pairs those with later changes in coordinate $j$ _(intuition: this measures how much change in coordinate $i$ is followed by change in coordinate $j$, and higher levels represent increasingly detailed ordered combinations of changes)_.

---
<br/>
<b>Example: linear interpolation</b>

We now provide an illustrative example. Suppose $X:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^d$ is the <b>linear interpolation</b> between two points $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^d$ (i.e., the straight line from $x$ to $y$ as time goes from $a$ to $b$), so that:

$$
X_t = x + \frac{t-a}{b-a}(y-x) = (1-s)x + sy, \qquad s = \frac{t-a}{b-a}.
$$

Then its signature is the collection of tensor powers of its total increment $y-x$:

$$
\operatorname{Sig}(X) = \left(1, y-x, \frac{(y-x)^{\otimes 2}}{2!}, \frac{(y-x)^{\otimes 3}}{3!}, \ldots, \frac{(y-x)^{\otimes k}}{k!}, \ldots\right).
$$

To show this, let $\Delta X=y-x$ and $T=b-a$. Then,

$$
X_t=x+\frac{t-a}{b-a}\Delta X, \qquad dX_t=\frac{\Delta X}{b-a}\,dt.
$$

Substituting this into the signature levels gives:

$$
S^1(X)=\int_a^b dX_t=\frac{\Delta X}{T}\int_a^b dt=\Delta X.
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
S^2(X)
&=\int_a^b\left(\int_a^{t_2}dX_{t_1}\right)\otimes dX_{t_2}\\
&=\frac{\Delta X\otimes\Delta X}{T^2}\int_a^b\int_a^{t_2}dt_1\,dt_2\\
&=\frac{\Delta X\otimes\Delta X}{T^2}\int_a^b(t_2-a)\,dt_2\\
&=\frac{\Delta X\otimes\Delta X}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
S^3(X)
&=\frac{(\Delta X)^{\otimes 3}}{T^3}\int_a^b\int_a^{t_3}\int_a^{t_2}dt_1\,dt_2\,dt_3\\
&=\frac{(\Delta X)^{\otimes 3}}{3!}.
\end{aligned}
$$

_In general,_ the ordered region $a<t_1<\cdots<t_k<b$ has volume $T^k/k!$, so we have:

$$
S^k(X)=\frac{(\Delta X)^{\otimes k}}{k!}
$$

So the signature is:

$$
\operatorname{Sig}(X)=\left(1,\,\Delta X,\,\frac{(\Delta X)^{\otimes 2}}{2!},\,\frac{(\Delta X)^{\otimes 3}}{3!},\,\ldots,\,\frac{(\Delta X)^{\otimes k}}{k!},\,\ldots\right).
$$

---
<br/>
<b>Example: one-dimensional smooth path</b>

Suppose $X:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ is a one-dimensional smooth path. Its signature is the <b>collection of powers of its total increment</b>. We can proceed as follows:

$$
S^k(X)=\int_{a<t_1<\cdots<t_k<b}dX_{t_1}\cdots dX_{t_k}.
$$

Let $\Delta X=X_b-X_a$. The integral over the full cube factors into a product of integrals:

$$
\int_{[a,b]^k}dX_{t_1}\cdots dX_{t_k}=\left(\int_a^b dX_t\right)^k=(\Delta X)^k.
$$

The cube divides into $k!$ regions according to the possible orderings of the times. Since the one-dimensional integrand is symmetric in those times, each region contributes equally. Therefore,

$$
S^k(X)=\frac{(\Delta X)^k}{k!}.
$$

This gives:

$$
\operatorname{Sig}(X)=\left(1,\,X_b-X_a,\,\frac{(X_b-X_a)^2}{2!},\,\ldots,\,\frac{(X_b-X_a)^k}{k!},\,\ldots\right).
$$

We immediately notice that this is independent of $X(t)$ for $t\in(a,b)$. Furthermore, if $X$ is a random process, its expected signature is:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Sig}(X)]=\left(1,\,\mathbb{E}[X_b-X_a],\,\frac{1}{2!}\mathbb{E}[(X_b-X_a)^2],\,\ldots,\,\frac{1}{k!}\mathbb{E}[(X_b-X_a)^k],\,\ldots\right).
$$

If these moments exist, they are the moments of $X_b-X_a$. For a high-dimensional stochastic process $X(t)$, the expected signature is the generalization of the moments of the process (in other words, for a stochastic process, its expected signature characterizes the law of $(X_t)$).

Also, this second example shows that the signature of a one-dimensional path depends only on its increment. This implies that the signature of a path itself may not contain sufficient information to fully characterize the path, but this problem can be solved by considering <b>time-augmented</b> versions of the original path.

Indeed, given a path $X:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^d$, we can define the corresponding <b>time-augmented path</b> by $\hat{X}_t=(t,X_t)$, which is a path in $\mathbb{R}^{d+1}$.

---
<br/>
<b>Signature of Discrete Data</b>

If we want to compute the signature of discrete data streams, we can simply use <b>linear interpolation</b> and then apply the signature transform.

We can indeed define the <b>space of streams of data</b> as:

$$
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^d)=\{x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n):x_i\in\mathbb{R}^d,\ n\in\mathbb{N}\}.
$$

Given $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^d)$, the integer $n$ is called the <b>length</b> of $x$. <br/>
_Furthermore,_ for $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$ such that $a<b$, we can fix:

$$
a=u_1<u_2<\cdots<u_{n-1}<u_n=b
$$

Let $X=(X^1,\ldots,X^d):[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^d$ be continuous such that $X_{u_i}=x_i$ for all $i$ and linear on the intervals in between. Then $X$ is called the <b>linear interpolation</b> of $x$.

Given that $X$ is a linear interpolation of $x$, the signature of $x$ _(the initial stream of data)_ is defined as $\operatorname{Sig}(x)=\operatorname{Sig}(X)$, and the same applies to the truncated signatures, i.e., $\operatorname{Sig}^n(x)=\operatorname{Sig}^n(X)$.

<b><a id="2.3"></a>Key Properties of Signature</b>

<b><a id="2.4"></a>Theorem 1: Uniqueness</b>

Let $X:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^d$ be a continuous piecewise-smooth path. Then $\operatorname{Sig}(\hat X)$ uniquely determines $X$ up to translation.

_Intuition:_ the signature collects all iterated integrals, and these integrals are like the moments of a distribution. "Up to translation" means that two paths are considered the same if one is just the other shifted by a constant vector.

---
<br/>
<b><a id="2.5"></a>Theorem 2: Universal nonlinearity (Weierstrass Theorem)</b>

Let $F$ be a real-valued continuous function on continuous piecewise-smooth paths in $\mathbb{R}^d$ and let $\mathcal{K}$ be a compact set of such paths. Also assume that $X_0 = 0$ for all $X \in \mathcal{K}$.

Consider now $\epsilon > 0$. Then there exists a linear functional $L$ such that

$$
\forall X \in \mathcal{K},\ |F(X) - L(\operatorname{Sig}(\hat X))| < \epsilon.
$$

_Intuition:_ any continuous function of a path, even if not linear, can be approximated as closely as we want by a linear combination of signatures.

We can think of this theorem by analogy. The Weierstrass theorem says that any continuous function $f$ on an interval can be approximated by a polynomial, _i.e._
$f(x) \approx a_0 + a_1x + a_2x^2 + \cdots + a_nx^n$

that is <b>linear in the coefficients</b> $a_k$, and the "features" are the monomials $x^k$. _Well,_ theorem 2 is the same: the features are the iterated integrals _(signatures)_, and the polynomial is $L(\operatorname{Sig}) = \sum (\text{coefficients}) \times (\text{iterated integrals})$.

This works _firstly because_ products of signature terms are again signatures _(\implies we can write the product of two iterated integrals as a linear combination of higher-level ones)_ and because the signature separates paths _(i.e. it is unique up to translation, but in the theorem we assume $X_0 = 0$ to avoid this)_.

This <b>universal nonlinearity</b> is the key property of the signature transform, and it is necessary for the model and its applications. _Note also that_ the signature, by definition, is an infinitely-dimensional tensor; however, we often deal with the truncated signature $\operatorname{Sig}^N$ up to some depth $N$.

---
<br/>
<b><a id="2.6"></a>Theorem 3: Factorial Decay</b>

Let $X:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^d$ be a continuous piecewise-smooth path, and let $\|\cdot\|$ be a tensor norm on $(\mathbb{R}^d)^{\otimes k}$.

$$
\left\| \int_{a<t_1<\cdots<t_k<b} \mathrm{d}X_{t_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathrm{d}X_{t_k} \right\| \le \frac{C(X)^k}{k!},
$$

where $C(X)$ is a constant depending on $X$.

_Intuition:_ the "size" of a level-$k$ signature is at most $C(X)^k/k!$, so higher levels of the signature "shrink" faster than the exponential (the norm of the level-$k$ term gets smaller as $k$ grows), so a few levels can capture almost everything and this makes truncation justified.

<br/>

<b><a id="2.7"></a>Theorem 4: Invariance to Time Reparametrization</b>

Let $X:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^d$ be a continuous piecewise-smooth path. Let $\phi:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ be a continuously differentiable, increasing and surjective function. Then $\operatorname{Sig}(X)=\operatorname{Sig}(X\circ\phi)$.

<b>Proof</b>

First of all, we set $Y=X\circ\phi$.

Since $\phi$ is continuous, increasing, and surjective, we have $\phi(0)=0$ and $\phi(1)=1$. Since $\phi\in C^1$ and $X$ is piecewise smooth, $Y$ is also continuous and piecewise smooth.

_Furthermore,_ by the chain rule, we have

$$
\dot{Y}_t=\dot{X}_{\phi(t)}\,\phi'(t).
$$

We now prove the following:

$$
S^k(Y)_{0,t}=S^k(X)_{0,\phi(t)}.
$$

We can then proceed by induction on $k$, first proving that it holds for $k=0$. _Indeed,_ we have:

$$
S^0(Y)_{0,t}=1=S^0(X)_{0,\phi(t)}.
$$

We now do the <b>inductive step</b>, i.e. we assume that it also holds for $k-1$. So we have:

$$
\begin{aligned} S^k(Y)_{0,t} &=\int_0^t S^{k-1}(Y)_{0,u}\otimes \dot Y_u\,du\\ &=\int_0^t S^{k-1}(X)_{0,\phi(u)}\otimes \dot X_{\phi(u)}\,\phi'(u)\,du \qquad\text{(induction hypothesis and chain rule)}\\ &=\int_0^{\phi(t)} S^{k-1}(X)_{0,s}\otimes \dot X_{s}\,ds \qquad\text{(substitution } s=\phi(u))\\ &=S^k(X)_{0,\phi(t)} . \end{aligned}
$$

If we now take $t=1$ and $\phi(1)=1$, we have:

$$
S^k(X\circ\phi)=S^k(Y)_{0,1}=S^k(X)_{0,\phi(1)}=S^k(X)_{0,1}=S^k(X)\quad\text{for all }k\ge0.
$$

---

_Intuition:_ $\phi$ represents the time change, so the path $X\circ\phi$ is the exact same curve as $X$ but at a different speed. The theorem says that speeding up or slowing down $X_t$ doesn't change the signature. This is an important feature because it implies that the signature encodes data by its arrival order and independently of its arrival time.

From this theorem, note that the signature of a (discrete) stream of data is _not_ dependent on the choice of interpolation times $\mu_i$.

_Furthermore,_ if we consider $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^d)$ as a data stream of length $n$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$, then $\operatorname{Sig}^N(x)$ has $M(d,N)$ components, with

$$
M(d,N) = \sum_{k=0}^N d^k = \frac{d^{N+1}-1}{d-1}.
$$

The idea is that, since the truncated signature is a long vector of numbers, each number is one iterated integral of the path, where each level $k$ has $d^k$ components _(note also that this is independent of the length of the data stream)_.

---

<br/>
<b><a id="2.8"></a>Computation of Signature Transform</b>

The signature transform of a data stream can be computed in an efficient way with the help of Chen's identity.

The main idea is that the signature of a path is a list of numbers that represent the path through iterated integrals _(level 1: displacement, level 2: areas between coordinates, etc.)_.

Chen's identity states that the signature of a path made of <b>two consecutive pieces</b> is the <b>product of the signatures of the pieces</b>.

That means that we do _NOT_ have to process the whole path at once, but we can instead compute the signature of each small piece and then multiply them with $\boxtimes$. This can be done _incrementally_ and far more cheaply, so, _when a new data point arrives,_ we can update the signature with one $\boxtimes$ operation instead of recomputing everything.


_Formally speaking,_ we first have to introduce the $\boxtimes$ operator.

Set $A_0 = B_0 = 1$ and define $\boxtimes$ by:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boxtimes : \left(\prod_{k=1}^{N} \left(\mathbb{R}^d\right)^{\otimes k}\right) \times \left(\prod_{k=1}^{N} \left(\mathbb{R}^d\right)^{\otimes k}\right) &\to \prod_{k=1}^{N} \left(\mathbb{R}^d\right)^{\otimes k}, \\
(A_1, \dots, A_N) \boxtimes (B_1, \dots, B_N) &\mapsto \left(\sum_{j=0}^{k} A_j \otimes B_{k-j}\right)_{1 \le k \le N}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This can be interpreted as multiplication of signatures, _i.e._

$$
(A\boxtimes B)_k=\sum_{j=0}^{k}A_j\otimes B_{k-j}
$$

_Note that_ $(\mathbb{R}^d)^{\otimes k}$ is the space of tensors obtained via the tensor product _( $u \otimes v$ is a $d \times d$ matrix with entries $(u \otimes v)_{ab} = u_a v_b$ )_, and $\prod_{k=1}^{N}$ represents a list, not a product of numbers. Thus, each element of $\prod_{k=1}^N (\mathbb{R}^d)^{\otimes k}$ is a tuple $(A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_N)$ where $A_k$ lives at level $k$.

_Furthermore,_ $(A_1,\ldots,A_N) \boxtimes (B_1,\ldots,B_N)$ represents the two signatures being multiplied. Each is a list with one entry per level, and the indices $j$ and $k-j$ sum to $k$.

Then, Chen's identity states that the image of the signature transform forms a group structure with respect to $\boxtimes$.

---
<br/>
<b><a id="2.9"></a>Chen's Identity</b>


_Formally,_ this means that, given a sequence of data $(x_1,\ldots,x_L) \in \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^d)$ and $j \in \{2,\ldots,L-1\}$, we have that

$$
\operatorname{Sig}^N((x_1,\ldots,x_L)) = \operatorname{Sig}^N((x_1,\ldots,x_j)) \boxtimes \operatorname{Sig}^N((x_j,\ldots,x_L)).
$$

<b>Proof of Chen's Identity</b>

We start with the following definition:

$$
S^k(X)_{i_1, \ldots, i_k}=\int_{a<t_1<\cdots<t_k<c} dX^{i_1}_{t_1}\cdots dX^{i_k}_{t_k}
$$

This can be split into $k+1$ disjoint pieces by cutting the interval at $b$, so we have $X_1$ on $[a,b]$ and $X_2$ on $[b,c]$:

$$
\{a<t_1<\cdots<t_k<c\}=\bigsqcup_{j=0}^{k}\underbrace{\{a<t_1<\cdots<t_j<b\}}_{\Delta_j(a,b)}\times\underbrace{\{b<t_{j+1}<\cdots<t_k<c\}}_{\Delta_{k-j}(b,c)}
$$

_Note that_ the pieces are disjoint and the equality holds up to a null set: together they miss only the points where some $t_i=b$, which have measure zero, so they do not change the integral.

Since $X$ is piecewise smooth, the integral with $dX_t=\dot{X}_t\,dt$ is a traditional Lebesgue integral with a bounded integrand.

_So,_ on each piece the integrand is, by construction, a product of a function of $(t_1,\ldots,t_j)$ and a function of $(t_{j+1},\ldots,t_k)$, and Fubini's (Tonelli's) theorem lets the integral factor.

_Recall that_ Fubini's theorem says that, when the integration region is $A \times B$ and the integrand is a product $f(x)g(y)$, the integral is:

$$
\int_{A\times B} f(x)g(y)\,dx\,dy=\Big(\int_A f\Big)\Big(\int_B g\Big)
$$

_In our case,_ this translates to:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\Delta_j(a,b)\times\Delta_{k-j}(b,c)}\!\!\! dX^{i_1}_{t_1}\cdots dX^{i_k}_{t_k}
&=\underbrace{\int_{\Delta_j(a,b)} dX^{i_1}_{t_1}\cdots dX^{i_j}_{t_j}}_{S^j(X_1)_{i_1\ldots i_j}} \cdot \underbrace{\int_{\Delta_{k-j}(b,c)} dX^{i_{j+1}}_{t_{j+1}}\cdots dX^{i_k}_{t_k}}_{S^{k-j}(X_2)_{i_{j+1}\ldots i_k}}\\
&=\bigl(S^j(X_1)\otimes S^{k-j}(X_2)\bigr)_{i_1\ldots i_k}.
\end{aligned}
$$


_And note that_ the product of the two factors is the tensor product $S^j(X_1)\otimes S^{k-j}(X_2)$, so, if we sum over $j$, we get:

$$
S^k(X)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}S^j(X_1)\otimes S^{k-j}(X_2)
$$

This is the $k$-component of $\boxtimes$ as previously mentioned. If we set $A_0 = B_0 = 1$ and apply it for $1 \le k \le N$, we have:

$$
\operatorname{Sig}^N(X)=\operatorname{Sig}^N(X_1)\boxtimes\operatorname{Sig}^N(X_2).
$$

---
<br/>

_Furthermore,_ the signature of a sequence of length 2 can be computed _explicitly_ from the definition. Consider the following.

$$
\exp:\mathbb{R}^d \to \prod_{k=1}^N(\mathbb{R}^d)^{\otimes k}
$$

$$
\exp : v \to \left(v, \frac{v^{\otimes 2}}{2!}, \frac{v^{\otimes 3}}{3!}, \dots, \frac{v^{\otimes N}}{N!}\right),
$$

so we have that

$$
\operatorname{Sig}^N((x_1,x_2)) = \exp(x_2 - x_1).
$$

That is a nice way to compute the signature of a single line segment, where $v$ in the previous formula is the displacement of the segment ($v = x_{i+1} - x_i$) and $v^{\otimes k}$ is $v$ tensored with itself $k$ times _(an order-$k$ array with entries $v_{a_1} \cdot v_{a_2} \cdot \ldots \cdot v_{a_k}$)_.

But, _according to Chen's identity,_ the signature transform can be computed as

$$
\operatorname{Sig}^N((x_1,\ldots,x_L)) = \exp(x_2 - x_1) \boxtimes \exp(x_3 - x_2) \boxtimes \cdots \boxtimes \exp(x_L - x_{L-1}).
$$

This implies that computing the signature of an incoming data stream is doable, efficient and, _most importantly,_ <b>scalable!</b>

Indeed, if we have some data and receive another data point, we do _NOT_ need to recompute the signature of the entire signal. We can just compute the signature of the new piece of information and then combine it with the previously computed signature using the tensor product.

---
<br/>
<b><a id="2.10"></a>Improving computational efficiency</b>

The key component of the signature computation is to evaluate the following:

$$
\left(\prod_{k=1}^{N} \left(\mathbb{R}^d\right)^{\otimes k}\right) \times \mathbb{R}^d \to \prod_{k=1}^{N} \left(\mathbb{R}^d\right)^{\otimes k}, \quad A, z \mapsto A \boxtimes \exp(z).
$$

That means that for each data point we need to compute $A \boxtimes \exp(z)$, and so we have to compute each level from scratch. However, this is not efficient since many terms may overlap _(the $k$th power of $z$ is just the $(k-1)$th power times $z$ again)_, so we can reuse the previous result and multiply it by a small factor.


However, _instead of_ computing $A \boxtimes \exp(z)$ conventionally with the composition of $\exp$ and $\boxtimes$, we can speed up the computation using <b>Horner's method</b>.

_Intuition:_ Horner's method rewrites a polynomial $a_0x^k+a_1x^{k-1}+\dots+a_k$ as $(\dots((a_0x+a_1)x+a_2)\dots)x+a_k$, which uses far fewer multiplications.

In our case, this means expanding $A \boxtimes \exp(z)$ as:

$$
A \boxtimes \exp(z) = \left(\sum_{i=0}^{k} A_i \otimes \frac{z^{\otimes (k-i)}}{(k-i)!}\right)_{1 \le k \le N},
$$


so that the $k$-th term can be computed by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i=0}^{k} A_i \otimes \frac{z^{\otimes (k-i)}}{(k-i)!}
&= \left(\left(\cdots\left(\left(\frac{z}{k} + A_1\right) \otimes \frac{z}{k-1} + A_2\right) \otimes \frac{z}{k-2} + \cdots\right)\right. \\
&\qquad \left.\otimes \frac{z}{2} + A_{k-1}\right) \otimes z + A_k.
\end{aligned}
$$

The cost of level $k$ is the sum of the step sizes:

$$
d^2+d^3+\dots+d^k=\mathcal{O}(d^k),
$$

because this is a geometric series dominated by its last term (for $d\ge2$). Summing over levels gives

$$
\sum_{k=1}^N\mathcal{O}(d^k)=\mathcal{O}(d^N).
$$

_Note that_ this method has <b>fewer scalar multiplications</b> than the conventional approach and reduces the <b>asymptotic complexity</b> from $\mathcal{O}(N d^N)$ _(where $N$ comes from adding up k +1 tensors at each level)_ to $\mathcal{O}(d^N)$.

This rate is <b>asymptotically optimal</b> since the size of the result is itself of size $\mathcal{O}(d^N)$.

---


## <b><a id="3"></a>A Generic Framework for Linear Statistical Models with Signature Transform</b>

The core idea of this section is to use the <b>universal nonlinearity</b> of the signature transform to capture complex nonlinear relationships and translate them into linear models.


<b><a id="3.1"></a>Forecasting Problem</b>

We can start by considering a time-series forecasting problem with two time series, $\{x_\tau\}$ and $\{y_\tau\}$, where $x_\tau \in \mathcal{X} \subset \mathbb{R}^{d_0}$ is a $d_0$-dimensional vector representing the factor values at time $\tau$.

The target $y_\tau$ takes values in $\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}$ _(in our case: the freight rate)_. The goal is to predict the value of $y_{\tau+\Delta_t}$ for a given time interval $\Delta_t$, _that means_ finding a _(possibly nonlinear)_ model $f^*_{\Delta_t} \in \mathcal{F} \subset \{f \mid f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}\}$ such that, as closely as possible,

$$
f^*_{\Delta_t}(x_\tau) \approx y_{\tau+\Delta_t}.
$$

where $\mathcal{F}$ is the class of all possible models. _Note that_ here $f$ is written as a function of the current factor values $x_\tau$ for simplicity; in the next section it will depend on the recent window $x_{\tau-l:\tau}$ through its signature, which is what lets the model use the history of the factors.

Specifically, given data up to time $t$, the standard approach is to solve the following optimization problem:

$$
f^*_{\Delta_t} \in \underset{f\in\mathcal{F}}{\arg\min}
\frac{1}{t-\Delta_t}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-\Delta_t}
L\left(f(x_\tau),y_{\tau+\Delta_t}\right).
$$

where $L:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}$ is the <b>loss function</b>.

---
<br/>
<b><a id="3.2"></a>Linear Statistical Models with Signature Transforms</b>

For any time step $\tau\in\mathbb{N}^*$ and a time window of size $l\in\mathbb{N}$, we can denote the slice of the time series from $\tau-l$ to $\tau$ as

$$
x_{\tau-l:\tau}=(x_{\tau-l},\ldots,x_\tau).
$$

Let $N$ be the depth of the truncated signature, and let $\operatorname{Sig}^N(x_{\tau-l:\tau})$ be the corresponding signature for the truncated path from $\tau-l$ to $\tau$.

We now consider $\Theta_{\text{linear}}$ as the <b>class of linear models</b> that map $\operatorname{Sig}^N(x_{\tau-l:\tau})$ to $y_{\tau+\Delta_t}$.

Given the training data set $\{(x_\tau,y_\tau)\}_{\tau=1}^t$, the <b>optimal predictor</b> $\hat{\theta}$ is obtained by minimizing the <b>empirical loss</b>, _i.e._ by computing:

$$
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \in \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^{M(d_0,N)}} \left\{ \frac{1}{t-\Delta_t} \sum_{\tau=1}^{t-\Delta_t} L_{\text{linear}}\left(y_{\tau+\Delta_t}, \operatorname{Sig}^N(\boldsymbol{x}_{\tau-l:\tau}); \boldsymbol{\theta}\right) \right\},
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ has one component per signature feature, i.e. $M(d_0,N)$ of them _(or $M(d_0+1,N)$ if the window is time-augmented)_.

where $L_{\text{linear}}$ is the loss function. _For example,_ in the case of ordinary least squares (OLS), $L_{\text{linear}}$ is defined as the squared error, _i.e._

$$
L_{\text{OLS}}=\left(y_{\tau+\Delta_t}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}\operatorname{Sig}^N(x_{\tau-l:\tau})\right)^2.
$$

_Furthermore,_ for <b>Lasso regression</b>, the loss function is augmented with an $L_1$-regularization term to promote sparsity in the parameters, _i.e._

$$
L_{\text{Lasso}}=\left(y_{\tau+\Delta_t}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}\operatorname{Sig}^N(x_{\tau-l:\tau})\right)^2+\lambda\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_1.
$$

where $\lambda>0$ is the regularization parameter.

_Similarly,_ <b>Ridge regression</b> adds an $L_2$-regularization term to penalize large parameters, _i.e._

$$
L_{\text{Ridge}}=\left(y_{\tau+\Delta_t}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}\operatorname{Sig}^N(x_{\tau-l:\tau})\right)^2+\lambda\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_2^2.
$$

---
<br/>
<b>Adaptive weight via Signature Kernel</b>

In the classical approach to linear statistical modelling, each historical sample has equal weight in the optimization problem. _However,_ this equal weighting may fail to account for changes in regime or seasonality.

To address this, we can adopt a more effective approach to assign weights based on their similarity to the current period.

_To do so,_ we first recall the <b>signature feature map</b> as:

$$
\Phi:\mathcal{X}\to\operatorname{Sig}(\hat{\mathcal{X}}).
$$

To avoid computations over a large space of functions, we can define the <b>signature kernel</b> for any discrete time series $a,b$ _(where $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ is the inner product on the linear space of signatures)_ as

$$
k(a,b)=\langle\Phi(a),\Phi(b)\rangle.
$$

_Next,_ we want to measure the similarity between two discrete time series $a,b$ by considering the <b>squared distance induced</b> by the signature kernel, defined as

$$
d_{\mathrm{Sig}}(a,b)=k(a,a)-2k(a,b)+k(b,b)=\|\Phi(a)-\Phi(b)\|^2.
$$

_Note that_ this is a squared distance, so it is not a metric (the triangle inequality can fail), and with truncation depth $N$ it is only a pseudometric (two different series can have distance $0$).

_The idea_ is that a small $d_{\mathrm{Sig}}(a,b)$ implies a <b>higher similarity</b> between patterns _(time series)_ in $a,b$ $\implies$ $a,b$ come from the same regime and share the same seasonality.

_Finally,_ we can adapt the weights in the linear models according to the signature kernel _(and this is called 'AdaWeight.Sig')._

_More precisely, as before,_ we can consider a forecast horizon $\Delta_t$, a window size $l$, signature depth $N$, kernel distance $d_{\mathrm{Sig}}^N$, and <b>temperature parameter</b> $\gamma\geq 0$.

Then, we can define, for all $\tau_1,\tau_2\in\mathbb{N}$ with $\tau_1<\tau_2$:

$$
z_\tau=(x_\tau,y_{\tau+\Delta_t})
$$

and then denote by $z_{\tau_1:\tau_2}=(z_{\tau_1},\ldots,z_{\tau_2})$ the slice of the time series $z$ from $\tau_1$ to $\tau_2$.

At each time $t$, 'AdaWeight.Sig' takes the historical samples as inputs and calculates the <b>adaptive weight vector</b> as follows.

$$
\boldsymbol{W}^{(\Delta_t)} := \left(w_1^{(\Delta_t)}, w_2^{(\Delta_t)}, \cdots, w_{t-\Delta_t}^{(\Delta_t)}\right) \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{t-\Delta_t},
$$


such that

$$
\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-\Delta_t}w_\tau^{(\Delta_t)}=1.
$$

_Intuition:_ 'AdaWeight.Sig' assigns a higher weight to the sample if its seasonality pattern near $\tau$ is similar to the current pattern. _Furthermore,_ whenever each new sample arrives, the weight vector is recomputed to adapt to recent changes.

---
<br/>
<b><a id="3.3"></a>Algorithm 1: Adaptive Weight via Signature Kernel ('AdaWeight.Sig')</b>

1. <b>Input</b>: forecast horizon $\Delta_t$, window size $l$, depth $N$, temperature $\gamma$, kernel distance $d_{\mathrm{Sig}}^N$, dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{z_\tau=(x_\tau,y_{\tau+\Delta_t})\}_{1\leq\tau\leq t-\Delta_t}$.
2. For $\tau=1,2,\ldots,t-\Delta_t$:
    - compute the distance $\delta_\tau$ between the truncated signatures of $z_{\tau-l:\tau}$ and $z_{t-\Delta_t-l:t-\Delta_t}$:

    $$
    \delta_\tau=d_{\mathrm{Sig}}^N(z_{\tau-l:\tau},z_{t-\Delta_t-l:t-\Delta_t}).
    $$

3. For $\tau=1,2,\ldots,t-\Delta_t$:
    - compute the weights $w_\tau^{(\Delta_t)}$ as

    $$
    w_\tau^{(\Delta_t)}:=\frac{\exp(-\gamma\delta_\tau)}{\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-\Delta_t}\exp(-\gamma\delta_\tau)}.
    $$


4. Output the weight vector $\boldsymbol{W}^{(\Delta_t)}=(w_1^{(\Delta_t)},\ldots,w_{t-\Delta_t}^{(\Delta_t)})$.
We can now spend some time defining the algorithm.

---

<br/>
<b><a id="3.4"></a>Algorithm 2: Adaptive Linear Models with Signature Kernel</b>

Once we have defined the adaptive weight algorithm as above, we can proceed with the forecasting algorithm.

1. <b>Input</b>: forecast horizon $\Delta_t$, regularization parameter $\lambda$, window size $l$, depth $N$, temperature parameter $\gamma$, kernel distance $d_{\mathrm{Sig}}^N$, and dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{z_\tau=(x_\tau,y_{\tau+\Delta_t})\}_{1\leq\tau\leq t-\Delta_t}$.
2. Run 'AdaWeight.Sig' _(Algorithm 1, as defined above)_ once, at the current time $t$, with $\Delta_t$, $l$, $N$, $\gamma$, $d_{\mathrm{Sig}}^N$, and $\mathcal{D}$ to <b>identify the regime</b> and obtain $\boldsymbol{W}^{(\Delta_t)}$.
3. Fit the linear model on the dataset $\mathcal{D}$ with the <b>adaptive weights</b> $\boldsymbol{W}^{(\Delta_t)}$ and regularization parameter $\lambda$, i.e. solve the weighted problem _(the sum already runs over all $\tau$)_:

    $$
    \widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\lambda}_{\Delta_t} \in \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^{M(d_0,N)}} \left\{ \sum_{\tau=1}^{t-\Delta_t} w_\tau^{(\Delta_t)} \cdot L_{\text{linear}}\left(y_{\tau+\Delta_t}, \operatorname{Sig}^N(\boldsymbol{x}_{\tau-l:\tau}); \boldsymbol{\theta}\right) \right\}.
    $$

4. Predict:

    $$
    \widehat{y}_{t+\Delta_t} = \operatorname{Sig}^N(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-l:t}) \cdot \widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\lambda}_{\Delta_t}.
    $$


_To sum up,_ we can say that measuring similarity via the signature kernel leads to a new linear modelling approach, where we adapt weights according to similarities between time series to capture seasonality and regime.

Note that this is equivalent to identifying different distributions for a given set, but in a linearized feature space from the signature transform.

## <b><a id="4"></a>Signature-Based Deep Hedging</b>

We now present an example of application of the signature transform. In particular, we will consider a <b>self-financing strategy</b> that replicates a <b>path-dependent terminal payoff</b>.

We will simulate asset prices, encode the asset price's path using a truncated time-augmented signature, then fit a linear signature strategy by $L_2$ regression and finally train a nonlinear neural strategy on 256 newly simulated paths.


Before starting, we define the requirements.

```python

import copy
import math
import numpy as np
import torch
from torch import nn
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Dependencies: numpy, torch, matplotlib
torch.set_num_threads(2)
torch.manual_seed(7)
```

<br/>
<b>Asset Prices</b>

We can model asset prices using a <b>discretized geometric Brownian motion</b> given by

$$
S_{n+1}=S_n\exp\left((\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2)\Delta t+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_n\right).
$$

with $Z_n\sim N(0,1)$ and $S_0=1$. Solving the GBM stochastic differential equation gives this expression, so there is no Euler error in the prices.

_Furthermore,_ note that the term $-\frac12\sigma^2$ is the <b>Itô correction</b> (convexity correction) applied because, _without it,_ the expected growth would be larger than $\mu$.


```python
n_calibration, n_val, n_test = 2048, 2048, 4096
paths_per_epoch, epochs = 256, 2000
n_pricing = 100_000
n_steps, n_windows, d = 40, 10, 1
T, sigma, rho, strike = 1.0, 0.25, 0.3, 1.0
mu, r = 0.08, 0.03
depth = 3
claim_type = 'geometric_put'  # Alternative: 'asian_call'
weights = torch.full((d,), 1.0 / d)

assert n_steps % n_windows == 0, 'Each window must contain an integer number of steps.'
assert d == 1 or -1 / (d - 1) < rho < 1

feature_dim = sum((d + 1)**k for k in range(1, depth + 1))
memory_gb = (n_calibration + paths_per_epoch + n_val + n_test) * n_steps * feature_dim * 4 / 1e9

print(f'{feature_dim} features; approximately {memory_gb:.2f} GB for feature arrays.')

# 14 features; approximately 0.02 GB for feature arrays.


def simulate_paths(n_paths, seed, drift):
    """Return prices [path, time, asset], including time zero."""
    generator = torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed)

    # Correlation matrix between assets.
    corr = (1 - rho) * torch.eye(d) + rho * torch.ones(d, d)

    # Compute the lower-triangular L, with LL^T = corr.
    chol = torch.linalg.cholesky(corr)

    # Draw independent N(0,1) shocks and multiply by L^T.
    z = torch.randn(n_paths, n_steps, d, generator=generator) @ chol.T
    dt = T / n_steps

    # Log return at each step (Gaussian with mean (drift − ½σ²)Δt
    # and standard deviation σ√Δt).
    log_returns = (drift - 0.5 * sigma**2) * dt + sigma * math.sqrt(dt) * z
    log_prices = torch.cat([torch.zeros(n_paths, 1, d), log_returns.cumsum(1)], dim=1)
    return log_prices.exp()

S_calibration = simulate_paths(n_calibration, 10, drift=mu)
S_val = simulate_paths(n_val, 20, drift=mu)
S_test = simulate_paths(n_test, 30, drift=mu)
times = torch.linspace(0, T, n_steps + 1)


```

_Consider that_ in the previous code we assumed `n_steps = 40` and $T=1$ year, so we have $\Delta t=0.025$ ($\approx$ rebalanced hedging every six trading days).

![Bias variance tradeoff](/images/notes/third-semester/signature-1.png)
</div>

In the notebook, we use the $\mathbb P$ (physical) measure, with drift $\mu=0.08$ and volatility $\sigma=0.25$, for training, validation, testing, and feature calibration. We use the $\mathbb Q$ (risk-neutral) measure, with drift $r=0.03$ and the same volatility, for pricing only.

_Intuition:_ we need $\mathbb Q$ for pricing because it gives the initial required capital $\tilde b$, which represents _"the fair cost of hedging"_. However, we need $\mathbb P$ for training and testing because we are interested in how the strategy performs in the real world.

_Note that_ moving from P to Q changes only the drift through the <b>Girsanov change of measure</b>.

Indeed, under P, the price solves $dS_t=\mu S_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW^{\mathbb P}_t$, where $W^{\mathbb P}$ is a P-Brownian motion. Let $ \theta=\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma}$ be the <b>market price of risk</b> _(the excess return per unit of volatility)_.

<b>Girsanov's theorem</b> _(constant-drift case)_ states that, if $ L_t=\exp\left(-\theta W^{\mathbb P}_t-\tfrac12\theta^2t\right) $ is a martingale _(which is true here because $\theta$ is constant, e.g. by Novikov's condition)_, then the measure $\mathbb Q$ defined by $\frac{d\mathbb Q}{d\mathbb P}\big|_{\mathcal F_T}=L_T$ is equivalent to $\mathbb P$ and $W^{\mathbb Q}_t=W^{\mathbb P}_t+\theta t $ is a $\mathbb Q$-Brownian motion.

_Substituting_ $dW^{\mathbb P}_t=dW^{\mathbb Q}_t-\theta\,dt$ in the dynamics of $S$:

$$
dS_t=\mu S_tdt+\sigma S_t\left(dW^{\mathbb Q}_t-\theta dt\right)=\bigl(\mu-\sigma\theta\bigr)S_tdt+\sigma S_tdW^{\mathbb Q}_t=rS_tdt+\sigma S_tdW^{\mathbb Q}_t.
$$

_Intuition:_ the change of measure removes the risk premium $\mu-r$ from the drift and replaces it with the risk-free rate, while the randomness stays the same.


$\sigma$, _on the other hand_ is unchanged since it is linked to the path's quadratic variation: $d\langle\log S\rangle_t=\sigma^2dt$.
_Furthermore,_ note that under $\mathbb Q$ the discounted price $\tilde S_t=e^{-rt}S_t$ is a martingale, which _formally_ means:

$$
\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}\left[\tilde S_t\mid\mathcal F_s\right]=\tilde S_s,\qquad 0\le s\le t\le T.
$$

_To see this,_ solve the SDE under $\mathbb Q$:

$$
\tilde S_t=\tilde S_s\exp\left(-\tfrac12\sigma^2(t-s)+\sigma\bigl(W^{\mathbb Q}_t-W^{\mathbb Q}_s\bigr)\right).
$$

The increment $W^{\mathbb Q}_t-W^{\mathbb Q}_s$ is independent of $\mathcal F_s$ and $N(0,t-s)$, and $\mathbb E[e^{\sigma N(0,t-s)}]=e^{\frac12\sigma^2(t-s)}$, so the factors cancel and we get $\tilde S_s$. On the grid the same computation gives $\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}[\tilde S_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=\tilde S_n$.

_Under_ $\mathbb P$ _instead,_ the same computation gives $\mathbb E^{\mathbb P}[\tilde S_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=\tilde S_ne^{(\mu-r)\Delta t}$: the discounted price drifts upward at the risk premium, so it is _NOT_ a martingale.

This also implies that, _for any holding $h$,_ the discounted gains $\sum_k h_k\cdot\Delta\tilde S_k$ have mean zero under $\mathbb Q$. _Indeed,_ since $h_k$ is $\mathcal F_{t_k}$-measurable, by the tower property

$$
\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}\left[h_k\cdot\Delta\tilde S_k\right]=\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}\Bigl[h_k\cdot\underbrace{\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}[\Delta\tilde S_k\mid\mathcal F_{t_k}]}_{=0}\Bigr]=0
$$

_(assuming enough integrability, e.g. bounded or square-integrable $h$)_. Hence $\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}[e^{-rT}V_T]=V_0$.

_To sum up..._ if a strategy replicated the claim exactly, $V_T=H$, then $V_0=\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}[e^{-rT}H]$. This is why the Q-expectation is the <b>no-arbitrage price,</b> which is why we use it as initial capital.

_Under_ $\mathbb P$ the gains have mean $\neq0$ _(they earn the risk premium)_, which is why the hedging error under $\mathbb P$ can show a bias.

---
<br/>
<b>Path-dependent claim</b>

We now split $[0,T]$ into $M$ equal windows and, _for each simulated asset,_ compute the arithmetic average in each window and then the geometric mean across windows.

$$
A_j^i=\frac{1}{u_{j+1}-u_j}\int_{u_j}^{u_{j+1}}S_t^i\,dt,
\qquad I_i=\exp\left(\frac1M\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\log A_j^i\right)
$$

We then build the <b>terminal claim</b> as

$$
H=\left(K-\sum_{i=1}^d w_i I_i\right)^+.
$$

_We immediately notice_ that $H$ <b>depends on the whole trajectory,</b> not only on the price at expiry, so the value at time $t$ is _NOT_ a function of $(t,S_t)$ alone.

_Therefore,_ we cannot use Black–Scholes; we can use the signature for hedging because it represents how the path has evolved so far.


_Note that_ the payoff $H$ can be defined as an <b>Asian-style put</b> with a variant. Indeed, an Asian put value depends on the average (arithmetic or geometric average across the windows) of the underlying price over time, not on $S_T$ alone.

_Formally,_ this means:

$$
\underbrace{(K-S_T)^+}_{\text{vanilla put}},
\qquad
\underbrace{\Bigl(K-\tfrac1T\int_0^TS_t\,dt\Bigr)^+}_{\text{arithmetic Asian put}}
\qquad
\underbrace{\Bigl(K-\exp\bigl(\tfrac1T\int_0^T\log S_t\,dt\bigr)\Bigr)^+}_{\text{geometric Asian put}}.
$$

Our payoff $H=(K-I)^+$ sits between the last two: if $M=1$, then $I$ is the arithmetic average over $[0,T]$; if each window shrinks to a single observation date, $I$ is the geometric average of the prices.

In our case, we are dealing with a particular case: in each window the average is not lognormal, so there's no exact closed form for the full payoff _(only the pure geometric Asian put has one, since the geometric average of a lognormal process is lognormal)_.


```python
def terminal_claim(S, kind=claim_type):
    """Return one terminal payoff per path."""
    midpoint_prices = 0.5 * (S[:, :-1] + S[:, 1:])
    if kind == 'geometric_put':
        steps_per_window = n_steps // n_windows
        window_means = midpoint_prices.reshape(-1, n_windows, steps_per_window, d).mean(2)
        geometric_means = window_means.log().mean(1).exp()
        return (strike - geometric_means @ weights).clamp_min(0)
    if kind == 'asian_call':
        arithmetic_means = midpoint_prices.mean(1)
        return (arithmetic_means @ weights - strike).clamp_min(0)
    raise ValueError('Unknown claim type')
```


_Furthermore,_ for this case, we can estimate the <b>risk-neutral value</b> of the discretized claim using 100,000 independent pricing paths with drift $r$ and discounting the payoff to time zero. _Formally, we have:_

$$
\widehat p\approx\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}[e^{-rT}H],\qquad\widehat p=\frac{e^{-rT}}{N_{\mathrm{price}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{price}}}H^{(j)}
$$

```python
def estimate_price(n_paths, batch_size=4096):
    """Estimate the claim price and its Monte Carlo standard error."""
    payoffs = []

    # Walk through 100,000 paths in chunks of 4,096 (25 batches)
    # to keep memory small.
    for batch, start in enumerate(range(0, n_paths, batch_size)):
        size = min(batch_size, n_paths - start)

        # simulation under Q
        S = simulate_paths(size, seed=1_000_000 + batch, drift=r)
        payoffs.append(terminal_claim(S))

    # Discounting.
    H = math.exp(-r * T) * torch.cat(payoffs).double()

    # return price p = mean(H) and standard error: std(H̃)/√N
    return H.mean().item(), (H.std() / math.sqrt(n_paths)).item()

initial_capital, price_se = estimate_price(n_pricing)
H_val = terminal_claim(S_val)
H_test = terminal_claim(S_test)

print(f'Fixed initial capital (risk-neutral price): {initial_capital:.5f}')
print(f'Pricing Monte Carlo SE: {price_se:.5f}')
print(f'Approximate 95% pricing CI: [{initial_capital - 1.96*price_se:.5f}, '
      f'{initial_capital + 1.96*price_se:.5f}]')
```

<b>Time-augmented Signature</b>

The idea is that the hedging strategy at time t only <b>depends on the path </b> of the asset price up to t. We can then compress this information into the <b>signature</b>.

_To do so,_ we firstly consider the following <b>time-augmented path</b> for the generic case with d assets:

$$
Z_t=(t/T,S_t^1/S_0^1-1,\ldots,S_t^d/S_0^d-1)
$$

where the time channel is added since the plain signature ignores the "timing" dynamics of the path _(i.e. it ignores how fast the path moves)_.

We then normalize prices to start at 0 and consider the case with $d=1$ asset, so the path has a total of 2 channels.

The path is <b>linearly interpolated</b> between two observations, so if we consider the segment $v=Z_{n+1}-Z_n$, the signature has the following <b>closed form</b>:

$$
E^{(0)}=1,\qquad E^{(k)}=\frac{v^{\otimes k}}{k!}
$$

We can now apply <b>Chen's identity</b> to update the signature only when a new segment is added _(i.e. when a new data point is received)_:

$$
A_{\mathrm{new}}^{(k)}=\sum_{j=0}^k A_{\mathrm{old}}^{(j)}\otimes E^{(k-j)}
$$

_Then,_ we apply <b>truncation</b> so that only levels $1,\ldots,m$ are kept _(in this case, $m=3$)_. The number of features is therefore $\sum_{k=1}^m (d+1)^k$ _(it grows exponentially with depth and asset count)_.

In the code, `tensor_product` computes the flattened outer product. Then `prefix_signatures` loops over time steps, appends the current signature, adds a new segment, and builds the segment's levels <b>recursively</b> using $E^{(k)}=E^{(k-1)}\otimes v/k$ and Chen's identity.

```python
def tensor_product(a, b):
    """Flattened tensor product for each path."""
    return (a.unsqueeze(-1) * b.unsqueeze(-2)).flatten(1)

def prefix_signatures(Z, depth):
    """Prefix signatures before each price increment: [batch, steps, features]."""
    batch, nodes, channels = Z.shape
    levels = [torch.ones(batch, 1, dtype=Z.dtype)]
    levels += [torch.zeros(batch, channels**k, dtype=Z.dtype) for k in range(1, depth + 1)]
    prefixes = []
    for n in range(nodes - 1):
        # Record the prefix at n before adding the segment n -> n+1.
        prefixes.append(torch.cat(levels[1:], dim=1))
        increment = Z[:, n + 1] - Z[:, n]
        segment = [torch.ones(batch, 1, dtype=Z.dtype)]
        for k in range(1, depth + 1):
            segment.append(tensor_product(segment[-1], increment) / k)
        old = levels
        levels = [sum(tensor_product(old[j], segment[k-j]) for j in range(k+1))
                  for k in range(depth + 1)]
    return torch.stack(prefixes, dim=1)

def make_features(S):
    time_channel = times.reshape(1, -1, 1).expand(S.shape[0], -1, -1) / T
    relative_prices = S / S[:, :1] - 1
    augmented_path = torch.cat([time_channel, relative_prices], dim=-1)
    return prefix_signatures(augmented_path, depth)
```

We then standardize each feature into $(x - \text{mean})/\text{std}$ so every feature _(every iterated integral)_ has a comparable scale.

```python
# Fit scaling once on an independent calibration sample.

feature_mean = F_calibration.mean(dim=(0, 1), keepdim=True)
feature_scale = F_calibration.std(dim=(0, 1), keepdim=True).clamp_min(1e-6)

def scaled_features(S):
    return (make_features(S) - feature_mean) / feature_scale
```

<b>Self-financing Portfolio and $L_2$ Objective</b>

At each date $t$, suppose we choose to hold $h_n$ shares of the asset using only information up to $t$, with initial fixed capital $\hat p$.

The cash balance is then b_n = V_n - h_n S_n, subject to interest rate r, so the <b>total wealth</b> is:

$$
V_{n+1}=h_n\cdot S_{n+1}+(V_n-h_n\cdot S_n)e^{r\Delta t}
$$

where the first term represents the share's new value and the second the cash left in the bank at interest rate $r$. Note that nothing is added or withdrawn, so the strategy is <b>self-financing</b>.

We now define the <b>discounted asset price</b> as $\widetilde S_n=e^{-rt_n}S_n$. If we divide the recursion by $e^{rt_{n+1}}$, we reach the following closed form for the total wealth:

$$
V_n=e^{rt_n}\Big(\widehat p+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}h_k\cdot\Delta\widetilde S_k\Big)
$$

_Intuition:_ the discounted wealth equals the initial capital plus the accumulated and discounted trading gains.

We now want to minimize the mean squared hedging error at maturity under the physical measure $\mathbb P$; that means that we want to minimize the following <b>loss function</b>:

$$
\mathcal L(h)=\mathbb E^{\mathbb P}[(V_N-H)^2]
$$

Since $V_N=e^{rT}(\widehat p+\text{gains})$ and the rest is fixed, this implies minimizing:

$$
\mathbb E^{\mathbb P}\Big[\Big(\sum_n h_n\cdot\Delta\widetilde S_n-Y\Big)^2\Big],\qquad Y=e^{-rT}H-\widehat p
$$

So the target is $Y$, the discounted claim minus the initial capital, and the model tries to reproduce it with trading gains.

_Note that_ this is not a regression of the claim on the signature of the whole path. The model is fitted through the trading gains; hence, the signature only appears as the input for the holdings $h_n$.

```python
def wealth_from_holdings(h, S, capital):
    """Self-financing wealth, including interest on the cash balance."""

    bank_account = torch.exp(r * times).reshape(1, -1)
    discounted_S = S / bank_account.unsqueeze(-1)
    gains = (h * (discounted_S[:, 1:] - discounted_S[:, :-1])).sum(dim=-1)
    initial = torch.as_tensor(capital, dtype=S.dtype).expand(S.shape[0], 1)

    discounted_V = torch.cat([initial, initial + gains.cumsum(dim=1)], dim=1)
    return bank_account * discounted_V
```

<b>Linear Signature Hedging by Least Squares</b>

The starting point of the hedging problem is that, _for one path,_ the trading gain is a sum over 40 dates:

$$
G=\sum_{n=0}^{39} h_n\,\Delta\widetilde S_n
$$

where each term is the profit or loss of that interval. We then want $G$ to be as close as possible to $Y=e^{-rT}H-\tilde p$, the discounted payoff minus the Asian put we owe and the initial capital _(which implies that the trading gains have to cover that difference)_.

We now consider $F_m$, the standardized signature up to degree $m$, and include a constant coordinate by defining:

$$
\Phi_n=(1,F_n)\in\mathbb R^q
$$



def add_constant(features):
    return torch.cat([torch.ones_like(features[..., :1]), features], dim=-1)


We then define the holdings $h_n$ as a linear combination of $\Phi_n$:

$$
h_n=C^\top\phi_n=C_0\phi_{n,0}+C_1\phi_{n,1}+\dots+C_{14}\phi_{n,14}=\sum_a C_a\,\phi_{n,a}.
$$

_Intuition:_ we want to find the weights $C$ at every date, and the time dependence comes from the feature because the signature contains $t/T$. The strategy is therefore a <b>linear combination</b> of the path's iterated integrals.

We can now substitute $h_n=\sum_a C_a\,\phi_{n,a}$ in the gains formula and change the order of summation since $C_a$ does not depend on $n$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
G &= \sum_n\Big(\sum_a C_a\phi_{n,a}\Big)\Delta\widetilde S_n
= \sum_n\sum_a C_a\,\phi_{n,a}\,\Delta\widetilde S_n \\
&= \sum_a C_a\underbrace{\Big(\sum_n \phi_{n,a}\,\Delta\widetilde S_n\Big)}_{A_a} \\
&= C_0A_0+C_1A_1+\dots+C_{14}A_{14}.
\end{aligned}
$$

We recall that $A_a=\sum_n\phi_{n,a}\Delta\widetilde S_n$ is the gain we would make following the _"elementary strategy"_ _(hold $\phi_{n,a}$ shares at date $n$, with weight 1; this is a number you can compute for each path before fitting anything.)_

```python
def regression_batch(S):
    phi = add_constant(scaled_features(S)).double()

    discounted_S = S.double() * torch.exp(-r * times.double()).reshape(1, -1, 1)

    increments = discounted_S[:, 1:] - discounted_S[:, :-1]

    # build A with path b, date n, feature q, asset i
    A = torch.einsum('bnq,bni->bqi', phi, increments).flatten(1)
    y = math.exp(-r * T) * terminal_claim(S).double() - initial_capital
    return A, y
```

_Again,_ we want $G$ to match $y$ ($y\approx AC$), so we can find $\hat C$ that minimizes the squared loss:

$$
\widehat C=\arg\min_C\ \frac1J\sum_{j}\Big(y_j-\sum_aC_aA_{j,a}\Big)^2=\arg\min_C\frac1J\|y-AC\|^2
$$

where $j$ indexes the paths, and each $y_j-\sum_aC_aA_{j,a}$ is the discounted hedging error on path $j$ _(note that this is the "Monte Carlo version" of $\mathbb E^{\mathbb P}[(\text{gain}-Y)^2]$)_

If we set the gradient to zero, we obtain $A^\top A\,C=A^\top y$ and, if $A^\top A$ is invertible, we get $\widehat C=(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top y$.

_However,_ note that many columns are _nearly or exactly_ <b>linearly dependent</b>, so inverting the matrix gives <b>unstable coefficients</b>.

Therefore, we can use an <b>SVD-based solver</b> that handles dependencies without inverting $A^\top A$.

```python
def fit_linear_signature(n_batches=epochs, batch_size=paths_per_epoch):
    """Fit empirical L2 hedging on the same simulated paths as the NN."""
    rows, targets = [], []

    # Generate 2,000 batches of 256 paths under the physical measure P.
    for batch in range(n_batches):
        S = simulate_paths(batch_size, seed=10_000 + batch, drift=mu)
        A, y = regression_batch(S)
        rows.append(A)
        targets.append(y)
    A, y = torch.cat(rows), torch.cat(targets)

    # lstsq with gelsd solves min|AC-y|^2 with an SVD
    # solution: vector of 15 numbers
    fit = torch.linalg.lstsq(A, y, driver='gelsd', rcond=1e-10)
    coefficients = fit.solution.reshape(feature_dim + 1, d).float()
    print(f'Least squares: {len(y):,} paths, {A.shape[1]} coefficients, rank {fit.rank.item()}')
    return coefficients

def linear_portfolio(coefficients, features, S):
    h = add_constant(features) @ coefficients
    return wealth_from_holdings(h, S, initial_capital), h
```

Once we have fitted the model and found $C$, we can apply $h_n=C^\top\phi_n$ at every date $n$ and then compute the wealth, starting from $\tilde p$.

```python
linear_coefficients = fit_linear_signature()
with torch.no_grad():
    V_linear_val, _ = linear_portfolio(linear_coefficients, F_val, S_val)
    print(f'Linear validation MSE: {(V_linear_val[:, -1] - H_val).square().mean():.6f}')

# The regression prediction must equal the discounted trading gain.
A_check, _ = regression_batch(S_val[:4])
V_check, _ = linear_portfolio(linear_coefficients, F_val[:4], S_val[:4])

```

<b>Neural Signature Hedging</b>

We now apply the same procedure, but replace the linear map with a <b>nonlinear neural network</b> of the following form:

$$
h_n=f_\theta(F_n)
$$

_Note that_ the network has two hidden layers of 32 units with Tanh activation and a linear output. Consider also that holdings can be negative.

```python
def portfolio(model, features, S):
    """V includes V_0; h[:, n] is held over the interval n -> n+1."""
    h = model(features)
    V = wealth_from_holdings(h, S, model.capital)
    return V, h

def hedging_loss(model, features, S, H):
    V, _ = portfolio(model, features, S)
    return (V[:, -1] - H).square().mean()

model = HedgeNet(feature_dim, d, initial_capital)
assert "capital" not in dict(model.named_parameters())
```

_Then,_ the initial capital, features, simulated paths, and objective are the same as in the previous linear regression case.

_Note also_ that in this case the capital is not a parameter: the model stores it but never updates it. Otherwise, if capital were trainable, the model could lower the loss by increasing the starting wealth, breaking the setup.

We can then proceed with the <b>training on new simulations</b>: each epoch generates 256 new paths under the physical measure, computes their payoffs and signature features, and performs one <b>gradient update</b>.

Then, every 50 epochs the model is evaluated on the fixed validation set and, if the validation improves, the weights are stored. Only at the end, the best weights are loaded _(however, note that the loss is not convex $\implies$ no global optimum is guaranteed)_.


```python
def train_model(model, epochs=epochs, batch_size=paths_per_epoch, learning_rate=0.002):
    optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=learning_rate)
    history = {'train': [], 'validation': [], 'validation_epoch': []}
    best_loss, best_state = float('inf'), None
    for epoch in range(epochs):

        # One new Monte Carlo batch and one optimizer step per epoch.
        S = simulate_paths(batch_size, seed=10_000 + epoch, drift=mu)
        H = terminal_claim(S)
        F = scaled_features(S)

        model.train()
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss = hedging_loss(model, F, S, H)
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()
        history['train'].append(loss.item())

        if (epoch + 1) % 50 == 0 or epoch + 1 == epochs:
            model.eval()
            with torch.no_grad():
                val_loss = hedging_loss(model, F_val, S_val, H_val).item()
            history['validation'].append(val_loss)
            history['validation_epoch'].append(epoch + 1)
            if val_loss < best_loss:
                best_loss = val_loss

                # copy weights if loss is improved
                best_state = copy.deepcopy(model.state_dict())
            if (epoch + 1) % 250 == 0 or epoch + 1 == epochs:
                print(f'Epoch {epoch+1:4d} | fresh-batch MSE {loss.item():.6f} | validation {val_loss:.6f}')

    model.load_state_dict(best_state)
    model.eval()
    return history

capital_before_training = model.capital.clone()
history = train_model(model)
```
