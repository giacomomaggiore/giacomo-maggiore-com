## <a id = "0"><b>Basics of probability theory</b></a>

<b>Probability spaces</b>

A probability space is a mathematical concept that provides a formal model for a random experiment. It is composed of:
- <b>sample space</b> $\Omega$: the set of all possible outcomes.
- <b>event space</b> $\mathcal{F}$: the $\sigma$-algebra of subsets of $\Omega$. These subsets are called events, and they are the collections of outcomes to which we can assign probabilities.
- <b>probability measure</b> $P$: a function $P: \mathcal{F} \to [0,1]$ that assigns a probability to each event. Note that $P$ satisfies the following:
    - $P(A) \ge 0$ for all $A \in \mathcal{F}$.
    - $P(\Omega) = 1$.
    - For any countable sequence of events $(A_i)_{i \in \mathbb{N}} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$, $P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i)$.
<br/>
<b>Expectation, Variance and Covariance</b>

For a random variable $X$, the expectation $\mathbb{E}[X]$ is the average value over a large number of trials. <br/>If $X$ is discrete with probability mass function $p(x)$, we have:

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x p(x)x
$$

If $X$ is continuous with probability density function $f(x)$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f(x)\,dx
$$

For the same random variable $X$, the variance $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ measures the dispersion of $X$ values around the expected value, if it exists.

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2
$$

For two random variables $X, Y$, the covariance $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$ measures how they change together.

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]
$$

Note that variance and covariance have the following important properties:
- $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \operatorname{Cov}(X, X) \ge 0$.
- $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(Y, X)$.
- $\operatorname{Cov}(aX + bZ, Y) = a\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) + b\operatorname{Cov}(Z, Y)$ for constants $a, b$.
- $\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$ for constants $a, b$.
- If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, then $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$; the converse does not generally hold.

Furthermore, we can define the law $\mu$ of a random variable $X$ as the probability measure that satisfies $\mu(B) = P[X \in B]$, also called the pushforward measure.

Lastly, a fundamental concept in probability theory is independence: a family of random variables is called independent if the joint law of the family is the product of the marginal laws. In formulas:

$$
f(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i)
$$

<b>The Law of Large Numbers (LLN)</b>

The Law of Large Numbers states that, as the number of trials tends to infinity, the sample mean converges to the expected true value of the underlying distribution. Formally, we write:

$$
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} \mathbb{E}[X_1]
$$

<b>Central Limit Theorem</b>

It states that the distribution of sample means of a large number of independent and identically distributed random variables will be approximately normal, regardless of the original distribution of the random variables.

---

<b>Conditional Probabilities and Laws</b>

The conditional probability of $A$ given that event $B$ has occurred is:

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \quad P(B) > 0
$$

For random variables, we can define <b>conditional probability mass functions (PMFs)</b> _(for discrete variables)_ as:

$$
P(X = x \mid Y = y) = \frac{P(X = x, Y = y)}{P(Y = y)}
$$

and <b>probability density functions (PDFs)</b> for continuous variables as:

$$
f(x \mid y) = \frac{f(x,y)}{f(y)}
$$

where $f(x,y)$ is the joint PDF. If $x$ and $y$ are independent, $f(x \mid y) = f(x)$.

<b>Bayes' Formula</b>

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}
$$

- $P(A \mid B)$ is the <b>posterior probability</b>.
- $P(B \mid A)$ is the <b>likelihood</b>, i.e. the probability of observing $B$ given that hypothesis $A$ is true.
- $P(A)$ is the <b>prior probability</b>, i.e. the initial hypothesis of $A$ before any evidence.
- $P(B)$ is the <b>evidence (marginal likelihood)</b>, i.e. the total probability of observing $B$.
    - $P(B) = \sum P(B \mid A_i)P(A_i)$ over all possible hypotheses $A_i$.

<br/>
<b>Acceptance-Rejection Algorithm as Bayesian Reasoning</b>

The Acceptance-Rejection (A-R) algorithm is a method for generating random samples from a target probability distribution $R(dx) = f(x)dx$ using samples from a proposal distribution $Q(dx) = g(x)dx$, _especially when $R$ is difficult to sample directly_.<br/>
This method can be viewed through a Bayesian lens, where the <b>decision to accept or reject</b> is observed data that updates our belief about whether the sample comes from the target distribution.

We then consider:
- <b>prior-proposal</b>: the samples drawn from the proposal distribution $Q$ as realizations of $Y$ _(we are proposing that $Y$ might also be a sample from $Q$ after accepting/rejecting)_
- <b>likelihood/acceptance criterion</b>: generate an independent uniform random variable $U \sim U(0,1)$, and accept if:

$$
U \le \frac{f(Y)}{cg(Y)}
$$

where $c$ is a constant such that $f(y) \le cg(y)$. Note that the acceptance criterion is our data that helps us decide whether we accept samples from $Y$ as samples from law $R$.

- <b>posterior</b>: if a sample $Y$ is accepted, it means that we have updated our belief such that this $Y$ is a valid sample from $R$. Iterating the process ensures that we accept only samples distributed according to $R$.

Then, assume random variables $Y, U$ live on a probability space with measure $P$.<br/>
We first calculate the relevant quantities:
- $B$ is the set of accepted sample events, i.e. $B = \left\{U \le \frac{f(Y)}{cg(Y)}\right\}$, with
    - $P(B \mid Y = y) = \frac{f(y)}{cg(y)}$
    - $P(B) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} P(B \mid Y = z)g(z)dz = 1 / c$
- this allows us to calculate the law of posterior $Y$ under acceptance, that is:

$$
f(y)dy = \frac{\frac{f(y)}{cg(y)}g(y)dy}{\frac{1}{c}}
$$

That means that, conditional on $B$, the random variable $Y$, _unconditionally $Q$-distributed_, is actually $R$-distributed.

<b>Monte Carlo Integration (Corollary of LLN and CLT)</b>

Monte Carlo integration is a numerical method for approximating the value of a definite integral, _especially in the multidimensional case or over complex domains_, by using the Law of Large Numbers.

We first consider the integral $\int_a^b f(x)dx$, which can be rewritten as:

$$
I = (b - a)\mathbb{E}[f(X)]
$$

where $X \sim U[a,b]$ (uniformly distributed on $[a,b]$). Thanks to the LLN, we can approximate $\mathbb{E}[f(X)]$ by averaging many samples of $f(X_i)$, so the integral becomes:

$$
\int_a^b f(x)dx \approx \frac{b-a}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N f(X_i)
$$

In this case, the CLT states that the error scales as $1/\sqrt{N}$.

<b>Pseudo-Random Number Generators (PRNGs) vs True Random Number Generators (TRNGs)</b>

Whenever we use Monte Carlo integration, we need a sequence of samples of random variables. To generate them, we can consider:

- <b>Pseudo-Random Number Generators (PRNGs)</b>:
    - deterministic algorithms that produce sequences of apparently random numbers but are determined by an initial value called a _"seed"_ _(given the same seed, a PRNG gives the same sequence)_
    - they are fast, efficient and, _most importantly_, reproducible
    - however, their deterministic nature makes them unsuitable for applications that require true randomness _(e.g. cryptography)_

- <b>True Random Number Generators (TRNGs)</b>
    - they extract randomness from physical phenomena _(e.g. atmospheric noise, thermal noise in resistors, etc.)_
    - they are slower and often require specialized hardware

Formally, we can say:

Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{A}, P)$ be a probability space and let $U_n : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$, for $n \in \mathbb{N}$, be a sequence of $P$-independent $\mathcal{U}_{[0,1]}$-distributed random variables.
<br/>$\mathcal{U}_{(0,1)}$-pseudo-random numbers are sequences of real numbers calculated by a deterministic algorithm that have similar statistical properties to $(U_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$.

Even if this is a very generic definition, the importance and advantage of $\mathcal{U}_{(0,1)}$-pseudo-random numbers are that they can be reproduced many times _(with the same output)_ with the same pseudo-random input.

We first show three important examples of stochastic differential equations, largely discussed in the sector and for which numerical approximations will be useful.

<b>The Black–Scholes Model</b>

Before starting: quick history recap: The <a href="" target="_blank">Black–Scholes Model</a> was initially published in 1973 by the economists Black and Scholes, based on a previous mathematical formulation by Merton. The model revolutionized modern finance by providing the first framework for pricing options. Curiously, the publication date (1973) coincided with the opening of the <a href="" target="_blank">Chicago Board Options Exchange (CBOE, which is still active and covers approximately 30% of total option trading volume per year)</a>, which provided the first centralized marketplace for trading options.

From the model, we can deduce the Black–Scholes formula, which returns the theoretical value of the price of a European option (recall that a European option can be exercised only at maturity, while an American option can be exercised anytime).
<br/>The model assumes that the price of the underlying asset follows a <b>geometric Brownian motion</b> (GBM, one of the most important stochastic models in financial economics) and [... place other assumptions]

We begin by drawing the trajectory of a Brownian motion in Python.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Parameters for Brownian Motion
T = 1.0  # Total time
N = 1000 # Number of steps
dt = T / N # Time step size

# Generate a Brownian Motion trajectory
np.random.seed(42) # for reproducibility

# Generate N independent increments
dW = np.random.normal(loc=0.0, scale=np.sqrt(dt), size=N)
W = np.cumsum(dW)

t = np.linspace(0, T, N)
```

In the code: we first generate $N = 1000$ independent increments with $0$ mean and $\sqrt{dt}$ standard deviation. Secondly, we build the Brownian motion by cumulative sums.

![Bias variance tradeoff](/images/notes/numerical-sol-sde/standard-bm.png)

Formally, we can write:

$$
dS_t = \mu S_t \, dt + \sigma S_t \, dW_t
$$

where
- $S_t$ is the stock price at time $t$.
- $\mu$ is the drift coefficient (the expected log return).
- $\sigma$ is the volatility (standard deviation of log returns).
- $W_t$ is a Wiener process (Brownian motion).

Recall that $W_t$ is a stochastic process $(W_t)_{t \ge 0}$ that satisfies:
- $W_0 = 0$ almost surely.
- It has independent increments for $0 \le s < t$: $W_t - W_s \sim N(0, t-s)$.
- Its paths are almost surely continuous.

Therefore we have:

$$
W_t \sim N(0, t), \quad \mathbb{E}[W_t] = 0, \quad \operatorname{Var}[W_t] = t.
$$

The equation should be understood as a (stochastic) integral equation.

_Intuition: the equation says that the infinitesimal increment $S_{t+\Delta t} - S_t$ at time $t$ is approximately normally distributed with variance $\sigma^2 S_t^2 \Delta t$ and mean $\mu S_t \Delta t$._

In Python, we can write:
```python

# Parameters for Black-Scholes simulation
S0 = 100   # Initial stock price
mu = 0.05  # Drift (expected return)
sigma = 0.20 # Volatility
T = 1.0    # Total time
N = 1000    # Number of steps
dt = T / N # Time step size

# Generate a single trajectory of stock prices
np.random.seed(42) # for reproducibility

S = np.zeros(N)
S[0] = S0

for i in range(1, N):
    dW = np.random.normal(loc=0.0, scale=np.sqrt(dt))
    S[i] = S[i-1] * np.exp((mu - 0.5 * sigma**2) * dt + sigma * dW)

t = np.linspace(0, T, N)

```

<b>Langevin Equation (Simplified SDE)</b>

The Langevin equation was inspired by the dynamics of a particle in a potential, where the gradient of the potential determines the force acting on the particle. It is the theoretical foundation of highly influential algorithms like Markov chain Monte Carlo or stochastic gradient descent. The goal of the equation is to sample from $\exp(-U/\epsilon)$.

Recall, a potential function $U(x)$ is a scalar function that defines a force/drift $b(x)$ through its negative gradient:

$$
b(x) = -\nabla U(x)
$$

where $b(x)$ is the drift function, i.e. the deterministic instantaneous tendency of the process in its state $x$.

In general, for a $U(x)$ in one dimension, the stochastic differential equation is given by:

$$
dX_t = -\frac{1}{2} \nabla U(X_t) \, dt + \sqrt{\epsilon} \, dW_t
$$

where
- $X_t$ is the position of the particle at time $t$.
- $U(X_t)$ is the potential energy function.
- $\nabla U(X_t)$ is the derivative of the potential with respect to $X_t$.
- $\epsilon$ is a parameter related to noise.
- $W_t$ is Brownian motion.

Note that the process converges in distribution to an invariant measure given by the density with respect to Lebesgue measure, i.e. solving the differential equation for large times brings samples of the invariant measure:

$$
p(x) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{U(x)}{\epsilon}\right)
$$

Recall that the invariant measure is a probability distribution that remains unchanged under the evolution of a stochastic process. <b>Formally</b>, if $X_0 \sim \pi$, then $X_t \sim \pi$ for all $t \ge 0$ and, intuitively, it is the process's stationary theoretical distribution _(long-run distribution)_.

In Python, we write the following:

```python

# Parameters for Langevin SDE
dt = 0.01  # Time step
T = 500    # Total simulation time
N = int(T / dt) # Number of steps

epsilon = 1.0 # Noise intensity parameter

# Define a potential function (e.g., a double well potential)
def potential(x):
    return 0.5 * (x**2 - 1)**2

def grad_potential(x):
    return 2 * x * (x**2 - 1) # dU/dx

# Initialize position
x = np.zeros(N)
x[0] = 0.0

# Simulate Langevin dynamics using Euler-Maruyama
for i in range(N - 1):
    dW = np.random.normal(loc=0.0, scale=np.sqrt(dt))
    x[i+1] = x[i] - 0.5 * grad_potential(x[i]) * dt + np.sqrt(epsilon) * dW


```

<b>The Kalman Filter (Continuous Time for OU Process)</b>

The <b>Kalman filter</b> provides an optimal estimate of the state of a linear system observed with additive Gaussian noise. <br/>_Intuition_: the filter repeats two steps:
1. use the dynamics to predict the following state
2. compare the prediction with the new observation and combine them, giving more weight to whichever is more reliable.

We now illustrate the filter with an <b>Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process</b>, which describes a mean-reverting particle with white noise, with the following SDE:

$$
dX_t = \theta(\mu_{\mathrm{ou}} - X_t)\,dt + \sigma_{\mathrm{ou}}\,dW_t
$$

where $\mu_{\mathrm{ou}}$ is the <b>mean of the process</b> and $\theta$ is the <b>reversion speed</b>

We now consider a <b>noisy observation </b>of this OU process.<br/>In this case, we observe $(Z_k)_{k \ge 0}$ such that $Z_k = H X_{t_k} + \varepsilon_k$, where $(\varepsilon_k)_{k \ge 0}$ are independent random variables with $\varepsilon_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R_{\mathrm{measurement}})$ representing the measurement noise.

_Intuition:_ in an OU process, $X_t$ is hidden and we only see the noisy signal, with $X_t$ as the unobserved OU state, $Z_k$ as the observation, and $\varepsilon_k$ as the noise.

Over a short interval $\Delta t$, we can say:

$$
Z_{k+1} = H X_{t_{k+1}} + \sqrt{R_{\mathrm{measurement}}}\,\varepsilon_{k+1}
$$

where $\varepsilon_{k+1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ represents the random measurement noise in the discretized observation.

```python

dt_sim = 0.01  # Simulation time step
T_sim = 100    # Total simulation time
N_sim = int(T_sim / dt_sim)

theta = 0.1    # Reversion speed
mu_ou = 0.5    # Mean
sigma_ou = 0.2 # Volatility

np.random.seed(43) # for reproducibility

X_true = np.zeros(N_sim)
X_true[0] = 0.0 #initialize at zero

for i in range(N_sim - 1):

    # wiener process
    dW = np.random.normal(loc=0.0, scale=np.sqrt(dt_sim))
    # sde
    X_true[i+1] = X_true[i] + theta * (mu_ou - X_true[i]) * dt_sim + sigma_ou * dW

t_sim = np.linspace(0, T_sim, N_sim)

# we now generate noisy observations

obs_noise_std = 0.5 # std of observation noise
R_obs = obs_noise_std**2 # variance of noise variance

# we sum up the simulated x_t and the noise
Z_obs = X_true + np.random.normal(0, obs_noise_std, N_sim)

```

We now spend some time explaining the Kalman filter. <br/>
Given $dX_t = \theta(\mu_{\mathrm{ou}} - X_t)\,dt + \sigma_{\mathrm{ou}}\,dW_t$ and $Z_k = H X_{t_k} + \varepsilon_k$, we can introduce the Kalman filter with the following parameters:
- $F = -\theta$: mean-reversion coefficient
- $L = \sigma_{\mathrm{ou}}$: intensity of the process noise
- $Q_{\mathrm{process}} = 1$: variance of the Brownian noise $dW_t$
- $H = 1$: observation coefficient
- $R_{\mathrm{measurement}}$: variance of the observation noise
Formally, we write:

$$
P^-_{k+1} \approx (1 + F\Delta t)^2 P_k + L^2 Q_{\mathrm{process}}\Delta t
$$

that, for this scalar OU model, translates to

$$
P^-_{k+1} \approx (1 - \theta\Delta t)^2 P_k + \sigma_{\mathrm{ou}}^2\Delta t
$$

Then, using Euler's method, we can approximate:

$$
P^-_{k+1} \approx (1 - \theta\Delta t)^2 P_k + \sigma_{\mathrm{ou}}^2\Delta t
$$

where $P^-_{k+1}$ is the uncertainty before using the following observation.

Then, we can still use the Euler approximation with $F = -\theta$ for the following expression:

$$
\hat X^-_{k+1} = \hat X_k + \theta(\mu_{\mathrm{ou}} - \hat X_k)\Delta t
$$

The Kalman gain determines how much we can trust the new observation _(large gain: we can trust it more)_ and is defined as:

$$
K_{k+1} = \frac{P^-_{k+1} H}{H^2 P^-_{k+1} + R_{\mathrm{measurement}}}
$$

Lastly, we update the state with:

$$
\hat X_{k+1} = \hat X^-_{k+1} + K_{k+1}(Z_{k+1} - H \hat X^-_{k+1})
$$

(the term in parentheses represents the <b>innovation</b> or prediction error, _i.e. the difference between the observation and the value predicted by the model)_

Starting from the previous code block, we can implement the Kalman filter as:

```python
# we now apply the Kalman Filter

# System parameters for Kalman Filter
F = -theta
L = sigma_ou
Q_process = 1.0     # Covariance of dW (in the case of unit variance)

H = 1.0
R_measurement = R_obs

dt_filter = dt_sim # Use the same time step for filter discretization

# initial conditions for Kalman filter
x_hat = np.zeros(N_sim)
P = np.zeros(N_sim)

x_hat[0] = Z_obs[0] # Initialize estimate with first observation
P[0] = 1.0           # Initial covariance (high uncertainty)

for k in range(N_sim - 1):

    # Prediction step (using discretized Riccati equation for P)
    P_dot = F * P[k] + P[k] * F + L * Q_process * L - P[k] * H * (1/R_measurement) * H * P[k]
    P_minus = P[k] + P_dot * dt_filter

    x_hat_minus = x_hat[k] + F * x_hat[k] * dt_filter

    # Kalman Gain (discrete-time equivalent for this observation model)
    K_k = P_minus * H / (H * P_minus * H + R_measurement / dt_filter) # Adjust R for discrete observation

    # Update step
    x_hat[k+1] = x_hat_minus + K_k * (Z_obs[k+1] - H * x_hat_minus)
    P[k+1] = (1 - K_k * H) * P_minus
```

## <a id="2"><b>The Inversion Method</b></a>

<b>Method for General Distributions</b>

<b>Inversion Method</b>

We now spend some time presenting the inversion method, useful for transforming _uniformly distributed random numbers_ into arbitrary distributions using a suitable generalized inverse distribution function.

<b>Definition (Generalized inverse distribution function associated to a distribution function)</b>

Let $F: \mathbb{R} \to [0,1]$ be a distribution function. We define the generalized inverse distribution function associated with $F$, denoted as $I_F: (0,1) \to \mathbb{R}$, as the function which satisfies, for all $y \in (0,1)$, the following:

$$
I_F(y) = \inf \{x \in \mathbb{R} : F(x) \ge y\} = \inf(F^{-1}([y,1]))
$$

The <b>crucial switching formula</b> is:

$$
I_F(u) \le x \iff u \le F(x)
$$

From the definition, we can draw some nice properties:
- For all $y \in (0,1)$, the set $\{x \in \mathbb{R}: F(x) \ge y\}$ is not empty and bounded from below.
    - _(This comes from the fact that, given $\lim_{x \to \infty} F(x) = 1$, for all $y$ the set is not empty.)_
    - The fact that $\lim_{x \to -\infty} F(x) = 0$ ensures that the set is bounded from below.
- $I_F$ is also sometimes referred to as the <b>quantile function associated with $F$</b>.
- Consider a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ and $X: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$, an $\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$-measurable function _(imagine $X$ as a model for the change in value of a financial portfolio)._<br/>
Consider $F$, the distribution of $-X$, and let $\alpha \in (0,1)$ be a confidence level, typically a number close to 1 (95%, 99.999%): Then the real number
$$
I_F(\alpha) = \min \{x \in \mathbb{R}: F(x) \ge \alpha\} = \min \{x \in \mathbb{R}: \mathbb{P}(-X \le x) \ge \alpha\} = \\
\min \{x \in \mathbb{R}: \mathbb{P}(-X > x) \le 1 - \alpha\}
$$

is called the <b>Value at Risk with confidence level $\alpha$ associated with $-X$</b> _(i.e. it holds with probability of at least $\alpha$ that the loss $-X$ does not exceed $I_F(\alpha)$)._

<b>Theorem - The Inversion Method</b>

Let $U \sim U(0,1)$ and assume $U \in (0,1)$ almost surely. Then $X = I_F(U)$ has distribution function $F$.

<b>Proof</b>

For every $x \in \mathbb{R}$, the switching formula gives:

$$
P(X \le x) = P(I_F(U) \le x) = P(U \le F(x)) = F(x)
$$

because $U$ is uniform on $(0,1)$. _Intuition: to sample from an arbitrary one-dimensional distribution, we can simply sample one uniform random variable and apply the quantile function to the sample._

---
<br/>
<b>Lemma (Properties of generalized inverse distribution functions)</b>

Let $F: \mathbb{R} \to [0,1]$ be a distribution function and $I_F : (0,1) \to \mathbb{R}$ the generalized inverse distribution function associated with $F$. Then $I_F$ fulfils the following:
1. $I_F$ is non-decreasing.
2. For all $y \in (0,1)$:
    - $F(I_F(y)) \ge y$.
    - $F^{-1}([y,1]) = [I_F(y), \infty)$.
    - $I_F(y) = \min(F^{-1}([y,1]))$.
3. For all $x \in F^{-1}((0,1)) = \{z \in \mathbb{R} : F(z) \in (0,1)\}$: $I_F(F(x)) \le x$.
4. For all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, $y \in (0,1)$: $I_F(y) < x$ if and only if $y \le F(x)$ _(switching formula)_.
5. For all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, $y \in (0,1)$: $I_F(y) > x$ if and only if $y > F(x)$ _(switching formula)_.
6. For all open sets $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ with the property that $F|_D : D \to [0,1]$ is <b>injective</b> and all $y \in F(D)$, we have that $(F|_D)^{-1}(y) = I_F(y)$.

_(Recall: a function $f$ is injective if $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$ implies $x_1 = x_2$; equivalently, distinct inputs have distinct outputs.)_

Note that distribution functions are càdlàg (right-continuous with left limits), while generalized inverse distribution functions are, in contrast, <b>càglàd</b> (left-continuous with right limits).

<br/>
<b>Definition (Generalized Inverse Distribution Function for the Bernoulli Distribution)</b>

Let $p \in [0,1]$ be a real number and let $F: \mathbb{R} \to [0,1]$ be the distribution function of $\operatorname{Ber}(p)$. Assume also that, for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, it holds:

$$
F(x) = \operatorname{Ber}_p((-\infty, x])
= (1-p)\cdot \delta_0^{\mathbb{R}}((-\infty, x])
+ p\cdot \delta_1^{\mathbb{R}}((-\infty, x])
=
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } x < 0,\\
1-p & \text{if } 0 \le x < 1,\\
1 & \text{if } x \ge 1.
\end{cases}
$$

Then the generalized inverse distribution function $I_F$ is defined as:

$$
I_F(y) = \inf\{x \in \mathbb{R} : F(x) \ge y\}
=
\begin{cases}
0, & 0 < y \le 1-p,\\
1, & 1-p < y \le 1.
\end{cases}
$$
