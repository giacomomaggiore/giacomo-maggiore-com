## <a id = "0"><b>Basics of probability theory</b></a>

<b>Probability spaces</b>

A probability space is a mathematical concept that provides a formal model for a random experiment. It is composed of:
- <b>sample space</b> $\Omega$: the set of all possible outcomes.
- <b>event space</b> $\mathcal{F}$: the $\sigma$-algebra of subsets of $\Omega$. These subsets are called events, and they are the collections of outcomes to which we can assign probabilities.
- <b>probability measure</b> $P$: a function $P: \mathcal{F} \to [0,1]$ that assigns a probability to each event. Note that $P$ satisfies the following:
    - $P(A) \ge 0$ for all $A \in \mathcal{F}$.
    - $P(\Omega) = 1$.
    - For any countable sequence of events $(A_i)_{i \in \mathbb{N}} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$, $P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i)$.
<br/>
<b>Expectation, Variance and Covariance</b>

For a random variable $X$, the expectation $\mathbb{E}[X]$ is the average value over a large number of trials. <br/>If $X$ is discrete with probability mass function $p(x)$, we have:

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x p(x)x
$$

If $X$ is continuous with probability density function $f(x)$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f(x)\,dx
$$

For the same random variable $X$, the variance $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ measures the dispersion of $X$ values around the expected value, if it exists.

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2
$$

For two random variables $X, Y$, the covariance $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$ measures how they change together.

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]
$$

Note that variance and covariance have the following important properties:
- $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \operatorname{Cov}(X, X) \ge 0$.
- $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(Y, X)$.
- $\operatorname{Cov}(aX + bZ, Y) = a\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) + b\operatorname{Cov}(Z, Y)$ for constants $a, b$.
- $\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$ for constants $a, b$.
- If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, then $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$; the converse does not generally hold.

Furthermore, we can define the law $\mu$ of a random variable $X$ as the probability measure that satisfies $\mu(B) = P[X \in B]$, also called the pushforward measure.

Lastly, a fundamental concept in probability theory is independence: a family of random variables is called independent if the joint law of the family is the product of the marginal laws. In formulas:

$$
f(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i)
$$

<b>The Law of Large Numbers (LLN)</b>

The Law of Large Numbers states that, as the number of trials tends to infinity, the sample mean converges to the expected true value of the underlying distribution. Formally, we write:

$$
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} \mathbb{E}[X_1]
$$

<b>Central Limit Theorem</b>

It states that the distribution of sample means of a large number of independent and identically distributed random variables will be approximately normal, regardless of the original distribution of the random variables.

---

<b>Conditional Probabilities and Laws</b>

The conditional probability of $A$ given that event $B$ has occurred is:

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \quad P(B) > 0
$$

For random variables, we can define <b>conditional probability mass functions (PMFs)</b> _(for discrete variables)_ as:

$$
P(X = x \mid Y = y) = \frac{P(X = x, Y = y)}{P(Y = y)}
$$

and <b>probability density functions (PDFs)</b> for continuous variables as:

$$
f(x \mid y) = \frac{f(x,y)}{f(y)}
$$

where $f(x,y)$ is the joint PDF. If $x$ and $y$ are independent, $f(x \mid y) = f(x)$.

<b>Bayes' Formula</b>

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}
$$

- $P(A \mid B)$ is the <b>posterior probability</b>.
- $P(B \mid A)$ is the <b>likelihood</b>, i.e. the probability of observing $B$ given that hypothesis $A$ is true.
- $P(A)$ is the <b>prior probability</b>, i.e. the initial hypothesis of $A$ before any evidence.
- $P(B)$ is the <b>evidence (marginal likelihood)</b>, i.e. the total probability of observing $B$.
    - $P(B) = \sum P(B \mid A_i)P(A_i)$ over all possible hypotheses $A_i$.

<br/>
<b>Acceptance-Rejection Algorithm as Bayesian Reasoning</b>

The Acceptance-Rejection (A-R) algorithm is a method for generating random samples from a target probability distribution $R(dx) = f(x)dx$ using samples from a proposal distribution $Q(dx) = g(x)dx$, _especially when $R$ is difficult to sample directly_.<br/>
This method can be viewed through a Bayesian lens, where the <b>decision to accept or reject</b> is observed data that updates our belief about whether the sample comes from the target distribution.

We then consider:
- <b>prior-proposal</b>: the samples drawn from the proposal distribution $Q$ as realizations of $Y$ _(we are proposing that $Y$ might also be a sample from $Q$ after accepting/rejecting)_
- <b>likelihood/acceptance criterion</b>: generate an independent uniform random variable $U \sim U(0,1)$, and accept if:

$$
U \le \frac{f(Y)}{cg(Y)}
$$

where $c$ is a constant such that $f(y) \le cg(y)$. Note that the acceptance criterion is our data that helps us decide whether we accept samples from $Y$ as samples from law $R$.

- <b>posterior</b>: if a sample $Y$ is accepted, it means that we have updated our belief such that this $Y$ is a valid sample from $R$. Iterating the process ensures that we accept only samples distributed according to $R$.

Then, assume random variables $Y, U$ live on a probability space with measure $P$.<br/>
We first calculate the relevant quantities:
- $B$ is the set of accepted sample events, i.e. $B = \left\{U \le \frac{f(Y)}{cg(Y)}\right\}$, with
    - $P(B \mid Y = y) = \frac{f(y)}{cg(y)}$
    - $P(B) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} P(B \mid Y = z)g(z)dz = 1 / c$
- this allows us to calculate the law of posterior $Y$ under acceptance, that is:

$$
f(y)dy = \frac{\frac{f(y)}{cg(y)}g(y)dy}{\frac{1}{c}}
$$

That means that, conditional on $B$, the random variable $Y$, _unconditionally $Q$-distributed_, is actually $R$-distributed.

<b>Monte Carlo Integration (Corollary of LLN and CLT)</b>

Monte Carlo integration is a numerical method for approximating the value of a definite integral, _especially in the multidimensional case or over complex domains_, by using the Law of Large Numbers.

We first consider the integral $\int_a^b f(x)dx$, which can be rewritten as:

$$
I = (b - a)\mathbb{E}[f(X)]
$$

where $X \sim U[a,b]$ (uniformly distributed on $[a,b]$). Thanks to the LLN, we can approximate $\mathbb{E}[f(X)]$ by averaging many samples of $f(X_i)$, so the integral becomes:

$$
\int_a^b f(x)dx \approx \frac{b-a}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N f(X_i)
$$

In this case, the CLT states that the error scales as $1/\sqrt{N}$.

<b>Pseudo-Random Number Generators (PRNGs) vs True Random Number Generators (TRNGs)</b>

Whenever we use Monte Carlo integration, we need a sequence of samples of random variables. To generate them, we can consider:

- <b>Pseudo-Random Number Generators (PRNGs)</b>:
    - deterministic algorithms that produce sequences of apparently random numbers but are determined by an initial value called a _"seed"_ _(given the same seed, a PRNG gives the same sequence)_
    - they are fast, efficient and, _most importantly_, reproducible
    - however, their deterministic nature makes them unsuitable for applications that require true randomness _(e.g. cryptography)_

- <b>True Random Number Generators (TRNGs)</b>
    - they extract randomness from physical phenomena _(e.g. atmospheric noise, thermal noise in resistors, etc.)_
    - they are slower and often require specialized hardware

Formally, we can say:

Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{A}, P)$ be a probability space and let $U_n : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$, for $n \in \mathbb{N}$, be a sequence of $P$-independent $\mathcal{U}_{[0,1]}$-distributed random variables.
<br/>$\mathcal{U}_{(0,1)}$-pseudo-random numbers are sequences of real numbers calculated by a deterministic algorithm that have similar statistical properties to $(U_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$.

Even if this is a very generic definition, the importance and advantage of $\mathcal{U}_{(0,1)}$-pseudo-random numbers are that they can be reproduced many times _(with the same output)_ with the same pseudo-random input.

We first show three important examples of stochastic differential equations, largely discussed in the sector and for which numerical approximations will be useful.

<b>The Black–Scholes Model</b>

Before starting: quick history recap: The <a href="" target="_blank">Black–Scholes Model</a> was initially published in 1973 by the economists Black and Scholes, based on a previous mathematical formulation by Merton. The model revolutionized modern finance by providing the first framework for pricing options. Curiously, the publication date (1973) coincided with the opening of the <a href="" target="_blank">Chicago Board Options Exchange (CBOE, which is still active and covers approximately 30% of total option trading volume per year)</a>, which provided the first centralized marketplace for trading options.

From the model, we can deduce the Black–Scholes formula, which returns the theoretical value of the price of a European option (recall that a European option can be exercised only at maturity, while an American option can be exercised anytime).
<br/>The model assumes that the price of the underlying asset follows a <b>geometric Brownian motion</b> (GBM, one of the most important stochastic models in financial economics) and [... place other assumptions]

We begin by drawing the trajectory of a Brownian motion in Python.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Parameters for Brownian Motion
T = 1.0  # Total time
N = 1000 # Number of steps
dt = T / N # Time step size

# Generate a Brownian Motion trajectory
np.random.seed(42) # for reproducibility

# Generate N independent increments
dW = np.random.normal(loc=0.0, scale=np.sqrt(dt), size=N)
W = np.cumsum(dW)

t = np.linspace(0, T, N)
```

In the code: we first generate $N = 1000$ independent increments with $0$ mean and $\sqrt{dt}$ standard deviation. Secondly, we build the Brownian motion by cumulative sums.

![Bias variance tradeoff](/images/notes/numerical-sol-sde/standard-bm.png)

Formally, we can write:

$$
dS_t = \mu S_t \, dt + \sigma S_t \, dW_t
$$

where
- $S_t$ is the stock price at time $t$.
- $\mu$ is the drift coefficient (the expected log return).
- $\sigma$ is the volatility (standard deviation of log returns).
- $W_t$ is a Wiener process (Brownian motion).

Recall that $W_t$ is a stochastic process $(W_t)_{t \ge 0}$ that satisfies:
- $W_0 = 0$ almost surely.
- It has independent increments for $0 \le s < t$: $W_t - W_s \sim N(0, t-s)$.
- Its paths are almost surely continuous.

Therefore we have:

$$
W_t \sim N(0, t), \quad \mathbb{E}[W_t] = 0, \quad \operatorname{Var}[W_t] = t.
$$

The equation should be understood as a (stochastic) integral equation.

_Intuition: the equation says that the infinitesimal increment $S_{t+\Delta t} - S_t$ at time $t$ is approximately normally distributed with variance $\sigma^2 S_t^2 \Delta t$ and mean $\mu S_t \Delta t$._

In Python, we can write:
```python

# Parameters for Black-Scholes simulation
S0 = 100   # Initial stock price
mu = 0.05  # Drift (expected return)
sigma = 0.20 # Volatility
T = 1.0    # Total time
N = 1000    # Number of steps
dt = T / N # Time step size

# Generate a single trajectory of stock prices
np.random.seed(42) # for reproducibility

S = np.zeros(N)
S[0] = S0

for i in range(1, N):
    dW = np.random.normal(loc=0.0, scale=np.sqrt(dt))
    S[i] = S[i-1] * np.exp((mu - 0.5 * sigma**2) * dt + sigma * dW)

t = np.linspace(0, T, N)

```

<b>Langevin Equation (Simplified SDE)</b>

The Langevin equation was inspired by the dynamics of a particle in a potential, where the gradient of the potential determines the force acting on the particle. It is the theoretical foundation of highly influential algorithms like Markov chain Monte Carlo or stochastic gradient descent. The goal of the equation is to sample from $\exp(-U/\epsilon)$.

Recall, a potential function $U(x)$ is a scalar function that defines a force/drift $b(x)$ through its negative gradient:

$$
b(x) = -\nabla U(x)
$$

where $b(x)$ is the drift function, i.e. the deterministic instantaneous tendency of the process in its state $x$.

In general, for a $U(x)$ in one dimension, the stochastic differential equation is given by:

$$
dX_t = -\frac{1}{2} \nabla U(X_t) \, dt + \sqrt{\epsilon} \, dW_t
$$

where
- $X_t$ is the position of the particle at time $t$.
- $U(X_t)$ is the potential energy function.
- $\nabla U(X_t)$ is the derivative of the potential with respect to $X_t$.
- $\epsilon$ is a parameter related to noise.
- $W_t$ is Brownian motion.

Note that the process converges in distribution to an invariant measure given by the density with respect to Lebesgue measure, i.e. solving the differential equation for large times brings samples of the invariant measure:

$$
p(x) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{U(x)}{\epsilon}\right)
$$

Recall that the invariant measure is a probability distribution that remains unchanged under the evolution of a stochastic process. <b>Formally</b>, if $X_0 \sim \pi$, then $X_t \sim \pi$ for all $t \ge 0$ and, intuitively, it is the process's stationary theoretical distribution _(long-run distribution)_.

In Python, we write the following:

```python

# Parameters for Langevin SDE
dt = 0.01  # Time step
T = 500    # Total simulation time
N = int(T / dt) # Number of steps

epsilon = 1.0 # Noise intensity parameter

# Define a potential function (e.g., a double well potential)
def potential(x):
    return 0.5 * (x**2 - 1)**2

def grad_potential(x):
    return 2 * x * (x**2 - 1) # dU/dx

# Initialize position
x = np.zeros(N)
x[0] = 0.0

# Simulate Langevin dynamics using Euler-Maruyama
for i in range(N - 1):
    dW = np.random.normal(loc=0.0, scale=np.sqrt(dt))
    x[i+1] = x[i] - 0.5 * grad_potential(x[i]) * dt + np.sqrt(epsilon) * dW


```

<b>The Kalman Filter (Continuous Time for OU Process)</b>

The <b>Kalman filter</b> provides an optimal estimate of the state of a linear system observed with additive Gaussian noise. <br/>_Intuition_: the filter repeats two steps:
1. use the dynamics to predict the following state
2. compare the prediction with the new observation and combine them, giving more weight to whichever is more reliable.

We now illustrate the filter with an <b>Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process</b>, which describes a mean-reverting particle with white noise, with the following SDE:

$$
dX_t = \theta(\mu_{\mathrm{ou}} - X_t)\,dt + \sigma_{\mathrm{ou}}\,dW_t
$$

where $\mu_{\mathrm{ou}}$ is the <b>mean of the process</b> and $\theta$ is the <b>reversion speed</b>

We now consider a <b>noisy observation </b>of this OU process.<br/>In this case, we observe $(Z_k)_{k \ge 0}$ such that $Z_k = H X_{t_k} + \varepsilon_k$, where $(\varepsilon_k)_{k \ge 0}$ are independent random variables with $\varepsilon_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R_{\mathrm{measurement}})$ representing the measurement noise.

_Intuition:_ in an OU process, $X_t$ is hidden and we only see the noisy signal, with $X_t$ as the unobserved OU state, $Z_k$ as the observation, and $\varepsilon_k$ as the noise.

Over a short interval $\Delta t$, we can say:

$$
Z_{k+1} = H X_{t_{k+1}} + \sqrt{R_{\mathrm{measurement}}}\,\varepsilon_{k+1}
$$

where $\varepsilon_{k+1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ represents the random measurement noise in the discretized observation.

```python

dt_sim = 0.01  # Simulation time step
T_sim = 100    # Total simulation time
N_sim = int(T_sim / dt_sim)

theta = 0.1    # Reversion speed
mu_ou = 0.5    # Mean
sigma_ou = 0.2 # Volatility

np.random.seed(43) # for reproducibility

X_true = np.zeros(N_sim)
X_true[0] = 0.0 #initialize at zero

for i in range(N_sim - 1):

    # wiener process
    dW = np.random.normal(loc=0.0, scale=np.sqrt(dt_sim))
    # sde
    X_true[i+1] = X_true[i] + theta * (mu_ou - X_true[i]) * dt_sim + sigma_ou * dW

t_sim = np.linspace(0, T_sim, N_sim)

# we now generate noisy observations

obs_noise_std = 0.5 # std of observation noise
R_obs = obs_noise_std**2 # variance of noise variance

# we sum up the simulated x_t and the noise
Z_obs = X_true + np.random.normal(0, obs_noise_std, N_sim)

```

We now spend some time explaining the Kalman filter. <br/>
Given $dX_t = \theta(\mu_{\mathrm{ou}} - X_t)\,dt + \sigma_{\mathrm{ou}}\,dW_t$ and $Z_k = H X_{t_k} + \varepsilon_k$, we can introduce the Kalman filter with the following parameters:
- $F = -\theta$: mean-reversion coefficient
- $L = \sigma_{\mathrm{ou}}$: intensity of the process noise
- $Q_{\mathrm{process}} = 1$: variance of the Brownian noise $dW_t$
- $H = 1$: observation coefficient
- $R_{\mathrm{measurement}}$: variance of the observation noise
Formally, we write:

$$
P^-_{k+1} \approx (1 + F\Delta t)^2 P_k + L^2 Q_{\mathrm{process}}\Delta t
$$

that, for this scalar OU model, translates to

$$
P^-_{k+1} \approx (1 - \theta\Delta t)^2 P_k + \sigma_{\mathrm{ou}}^2\Delta t
$$

Then, using Euler's method, we can approximate:

$$
P^-_{k+1} \approx (1 - \theta\Delta t)^2 P_k + \sigma_{\mathrm{ou}}^2\Delta t
$$

where $P^-_{k+1}$ is the uncertainty before using the following observation.

Then, we can still use the Euler approximation with $F = -\theta$ for the following expression:

$$
\hat X^-_{k+1} = \hat X_k + \theta(\mu_{\mathrm{ou}} - \hat X_k)\Delta t
$$

The Kalman gain determines how much we can trust the new observation _(large gain: we can trust it more)_ and is defined as:

$$
K_{k+1} = \frac{P^-_{k+1} H}{H^2 P^-_{k+1} + R_{\mathrm{measurement}}}
$$

Lastly, we update the state with:

$$
\hat X_{k+1} = \hat X^-_{k+1} + K_{k+1}(Z_{k+1} - H \hat X^-_{k+1})
$$

(the term in parentheses represents the <b>innovation</b> or prediction error, _i.e. the difference between the observation and the value predicted by the model)_

Starting from the previous code block, we can implement the Kalman filter as:

```python
# we now apply the Kalman Filter

# System parameters for Kalman Filter
F = -theta
L = sigma_ou
Q_process = 1.0     # Covariance of dW (in the case of unit variance)

H = 1.0
R_measurement = R_obs

dt_filter = dt_sim # Use the same time step for filter discretization

# initial conditions for Kalman filter
x_hat = np.zeros(N_sim)
P = np.zeros(N_sim)

x_hat[0] = Z_obs[0] # Initialize estimate with first observation
P[0] = 1.0           # Initial covariance (high uncertainty)

for k in range(N_sim - 1):

    # Prediction step (using discretized Riccati equation for P)
    P_dot = F * P[k] + P[k] * F + L * Q_process * L - P[k] * H * (1/R_measurement) * H * P[k]
    P_minus = P[k] + P_dot * dt_filter

    x_hat_minus = x_hat[k] + F * x_hat[k] * dt_filter

    # Kalman Gain (discrete-time equivalent for this observation model)
    K_k = P_minus * H / (H * P_minus * H + R_measurement / dt_filter) # Adjust R for discrete observation

    # Update step
    x_hat[k+1] = x_hat_minus + K_k * (Z_obs[k+1] - H * x_hat_minus)
    P[k+1] = (1 - K_k * H) * P_minus
```

## <a id="2"><b>The Inversion Method</b></a>

<b>Method for General Distributions</b>

<b>Inversion Method</b>

We now spend some time presenting the inversion method, useful for transforming _uniformly distributed random numbers_ into arbitrary distributions using a suitable generalized inverse distribution function.

<b>Definition (Generalized inverse distribution function associated to a distribution function)</b>

Let $F: \mathbb{R} \to [0,1]$ be a distribution function. We define the generalized inverse distribution function associated with $F$, denoted as $I_F: (0,1) \to \mathbb{R}$, as the function which satisfies, for all $y \in (0,1)$, the following:

$$
I_F(y) = \inf \{x \in \mathbb{R} : F(x) \ge y\} = \inf(F^{-1}([y,1]))
$$

The <b>crucial switching formula</b> is:

$$
I_F(u) \le x \iff u \le F(x)
$$

From the definition, we can draw some nice properties:
- For all $y \in (0,1)$, the set $\{x \in \mathbb{R}: F(x) \ge y\}$ is not empty and bounded from below.
    - _(This comes from the fact that, given $\lim_{x \to \infty} F(x) = 1$, for all $y$ the set is not empty.)_
    - The fact that $\lim_{x \to -\infty} F(x) = 0$ ensures that the set is bounded from below.
- $I_F$ is also sometimes referred to as the <b>quantile function associated with $F$</b>.
- Consider a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ and $X: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$, an $\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$-measurable function _(imagine $X$ as a model for the change in value of a financial portfolio)._<br/>
Consider $F$, the distribution of $-X$, and let $\alpha \in (0,1)$ be a confidence level, typically a number close to 1 (95%, 99.999%): Then the real number
$$
I_F(\alpha) = \min \{x \in \mathbb{R}: F(x) \ge \alpha\} = \min \{x \in \mathbb{R}: \mathbb{P}(-X \le x) \ge \alpha\} = \\
\min \{x \in \mathbb{R}: \mathbb{P}(-X > x) \le 1 - \alpha\}
$$

is called the <b>Value at Risk with confidence level $\alpha$ associated with $-X$</b> _(i.e. it holds with probability of at least $\alpha$ that the loss $-X$ does not exceed $I_F(\alpha)$)._

<b>Theorem - The Inversion Method</b>

Let $U \sim U(0,1)$ and assume $U \in (0,1)$ almost surely. Then $X = I_F(U)$ has distribution function $F$.

<b>Proof</b>

For every $x \in \mathbb{R}$, the switching formula gives:

$$
P(X \le x) = P(I_F(U) \le x) = P(U \le F(x)) = F(x)
$$

because $U$ is uniform on $(0,1)$. _Intuition: to sample from an arbitrary one-dimensional distribution, we can simply sample one uniform random variable and apply the quantile function to the sample._

---
<br/>
<b>Lemma (Properties of generalized inverse distribution functions)</b>

Let $F: \mathbb{R} \to [0,1]$ be a distribution function and $I_F : (0,1) \to \mathbb{R}$ the generalized inverse distribution function associated with $F$. Then $I_F$ fulfils the following:
1. $I_F$ is non-decreasing.
2. For all $y \in (0,1)$:
    - $F(I_F(y)) \ge y$.
    - $F^{-1}([y,1]) = [I_F(y), \infty)$.
    - $I_F(y) = \min(F^{-1}([y,1]))$.
3. For all $x \in F^{-1}((0,1)) = \{z \in \mathbb{R} : F(z) \in (0,1)\}$: $I_F(F(x)) \le x$.
4. For all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, $y \in (0,1)$: $I_F(y) < x$ if and only if $y \le F(x)$ _(switching formula)_.
5. For all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, $y \in (0,1)$: $I_F(y) > x$ if and only if $y > F(x)$ _(switching formula)_.
6. For all open sets $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ with the property that $F|_D : D \to [0,1]$ is <b>injective</b> and all $y \in F(D)$, we have that $(F|_D)^{-1}(y) = I_F(y)$.

_(Recall: a function $f$ is injective if $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$ implies $x_1 = x_2$; equivalently, distinct inputs have distinct outputs.)_

Note that distribution functions are càdlàg (right-continuous with left limits), while generalized inverse distribution functions are, in contrast, <b>càglàd</b> (left-continuous with right limits).

<br/>
<b>Generalized Inverse Distribution Function for the Bernoulli Distribution</b>

Let $p \in [0,1]$ be a real number and let $F: \mathbb{R} \to [0,1]$ be the distribution function of $\operatorname{Ber}(p)$. Assume also that, for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, it holds:

$$
F(x) = \operatorname{Ber}_p((-\infty, x])
= (1-p)\cdot \delta_0^{\mathbb{R}}((-\infty, x])
+ p\cdot \delta_1^{\mathbb{R}}((-\infty, x])
=
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } x < 0,\\
1-p & \text{if } 0 \le x < 1,\\
1 & \text{if } x \ge 1.
\end{cases}
$$

Then the generalized inverse distribution function $I_F$ is defined as:

$$
I_F(y) = \inf\{x \in \mathbb{R} : F(x) \ge y\}
=
\begin{cases}
0, & 0 < y \le 1-p,\\
1, & 1-p < y \le 1.
\end{cases}
$$

Equivalently, since $1 - U \stackrel{d}{=} U$, we can write:

$$
X = \mathbf{1}_{\{U < p\}}
$$

In Python, we can simulate by doing:

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(12345)

p = 0.3
N = 20_000
U = rng.random(N)
X = (U < p).astype(int)

print("sample mean =", X.mean(), "   theoretical mean =", p)
```

<b>Generalized Inverse Distribution Function for the Exponential Distribution</b>

For $X \sim \operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)$, recall that $F(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}$ for all $x \ge 0$.<br/>

We set $u = F(x)$ and solve for $x$.

$$
u = 1 - e^{-\lambda x} \implies e^{-\lambda x} = 1 - u \implies x = -\frac{\log(1-u)}{\lambda}
$$

Therefore, if $U \sim \operatorname{Uniform}(0,1)$:

$$
X = -\frac{\log(1-U)}{\lambda}
$$

has an $\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)$ distribution.

Since $1 - U$ is still uniformly distributed on $(0,1)$, we have:

$$
X = -\frac{\log U}{\lambda}
$$

So the inverse distribution function is given by:

$$
I_F(u) = -\frac{\log(1-u)}{\lambda}
$$

<b>Generalized Inverse Distribution Function for the Cauchy Distribution</b>

For $X \sim \operatorname{Cauchy}(\mu, \lambda)$:

$$
F(x) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\pi} \arctan\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\lambda}\right)
$$

With a similar substitution, we get:

$$
I_F(u) = \mu + \lambda \tan\left(\pi\left(u - \frac{1}{2}\right)\right)
$$

<b>Reduction to the standard normal</b>

Let $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$ and $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. Then $X = AZ + m$ is still Gaussian, with $\mathbb{E}[X] = m$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(X) = A I_d A^T = AA^T$, so we can write:

$$
AZ + m \sim \mathcal{N}(m, AA^T)
$$

Therefore, to sample from $\mathcal{N}(m, Q)$, we can find a Cholesky factor $A$ such that:

$$
Q = AA^T
$$

Then we can sample $Z_1, \ldots, Z_d \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and form $Z = (Z_1, \ldots, Z_d)^T$.

We can then compute $AZ + m$, which has the mean $m$ and covariance $AA^T = Q$ of the desired distribution.

<b>Box–Muller</b>

Consider $U_1, U_2$ to be independent uniformly distributed random variables on $(0,1)$. Then define:

$$
R = \sqrt{-2\log(U_1)}, \qquad \Theta = 2\pi U_2
$$

$$
X_1 = R\cos(\Theta), \qquad X_2 = R\sin(\Theta)
$$

Then we have:

$$
(X_1, X_2) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_2)
$$

In Python, this can be coded as:

```python
def box_muller(n, rng=np.random.default_rng()):
    m = (n + 1)//2
    u1 = rng.random(m)
    u2 = rng.random(m)
    r = np.sqrt(-2*np.log(u1))
    theta = 2*np.pi*u2
    z = np.empty(2*m)
    z[0::2] = r*np.cos(theta)
    z[1::2] = r*np.sin(theta)
    return z[:n]

z = box_muller(100_000, rng)
print("mean ≈", z.mean())
print("variance ≈", z.var())

#mean ≈ -0.0024074937397393107
# variance ≈ 1.0033222307341456

```

<b>Marsaglia Polar Method</b>

The Box–Muller method evaluates sine and cosine. Marsaglia replaces the angle by rejection sampling in the unit disk.

We first generate $V_1, V_2 \sim U(0,1)$ independently and set:

$$
Y_1 = 2V_1 - 1, \qquad Y_2 = 2V_2 - 1, \qquad S = Y_1^2 + Y_2^2
$$

We reject until $0 < S < 1$, then set:

$$
X_i = Y_i \sqrt{\frac{-2\log S}{S}}, \qquad \text{for } i = 1,2
$$

Then:

$$
(X_1, X_2) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_2)
$$

```python

def marsaglia(n, rng=np.random.default_rng()):
    out = []
    while len(out) < n:
        y = 2*rng.random(2) - 1
        s = y @ y
        if 0 < s < 1:
            factor = np.sqrt(-2*np.log(s)/s)
            out.extend((y*factor).tolist())
    return np.array(out[:n])

z = marsaglia(100_000, rng)
print("mean ≈", z.mean())
print("variance ≈", z.var())

# mean ≈ -0.0017264779658840894
# variance ≈ 1.001218327148807
```

<b>From $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ to $d$-dimensional $\mathcal{N}(m,Q)$</b>

To sample from a $d$-dimensional distribution $\mathcal{N}(m, Q)$, we first generate $d$ independent standard normals:

$$
Z = (Z_1, \ldots, Z_d) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)
$$

Then we factor the target covariance $Q$ as $Q = AA^T$ (Cholesky decomposition) and return $X = m + AZ$, which has the mean and covariance of the target $d$-dimensional Gaussian distribution.

```python

def gaussian_d(mean, cov, rng=np.random.default_rng()):
    mean = np.asarray(mean)
    cov = np.asarray(cov)
    # eigen-factorization also permits positive semidefinite covariance matrices
    lam, V = np.linalg.eigh(cov)
    lam = np.maximum(lam, 0.0)
    A = V @ np.diag(np.sqrt(lam))
    z = box_muller(len(mean), rng)
    return mean + A @ z

m = np.array([1.0, -1.0])
Q = np.array([[2.0, 1.2],
              [1.2, 1.0]])

samples = np.array([gaussian_d(m, Q, rng) for _ in range(30_000)])
print("empirical mean:\n", samples.mean(axis=0))
print("empirical covariance:\n", np.cov(samples.T))


# empirical mean: [ 0.99112792 -1.005388  ]
# empirical covariance:
# [[2.00303886 1.1975438 ] [1.1975438  0.99806283]]
```

<b>Approximation of a Multidimensional Integral by Quadrature: Left Rectangle Rule in Dimension $d$</b>

We now try to approximate $\int_{[a,b]^d} f(x)\,dx$ by quadrature, _i.e. on a $d$-dimensional hypercube $[a,b]^d$ using numerical integration methods that approximate integrals by calculating a weighted sum of function values at specific points_.

We first recall that, _for a function $f$ on a metric space_, its <b>modulus of continuity</b> is defined as:

$$
\omega_f(h) = \sup_{\lVert x-y\rVert \le h} |f(x) - f(y)|
$$

and it measures the maximum possible change in $|f(x) - f(y)|$ when the distance between $x$ and $y$ is at most $h$ _(intuition: it represents how smoothly or rapidly $f$ can change across small steps of size $h$)_.

Also, recall that if $f$ is an $\alpha$-Hölder function, we have:

$$
\omega_f(h) \le |f|_{C^\alpha} h^\alpha
$$

which means that, for a specific class of smooth functions, its maximum change over distance $h$ is bounded by a constant factor.

We now start by integrating using the <b>left rectangle rule in dimension $d$</b>, by first partitioning $[a,b]^d$ into $n^d$ cubes of side length:

$$
h = \frac{b-a}{n}
$$

We then define the <b>lower-left corners</b> (grid points) as:

$$
x_i = a\mathbf{1} + hi, \qquad i = (i_1, \ldots, i_d) \in \{0, \ldots, n-1\}^d
$$

where $i$ is a multi-index representing the grid coordinates in $d$ dimensions and $x_i$ gives the exact coordinates for the lower-left vertex of each sub-cube.

We then define the approximation formula as:

$$
R^n_{[a,b]^d}[f] = h^d \sum_{i \in \{0, \ldots, n-1\}^d} f(x_i)
$$

_Intuition: we evaluate $f$ at the lower corner $x_i$ of each sub-cube, then multiply $f(x_i)$ by $h^d$, which is the $d$-dimensional volume of a single sub-cube. We finally sum over all $n^d$ sub-cubes to get $R^n_{[a,b]^d}$ (a multidimensional generalization of the classical Riemann left-rectangle sum)._

<b>Proposition: Integration Error with Hölder Continuous Functions (2.2.5)</b>

For $f \in L^1([a,b]^d)$, we have:

$$
\left|R^n_{[a,b]^d}[f] - \int_{[a,b]^d} f(x)\,dx\right|
\le (b-a)^d\,\omega_f\!\left(\frac{(b-a)\sqrt{d}}{n}\right).
$$

_Intuition: the integration error is bounded by the volume $(b-a)^d$, the maximum cell diameter $\frac{(b-a)\sqrt{d}}{n}$, and $\omega_f$._

Furthermore, if $f$ is Hölder continuous with exponent $\alpha$, we also have:

$$
\left|R^n_{a,b,d}[f] - \int_{[a,b]^d} f(x)\,dx\right|
\le \frac{(b-a)^{d+\alpha}d^{\alpha/2}}{n^\alpha}\,|f|_{C^\alpha}.
$$

_Intuition: the error decreases with $n^{-\alpha}$, which means that more regular functions (larger $\alpha$) have faster convergence._

To better see this, we can proceed with an example and set $f(x_1, \ldots, x_d) = x_1$ and $[a,b]^d = [0,1]^d$.

In this case, we obtain exactly:

$$
\left|R^n[f] - \int f\right| = \frac{1}{2n}.
$$

Furthermore, we can easily see the following results in Python:

```python
def left_rectangle_1d(f, a, b, n):
    h = (b-a)/n
    x = a + h*np.arange(n)
    return h*np.sum(f(x))

f = lambda x: x
exact = 0.5

for n in [10, 20, 40, 80]:
    err = abs(left_rectangle_1d(f, 0, 1, n)-exact)
    print(f"n={n:3d}, error={err:.8f}, n*error={n*err:.8f}")

# n= 10, error=0.05000000, n*error=0.50000000
# n= 20, error=0.02500000, n*error=0.50000000
# n= 40, error=0.01250000, n*error=0.50000000
# n= 80, error=0.00625000, n*error=0.50000000
```

<b>Trapezoidal Rule</b>

We now proceed with another method for numerical integration _(quadrature)_ in one dimension. We first divide the interval $[a,b]$ into $n$ subintervals of equal width $h = (b-a)/n$, then we can set:

$$
T_{[a,b]}^n[f] = h \left[ \frac{f(a) + f(b)}{2} + \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} f(a + ih) \right].
$$

_Said differently,_ on each interval $[x_i, x_{i+1}]$ we integrate the average of the two endpoint values.

---
<br/>
<b>Proposition: Integration Error with the Trapezoidal Rule (2.2.10)</b>

$$
\left|T^n[f] - \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\right|
\le (b-a)\,\omega_f\!\left(\frac{b-a}{2n}\right).
$$

Therefore, if $f$ is an $\alpha$-Hölder function:

$$
\left|T^n[f] - \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\right|
\le \frac{(b-a)^{1+\alpha}}{(2n)^\alpha}\,|f|_{C^\alpha}.
$$

Furthermore, if $f \in C^1([a,b])$, then we have:

$$
\left|T^n[f] - \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\right|
\le \frac{(b-a)^2}{n}\,\omega_{f'}\!\left(\frac{b-a}{2n}\right).
$$

Therefore, if $f'$ is $\alpha$-Hölder:

$$
\left|T^n[f] - \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\right|
\le \frac{(b-a)^{2+\alpha}}{2^\alpha n^{1+\alpha}}\,|f'|_{C^\alpha}.
$$

So, if $f'$ is $\alpha$-Hölder continuous, the total error is $O(n^{-(1+\alpha)})$. Thus, for Lipschitz $f'$ (that is, $\alpha = 1$), this gives the familiar $O(n^{-2})$ <b>quadratic convergence</b>.

In Python, we can nicely see the convergence by coding:

```python
def trapezoidal(f, a, b, n):
    x = np.linspace(a, b, n+1)
    h = (b-a)/n
    return h*(0.5*f(x[0]) + f(x[1:-1]).sum() + 0.5*f(x[-1]))

# Smooth test: f(x)=exp(x), exact integral e-1
f = np.exp
exact = np.e - 1

```

## <a id="4"><b>Monte Carlo Integration</b></a>

<b>Integration equals expectation</b>

Let $(A, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ be a finite measure space with $\mu(A) > 0$ and consider $f \in L^1(\mu)$.

First, notice that $\mu$ need not be a probability measure because $\mu(A)$ may not be $1$. We therefore normalize it by defining $P(B) = \mu(B) / \mu(A)$, and then define $Y$ as a random point in $A$ distributed according to the normalized measure $\mu$:

$$
Y \sim \frac{\mu}{\mu(A)}.
$$

By the definition of expectation under this distribution, we then have:

$$
\mathbb{E}[f(Y)] = \frac{1}{\mu(A)} \int_A f(x)\,\mu(dx).
$$

If we multiply by $\mu(A)$, we obtain:

$$
\int_A f(x)\,\mu(dx) = \mathbb{E}[\mu(A)f(Y)].
$$

If we then set $X = \mu(A)f(Y)$, take independent samples $Y_1, Y_2, \ldots$ from the distribution of $Y$, and set

$$
X_n = \mu(A) f(Y_n),
$$

then we have

$$
\mathbb{E}[X_n] = \int_A f(x)\,\mu(dx).
$$

Thus, the <b>Monte Carlo estimator</b> is

$$
E_N = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N X_n = \frac{\mu(A)}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N f(Y_n).
$$

By the LLN, we also know that $E_N \to \int_A f(x)\,\mu(dx)$ as $N \to \infty$.

This is the <b>Monte Carlo estimator</b> of $\mathbb{E}[X_1]$.<br/>Furthermore, _by linearity_, we have

$$
\mathbb{E}[E_N] = \mathbb{E}[X_1],
$$

so we can conclude that the Monte Carlo estimator is <b>unbiased</b>.

_For example_, we can consider

$$
I = \int_{-1}^1 e^{\sqrt{|x|}}\,dx, \qquad Y \sim U(-1,1).
$$

Then we have

$$
I = \mathbb{E}[2e^{\sqrt{|Y|}}], \qquad I_N = \frac{2}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N e^{\sqrt{|Y_n|}}.
$$

The exact value is

$$
I = 2 \int_0^1 e^{\sqrt{x}}\,dx = 4 \int_0^1 u e^u\,du = 4.
$$

---
<br/>
<b>Why Monte Carlo is _particularly_ good in high dimensions</b>

Consider $U \sim U([a,b]^d)$. Then:

$$
\int_{[a,b]^d} f(x)\,dx = (b-a)^d\mathbb{E}[f(U)]
$$

and

$$
I_N = \frac{(b-a)^d}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N f(U_n).
$$

- If we consider the <b>deterministic rectangle rule</b> with $N=n^d$ evaluations and an $\alpha$-Hölder function $f$:
  $$
  \text{error} \sim N^{-\alpha/d}.
  $$
- If we use Monte Carlo, <b>independently of $d$</b>:
  $$
  \text{RMSE} \sim N^{-1/2}.
  $$

---

<br/>
<b>Theorem: Strong Law of Large Numbers</b>

Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots \in L^1$ be i.i.d., then:

$$
\frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N X_n \to \mathbb{E}[X_1] \quad \text{almost surely}.
$$

Therefore, the Monte Carlo approximation is <b>strongly consistent</b> _(i.e. it converges)_.

---
<br/>
<b>Theorem: Exact Root Mean Square Error</b>

For $X_1, X_2, \ldots \in L^2$ i.i.d., we have:

$$
\lVert E_N - \mathbb{E}[X_1] \rVert_{L^2} = \frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X_1)}}{\sqrt{N}}.
$$

<b>Proof</b>

Since the estimator is unbiased, we can write:

$$
\mathbb{E}[(E_N - \mathbb{E}[X_1])^2] = \operatorname{Var}(E_N).
$$

By independence:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(E_N) = \frac{1}{N^2} \sum_{n=1}^N \operatorname{Var}(X_n) = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(X_1)}{N}.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\lVert E_N - \mathbb{E}[X_1] \rVert_{L^2}
= \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(E_N)}
= \sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{Var}(X_1)}{N}}.
$$

Thus, RMSE $\sim N^{-1/2}$ _(reducing the error by a factor of 2 costs about 4 times as many samples)_.

---
<br/>
<b>Estimating the unknown variance</b>

Consider:

$$
\overline{X}_N = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N X_n, \qquad \sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var}(X_1).
$$

Two natural estimators are:

$$
\widehat{S}_N^2 = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N (X_n - \overline{X}_N)^2
$$

and the one using <b>Bessel's correction</b>:

$$
S_N^2 = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{n=1}^N (X_n - \overline{X}_N)^2.
$$

Then:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=1}^N (X_n - \overline{X}_N)^2\right] = (N-1)\sigma^2.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{S}_N^2] = \frac{N-1}{N}\sigma^2,
\qquad
\mathbb{E}[S_N^2] = \sigma^2.
$$

Thus, $\widehat{S}_N^2$ is <b>biased downward</b> by $-\sigma^2/N$, while $S_N^2$ is <b>unbiased</b>. _However, note that both are consistent, so being unbiased does not imply a smaller RMSE._

---
<br/>
<b>Theorem: RMSE of the corrected sample variance</b>

Assume $X_n \in L^4$ are i.i.d. and consider:

$$
\mu = \mathbb{E}[X_1], \qquad \sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var}(X_1), \qquad \mu_4 = \mathbb{E}[(X_1 - \mu)^4].
$$

Then, for $N \ge 2$:

$$
\lVert S_N^2 - \sigma^2 \rVert_{L^2}
= \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\left(\mu_4 - \frac{N-3}{N-1}\sigma^4\right)}.
$$

Furthermore, for $N \ge 4$:

$$
\lVert S_N^2 - \sigma^2 \rVert_{L^2} \le \sqrt{\frac{\mu_4}{N}}.
$$

<b>Corollary: RMSE of the biased sample variance</b>

For the biased sample variance $\widehat{S}_N^2$ defined above:

$$
\lVert \widehat{S}_N^2 - \sigma^2 \rVert_{L^2}
= \frac{\sqrt{(N-1)^2\mu_4 + (5N - N^2 - 3)\sigma^4}}{N^{3/2}}.
$$

Regardless of $N$:

$$
\lVert \widehat{S}_N^2 - \sigma^2 \rVert_{L^2} \le \sqrt{\frac{\mu_4}{N}}.
$$

_Recall_ that $\mu_4$ is the <b>fourth central moment</b>, which measures how strongly observations can deviate from the mean, _especially in the tails_. It is used to calculate the <b>kurtosis</b>:

$$
\gamma_2 = \frac{\mu_4}{\sigma^4}.
$$

_Recall also that_, for a Gaussian distribution, $\gamma_2 = 3$. We define the <b>excess kurtosis</b> of a random variable as $\gamma_2 - 3$ _(which is 0 for a Gaussian distribution)_.

---
<br/>
<b>Confidence intervals | CLT only</b>

Consider

$$
E_N = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N X_n, \qquad
\mu = \mathbb{E}[X_1], \qquad
\sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var}(X_1) > 0.
$$

The CLT gives:

$$
\frac{\sqrt{N}(E_N - \mu)}{\sigma}
\xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1).
$$

<b>Theorem: Asymptotically valid CLT confidence intervals</b>

For $\beta, \gamma \ge 0$, which determine the left and right widths of the interval:

$$
\lim_{N\to\infty}
\mathbb{P}\left(
\mu \in
\left[
E_N - \frac{\beta\sigma}{\sqrt{N}},
E_N + \frac{\gamma\sigma}{\sqrt{N}}
\right]
\right)
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}
\int_{-\beta}^{\gamma} e^{-x^2/2}\,dx.
$$

_Equivalently_, using the standard normal cumulative distribution function $\Phi$:

$$
\lim_{N\to\infty}
\mathbb{P}\left(
\mu \in
\left[
E_N - \frac{\beta\sigma}{\sqrt{N}},
E_N + \frac{\gamma\sigma}{\sqrt{N}}
\right]
\right)
= \Phi(\gamma) - \Phi(-\beta).
$$

In other words, if

$$
\frac{\sqrt{N}(E_N - \mu)}{\sigma}
\xrightarrow{d} Z,
\qquad Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1),
$$

then the probability that the interval contains $\mu$ converges to $\mathbb{P}(-\beta \le Z \le \gamma)$.

For a symmetric interval, set $\beta = \gamma = z$. The coverage probability is then:

$$
2\Phi(z) - 1.
$$

Using a _"practical version"_ of this theorem, we can replace $\sigma$ by

$$
S_N = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N (X_n - \overline{X}_N)^2}
$$

and obtain an <b>asymptotically valid</b> $(1-\alpha)$ confidence interval defined as:

$$
\overline{X}_N \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\frac{S_N}{\sqrt{N}}.
$$

---
<br/>
<b>Practical Numerical Integration</b>

Consider $A \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ to be bounded and contained in:

$$
B = [a_1,b_1] \times \cdots \times [a_d,b_d].
$$

We extend $f$ by zero outside $A$:

$$
\widetilde f(x) = f(x)\mathbf{1}_A(x).
$$

Consider independent and uniformly distributed random variables $U_1, U_2, \ldots$ on $B$, i.e. $U_n \sim U(B)$ are i.i.d. Since $U_1$ is uniform on $B$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde f(U_1)] = \frac{1}{|B|} \int_B \widetilde f(x)\,dx.
$$

Because $\widetilde f$ is zero outside $A$:

$$
\int_B \widetilde f(x)\,dx = \int_A f(x)\,dx.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\int_A f(x)\,dx = |B|\,\mathbb{E}[\widetilde f(U_1)].
$$

The <b>Monte Carlo estimator</b> is:

$$
I_N = \frac{|B|}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N f(U_n)\mathbf{1}_A(U_n),
$$

where $|B|$ is the _volume_ of the enclosing box:

$$
|B| = \prod_{i=1}^d (b_i-a_i).
$$

<b>Example: High-dimensional financial integration: Portfolio Value at Risk (VaR)</b>

In quantitative finance, evaluating risk metrics such as VaR for a portfolio of $d$ _correlated assets_ requires computing high-dimensional expectations. We can use the approach proposed above by modelling asset returns as a multivariate Gaussian distribution _(even if asset returns, especially stocks, are not normally distributed and have very fat tails!)_.

$$
R \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma),
$$

where $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the vector of <b>expected returns</b> and $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is the <b>covariance matrix</b>.<br/>Consider also $w \in \mathbb{R}^d$ as the <b>portfolio weight vector</b>, with $\sum_{i=1}^d w_i = 1$. Given this, the <b>total portfolio loss</b> $L$ is defined as:

$$
L(R) = -w^\top R.
$$

We can then define the $100(1-\alpha)\%$ Value at Risk, $\operatorname{VaR}_\alpha$, as the $(1-\alpha)$-quantile of the loss distribution:

$$
\mathbb{P}(L \le \operatorname{VaR}_\alpha) = 1-\alpha.
$$

In Python, we can estimate the VaR for a $d$-dimensional portfolio _(in the snippet below: $d=10$)_ using Monte Carlo integration and tracking the convergence.

```python

def run_portfolio_var_mc(d, N_list, alpha=0.95, rng=None):
    if rng is None:

        # Try to retrieve rng from globals, otherwise initialize a new one
        global_vars = globals()
        if 'rng' in global_vars:
            rng = global_vars['rng']
        else:
            rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)

    # Setup portfolio parameters (d=10 assets)

    # weights vector
    weights = np.ones(d) / d

    # expected return (drift)
    mu = np.linspace(0.01, 0.08, d)

    # Generate a random positive-definite covariance matrix
    A = rng.normal(size=(d, d))

    # Covariance matrix \Sigma = 0.02 * AA^T + 0.05 I_d
    # (the added identity improves stability and prevents \Sigma from being singular)

    Sigma = np.dot(A, A.T) * 0.02 + np.eye(d) * 0.05

    # Since this is Gaussian, we can calculate the analytical VaR for comparison

    port_mu = np.dot(weights, mu)
    port_vol = np.sqrt(np.dot(weights, np.dot(Sigma, weights)))
    from scipy.stats import norm

    # exact formulation
    exact_var = -(port_mu + port_vol * norm.ppf(1 - alpha))

    estimates = []
    ci_lower = []
    ci_upper = []

    # we now proceed with estimation

    for N in N_list:

        # High-dimensional Gaussian sampling via Cholesky decomposition
        L_mat = np.linalg.cholesky(Sigma)
        Z = rng.normal(size=(N, d))

        # R = \mu + Z L^T has approx. desired distribution
        R = mu + np.dot(Z, L_mat.T)

        # Portfolio losses
        losses = -np.dot(R, weights)

        # Estimate VaR (the empirical alpha-quantile)
        # alpha-quantile of the simulated losses

        est_var = np.percentile(losses, alpha * 100)
        estimates.append(est_var)

        # Bootstrap for quantile confidence intervals
        bootstrap_vars = []
        for _ in range(200):
            boot_sample = rng.choice(losses, size=N, replace=True)
            bootstrap_vars.append(np.percentile(boot_sample, alpha * 100))

        # The interval is an approximate 95% bootstrap confidence interval for VaR.
        ci_lower.append(np.percentile(bootstrap_vars, 2.5))
        ci_upper.append(np.percentile(bootstrap_vars, 97.5))

    return N_list, np.array(estimates), np.array(ci_lower), np.array(ci_upper), exact_var

# Fallback initialization in the script level if 'rng' is not defined globally
if 'rng' not in globals():
    rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)

d = 10
N_list = np.logspace(2.5, 5.5, num=10, dtype=int)
Ns, estimates, ci_lower, ci_upper, exact_var = run_portfolio_var_mc(d, N_list, rng=rng)
```

<b>Wiener Process from an Isonormal Gaussian Process</b>

In this section, we want to characterize a one-dimensional Wiener process. To do that, we start with the <b>isonormal Gaussian map</b>.

Let $H = L^2([0,1];\mathbb{R})$ and let $(e_n)_{n\geq 1}$ be an orthonormal basis of $H$. Assume that $\Omega$ carries independent $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ random variables $(\varepsilon_n)_{n\geq 1}$.

For $f \in H$, define:

$$
\eta_N(f) = \sum_{n=1}^N \langle f,e_n\rangle_H\,\varepsilon_n.
$$

Then:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[|\eta_N(f)-\eta_M(f)|^2\right]
= \sum_{n=M+1}^N |\langle f,e_n\rangle_H|^2
\longrightarrow 0.
$$

Therefore, we have:

$$
\eta(f) = \lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^N
\langle f,e_n\rangle_H\,\varepsilon_n.
$$

The map $\eta$ is <b>linear</b>, and we also have:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\eta(f)\eta(g)] = \langle f,g\rangle_H.
$$

Since every finite vector $(\eta(f_1),\ldots,\eta(f_k))$ is jointly Gaussian, $\eta$ is an <b>isonormal Gaussian process over $H$</b>.

We can now define the Wiener process by

$$
W_t = \eta(\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]}),
\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1.
$$

The process $W$ is centered Gaussian, and

$$
\mathbb{E}[W_sW_t]
= \left\langle \mathbf{1}_{[0,s]},\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]}\right\rangle_{L^2}
= \min(s,t).
$$

Therefore,

$$
W_t-W_s = \eta(\mathbf{1}_{(s,t]})
\sim \mathcal{N}(0,t-s).
$$

Furthermore, for two disjoint intervals $(s_i,t_i]$ and $(s_j,t_j]$,

$$
\left\langle \mathbf{1}_{(s_i,t_i]},\mathbf{1}_{(s_j,t_j]}\right\rangle_{L^2}=0.
$$

Thus, the corresponding Gaussian increments are <b>uncorrelated</b> and therefore independent.

<b>Numerical Picture of the Isonormal Construction</b>

To visualize this, we can construct the Brownian motion numerically. First, we choose a <b>real trigonometric orthonormal basis</b> of $L^2([0,1])$:

$$
e_0(x)=1,
$$

$$
e_{2k-1}(x)=\sqrt{2}\cos(2\pi kx),
\qquad
 e_{2k}(x)=\sqrt{2}\sin(2\pi kx),
\qquad k\geq 1.
$$

We can then construct $W_t$ for every $t$ as

$$
W_t=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon_n\int_0^t e_n(s)\,ds.
$$

_Recall that_ the space is

$$
L^2([0,1])=\left\{f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}:\int_0^1|f(x)|^2\,dx<\infty\right\},
$$

with inner product

$$
\langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^1 f(x)g(x)\,dx.
$$

In Python, we write:

```python
def isonormal_brownian_trig(t, modes=200, rng=rng):
    t = np.asarray(t)
    W = rng.standard_normal() * t
    for k in range(1, modes + 1):
        xi_cos = rng.standard_normal()
        xi_sin = rng.standard_normal()
        int_cos = np.sqrt(2) * np.sin(2 * np.pi * k * t) / (2 * np.pi * k)
        int_sin = np.sqrt(2) * (1 - np.cos(2 * np.pi * k * t)) / (2 * np.pi * k)
        W += xi_cos * int_cos + xi_sin * int_sin
    return W
```

## <a id="3"><b>Stochastic Integration and Itô Calculus</b></a>

<b>Stochastic Process</b>

Fix a complete probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$. We recall that a random variable $X$ is a mapping $\Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ such that

$$
X^{-1}(A) := \{\omega \in \Omega : X(\omega) \in A\} \in \mathcal{F} \quad \forall A \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}).
$$

We define a <b>real-valued stochastic process</b> as a family of real-valued random variables $(X_t)_{t \in \mathcal{T}}$ indexed by a non-empty set $\mathcal{T}$.

_We often write $(X_t)_{t \in \mathcal{T}}$ instead of $X_t$, $t \in \mathcal{T}$._

Stochastic processes can be defined both in <b>discrete time</b>, where $\mathcal{T} \subset \mathbb{N}_0$, and in continuous time, where $\mathcal{T} \subset [0, \infty)$. In the following notes, we consider $\mathcal{T} = [0,T]$ for a finite $T > 0$.

There are three ways to interpret stochastic processes:
1. for each time $t \in \mathcal{T}$, as a random variable $X_t: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ of the event $\omega \in \Omega$
2. for each event $\omega \in \Omega$, as a sample path $X(\omega): \mathcal{T} \to \mathbb{R}$
3. as a joint mapping $X: \Omega \times \mathcal{T} \to \mathbb{R}$ of event and time

In continuous time, we usually require continuity properties for the sample paths $t\mapsto X_t(\omega)$ and joint $\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{T})$-measurability of $(\omega,t)\mapsto X_t(\omega)$.

Furthermore, two real-valued stochastic processes $(X_t)_{t\in\mathcal{T}}$ and $(Y_t)_{t\in\mathcal{T}}$ on the same probability space are called:
- <b>modifications</b> if, for every $t\in\mathcal{T}$, $\mathbb{P}(X_t=Y_t)=1$;
- <b>indistinguishable</b> if there is an event $A\in\mathcal{F}$ with $\mathbb{P}(A)=1$ such that $X_t(\omega)=Y_t(\omega)$ for every $t\in\mathcal{T}$ and $\omega\in A$.

_Note that_ indistinguishability implies modification, but not conversely.

<b>Definition: Filtration</b>

A filtration on a probability space $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ is a family $(\mathcal{F}_t)_{t\in\mathcal{T}}$ of $\sigma$-algebras such that:
1. $\mathcal{F}_t\subseteq\mathcal{F}$ for all $t\in\mathcal{T}$;
2. $\mathcal{F}_s\subseteq\mathcal{F}_t$ whenever $s\le t$ ("memory is perfect").

_Intuition: $\sigma$-algebras represent information, while filtrations represent its flow over time._

A probability space equipped with a filtration $(\mathcal{F}_t)_{t\in\mathcal{T}}$ is called a <b>filtered probability space</b>, denoted by $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_t)_{t\in\mathcal{T}},\mathbb{P})$.

_Intuition: as time passes, $\mathcal{F}_t$ contains everything known at time $t$._ <br/>For example, for a Brownian motion $(W_t)$, we often use:

$$
\mathcal{F}_t^W=\sigma(W_s:0\le s\le t).
$$

Thus, $\mathcal{F}_t^W$ contains the history of the Brownian motion up to time $t$, but not its future.

<b>Natural Filtration and Adaptedness</b>

The natural filtration $(\mathcal{F}_t^X)$ of a stochastic process $(X_t)$ is defined by:

$$
\mathcal{F}_t^X=\sigma(X_s:0\le s\le t),\qquad t\in\mathcal{T}.
$$

The natural filtration describes the flow of information generated by observing the process over time.

Furthermore, a process $(X_t)$ is <b>adapted</b> to $(\mathcal{F}_t)$ if $X_t$ is $\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable for every $t\in\mathcal{T}$. Equivalently,

$$
\mathcal{F}_t^X\subseteq\mathcal{F}_t\qquad\forall t\in\mathcal{T}.
$$

Therefore, adaptedness means that $X_t$ can be determined using only information available at time $t$ and cannot depend on the future.

For example, if $\mathcal{F}_t$ contains the history of a Brownian motion up to time $t$, then $W_t$ is <b>adapted</b>. However, $W_{t+1}$ is generally not $\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable because it depends on <b>future randomness</b>.

<b>Usual Conditions for a Filtration</b>

Consider $\mathcal{T}=[0,T]$ or $\mathcal{T}=[0,\infty)$. A continuous-time filtration $(\mathcal{F}_t)_{t\in\mathcal{T}}$ satisfies the <b>usual conditions</b> if it is:
1. <b>complete</b>: if $A\subseteq B\in\mathcal{F}$ and $\mathbb{P}(B)=0$, then $A\in\mathcal{F}_0$;
2. <b>right-continuous</b>: $\mathcal{F}_t=\bigcap_{s>t}\mathcal{F}_s$ for every $t$ before the terminal time.

Let

$$
\mathcal{N}=\{A\subseteq B:B\in\mathcal{F},\ \mathbb{P}(B)=0\}
$$

be the collection of null sets. The right-continuous completion is defined by

$$
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_t=\bigcap_{s>t}\sigma(\mathcal{F}_s\cup\mathcal{N}),
$$

with $\overline{\mathcal{F}}_T=\sigma(\mathcal{F}_T\cup\mathcal{N})$ when $\mathcal{T}=[0,T]$.

The completeness condition makes null events known at time $0$. Right-continuity means that information available immediately after time $t$ contains nothing genuinely new compared with the information available at time $t$.

_Note that, in the context of stochastic integration, we usually work with filtrations satisfying the usual conditions._

<b>Random Times and Stopping Times</b>

Recall that $\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathbb{N}_0$ or $\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_+$.

A <b>random time</b> is a random variable $\tau:\Omega\to\mathcal{T}\cup\{\infty\}$.

_Generally speaking_, a random time represents when a random event occurs.

Given a filtration $(\mathcal{F}_t)$, a random time $\tau$ is an $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-<b>stopping time</b> if

$$
\{\tau\le t\}\in\mathcal{F}_t
\qquad\forall t\in\mathcal{T}.
$$

_Intuition: at any fixed time $t$, we can determine whether the stopping event has already occurred using only the information in $\mathcal{F}_t$._

Suppose that $\mathcal{T}=[0,\infty)$, that $(\mathcal{F}_t)$ satisfies the usual conditions, and that $(X_t)$ is a real-valued, $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-adapted process with almost surely continuous paths. Then, for $a>0$,

$$
\tau(\omega)=\inf\{t\ge0:|X_t(\omega)|>a\}
$$

is an $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-stopping time. Indeed, continuity of the paths gives

$$
\{\tau<t\}=\bigcup_{q\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,t)}\{|X_q|>a\}\in\mathcal{F}_t,
$$

and right-continuity then implies $\{\tau\le t\}\in\mathcal{F}_t$.

<b>Conditional Expectation</b>

First, consider a real-valued random variable $X\in L^1(\mathbb{P})$ and a $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}$.

The <b>conditional expectation</b> $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]$ is any random variable $Y$ such that:
- $Y$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable;
- $Y\in L^1(\mathbb{P})$;
- for every $C\in\mathcal{G}$,
  $$
  \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_C Y]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_C X].
  $$

<b>Existence Theorem</b>

Let $X\in L^1(\mathbb{P})$ and let $\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}$ be a $\sigma$-algebra. Then $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]$ exists and is unique up to almost-sure equality.

_Furthermore_, conditional expectation defines a positive, continuous, linear operator.

<b>Properties of Conditional Expectations</b>

Assume that all random variables are integrable and that $\mathcal{G}$ and $\mathcal{H}$ are $\sigma$-algebras.

1. If $X$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable, then $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]=X$.
2. If $Z$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable, then $\mathbb{E}[ZX\mid\mathcal{G}]=Z\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]$ almost surely.
3. If $\sigma(X)$ and $\mathcal{H}$ are independent, then $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{H}]=\mathbb{E}[X]$ almost surely.
4. If $\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{G}$, then
   $$
   \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\mid\mathcal{H}]=\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{H}]
   $$
   almost surely (the <b>tower property</b>).
5. For a convex function $g$ such that the expectations exist,
   $$
   \mathbb{E}[g(X)\mid\mathcal{G}]\ge g(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}])
   $$
   almost surely (conditional Jensen's inequality).

6. If $(X_n)$ is an increasing sequence of nonnegative random variables converging to $X$, then conditional monotone convergence gives
   $$
   \mathbb{E}[X_n\mid\mathcal{G}]\to\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]
   \qquad\text{almost surely}.
   $$
7. If $X_n\to X$ almost surely and $|X_n|\le Z$ almost surely for an integrable random variable $Z$, then conditional dominated convergence gives
   $$
   \mathbb{E}[X_n\mid\mathcal{G}]\to\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]
   \qquad\text{almost surely}.
   $$
8. If $X_n\ge Z$ almost surely for an integrable random variable $Z$, conditional Fatou's lemma gives
   $$
   \mathbb{E}[\liminf_{n\to\infty}X_n\mid\mathcal{G}]
   \le \liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[X_n\mid\mathcal{G}]
   \qquad\text{almost surely}.
   $$

<b>Martingales</b>

A stochastic process $(X_t)$ is an $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-<b>martingale</b> if:
1. $(X_t)$ is $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-adapted;
2. $\mathbb{E}[|X_t|]<\infty$ for every $t\in\mathcal{T}$;
3. $\mathbb{E}[X_t\mid\mathcal{F}_s]=X_s$ almost surely whenever $s\le t$.

A family $\mathcal{A}\subset L^1(\mathbb{P})$ is <b>uniformly integrable</b> if

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{Z\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}[|Z|\mathbf{1}_{\{|Z|>n\}}]=0.
$$

If $Y\in L^1(\mathbb{P})$ and $M_t=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{F}_t]$, then $(M_t)$ is a uniformly integrable martingale.

<br/>
<b>Brownian Motion</b>

Consider $\mathcal{T}=[0,T]$ or $\mathcal{T}=[0,\infty)$. A standard one-dimensional Brownian motion (Wiener process) $(W_t)$ is a continuous stochastic process such that:
1. $W_0=0$ almost surely;
2. the sample paths $t\mapsto W_t(\omega)$ are almost surely continuous;
3. for every $0=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_k$, the increments $(W_{t_i}-W_{t_{i-1}})_{i=1}^k$ are independent;
4. for $s<t$,
   $$
   W_t-W_s\sim\mathcal{N}(0,t-s).
   $$

An $n$-dimensional Brownian motion $(W_t)$ is a column vector $(W_t^1,\ldots,W_t^n)^\mathsf{T}$ whose components are independent standard one-dimensional Brownian motions.

---
<br/>
<b>Brownian Motion Relative to a Filtration</b>

We often want a stochastic process to be a Brownian motion with respect to the flow of information modeled by a filtration.

Given a filtration $(\mathcal{F}_t)$, a process $(W_t)$ is called an $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-Brownian motion if:
- $(W_t)$ is $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-adapted;
- $(W_t)$ is a Brownian motion;
- for every $t\ge0$, the future-increment process $(W_{t+s}-W_t)_{s\ge0}$ is independent of $\mathcal{F}_t$.

Every Brownian motion $(W_t)$ is a $(\overline{\mathcal{F}}_t^W)$-Brownian motion, where $(\overline{\mathcal{F}}_t^W)$ is the right-continuous completion of its natural filtration.

Furthermore, the map $(\omega,t)\mapsto W_t(\omega)$ is $\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{T})$-measurable.

---
<br/>
<b>$p$-Variation of Brownian Motion</b>

For $p\in(0,\infty)$ and $f:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}$, define the $p$-variation along the dyadic partitions by:

$$
V_p(f;t):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{2^n}
\left|f\left(\frac{Tk}{2^n}\wedge t\right)-f\left(\frac{T(k-1)}{2^n}\wedge t\right)\right|^p,
$$

when the limit exists. The $1$-variation is called the <b>total variation</b> _(it adds up all the absolute increments and measures the "total distance" traveled by the path)_, and the $2$-variation is called the <b>quadratic variation</b> _(it adds up the squares of the increments and, for Brownian motion, captures the accumulated randomness)_.

For larger $p$, small increments become much smaller after taking the power, so the sum is less sensitive to fine-scale fluctuations.

For Brownian motion, almost surely:

$$
[W]_t=V_2(W;t)=t,
\qquad
V_1(W;t)=\infty.
$$

Then, for any $p>2$, we have

$$
V_p(W;t)=0.
$$

<b>Martingales for Brownian Motions</b>

Let $(W_t)$ be an $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-Brownian motion. Then:
1. $(W_t)$ is a square-integrable $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-martingale.
2. The process
   $$
   X_t=W_t^2-t
   $$
   is an $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-martingale.
3. For any $\theta\in\mathbb{R}$, the process
   $$
   L_t=\exp\left(-\frac12\theta^2t-\theta W_t\right)
   $$
   is an $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-martingale.

<b>Stochastic Itô Integral of Simple Processes</b>

Let $\mathcal{T} = [0,T]$ for some $T > 0$. We fix the same probability space supporting an $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-Brownian motion $(W_t)$.

A stochastic process $(K_t)$ is a <b>simple process</b> if there exist times $0 = t_0 < t_1 < \ldots < t_n = T$ and bounded $\mathcal{F}_{t_j}$-measurable random variables $\bar K_j$ such that:

$$
K_t(\omega) = \bar K_{-1}(\omega)\mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}(t) + \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \bar K_j(\omega)\mathbf{1}_{(t_j, t_{j+1}]}(t).
$$

The <b>stochastic integral of a simple process</b> $(K_t)$ is then defined as:

$$
\left(\int_0^t K_s\,dW_s\right)(\omega) = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \bar K_j(\omega)\left(W_{t_{j+1} \wedge t}(\omega) - W_{t_j \wedge t}(\omega)\right),
$$

where $t \wedge s = \min\{t, s\}$.

_Intuition (gains/losses of a self-financing trading strategy): in finance, we assume the stock moves as Brownian motion. At time $t_j$, we buy $K_j$ shares at price $W_{t_j}$ and at time $t_{j+1}$ we sell at price $W_{t_{j+1}}$, so the integral between $j$ and $j+1$ is just the P&L in this trade. Note that $K_j$ has to be $\mathcal{F}_{t_j}$-measurable in order to get the number of shares to buy._

Then, <b>Itô's isometry</b> holds for all $t \in [0,T]$:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_0^t K_s\,dW_s\right)^2\right] = \mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^t K_s^2\,ds\right] = \int_0^t \mathbb{E}[K_s^2] \,ds.
$$

_Intuition: Itô's isometry states that the expected squared size of the stochastic integral equals the expected accumulated squared size of the integrand._


Then, the process $\left(\int_0^t K_s\,dW_s\right)_{0 \le t \le T}$ is a <b>square-integrable martingale</b>.

Then, _again_, for simple processes $K$ and $H$ and $a,b \in \mathbb{R}$, we have the very nice <b>linearity property</b>:

$$
\int_0^t (aK_s + bH_s)\,dW_s = a\int_0^t K_s\,dW_s + b\int_0^t H_s\,dW_s.
$$

The <b>continuity property</b> also holds, since the sample paths $t \mapsto \left(\int_0^t K_s\,dW_s\right)$ are continuous.

<b>Simple Processes: The Martingale Property</b>

Let $I_t := \int_0^t K_u\,dW_u$, which is <b>adapted</b> by definition and square-integrable by Itô's isometry.

We fix $0 \le s \le t \le T$ and refine the partition without changing $K$ or $I$, so that $s = t_l$ and $t = t_k$. Then we have:

$$
I_t - I_s = \sum_{j=l}^{k-1} \bar K_j\Delta W_j, \qquad \Delta W_j = W_{t_{j+1}} - W_{t_j}.
$$

For $j \ge l$, we have that $\mathcal{F}_s \subseteq \mathcal{F}_{t_j}$. <br/>The <b>tower property</b>, the independence of the Brownian increments, and their zero mean ($\mathbb{E}[\Delta W_j] = 0$) give:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}[\bar K_j\Delta W_j \mid \mathcal{F}_s]
&= \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\bar K_j\Delta W_j \mid \mathcal{F}_{t_j}] \mid \mathcal{F}_s] \\
&= \mathbb{E}[\bar K_j\mathbb{E}[\Delta W_j \mid \mathcal{F}_{t_j}] \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = 0.
\end{aligned}
$$

The first equality holds since $\mathcal{F}_s \subseteq \mathcal{F}_{t_j}$, and the second equality follows because $\Delta W_j$ is independent of $\mathcal{F}_{t_j}$.

If we sum over $j = l, \ldots, k-1$, we obtain:

$$
\mathbb{E}[I_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = I_s + \mathbb{E}[I_t - I_s \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = I_s.
$$

<b>Stochastic Itô Integral of Square-Integrable Processes</b>

<b>Definition: Predictable $\sigma$-algebra</b>

The predictable $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{P}$ on $\Omega \times [0,T]$ is the $\sigma$-algebra generated by all <b>left-continuous</b> and $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-adapted processes $(H_t)$. <br/>Note that the predictable $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{P}$ can also be defined as the $\sigma$-algebra generated by all simple processes $(K_t)$.

For a <b>predictable process</b> $(H_t)$, we define the $L^2$ norm as:

$$
\lVert H \rVert^2_{L^2(\Omega \times [0,T], \mathcal{P}, d\mathbb{P} \times dt)} = \mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^T H_s^2\,ds\right] = \int_0^T \mathbb{E}[H_s^2] \,ds.
$$

Note that since $H$ is predictable, it is also _progressively measurable_, i.e. $H_s(\omega)\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]}(s)$ is $\mathcal{F}_t \otimes \mathcal{B}([0,t])$-measurable _(intuition: up to time $t$, the whole path of $H$ is determined by information known at $t$)._

For each $H \in L^2(\Omega \times [0,T], \mathcal{P}, d\mathbb{P} \times dt)$, there is a sequence of simple processes $(K^n_t)_{0\le t\le T}$ such that

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^T (H_s-K^n_s)^2\,ds\right]
=\int_0^T \mathbb{E}[(H_s-K^n_s)^2] \,ds \to 0.
$$

_Intuition: simple predictable processes are dense in the space of square-integrable predictable processes. Their stochastic integrals therefore approximate the stochastic integrals of those processes in $L^2$, by Itô's isometry._

We now spend some time showing that every square-integrable predictable process $H$ can be approximated in $L^2$ by simple processes.

We first <b>truncate</b> the square-integrable process $H$ by defining:

$$
H^M := (-M) \vee (H \wedge M).
$$

_(This bounds $H$ in the interval $[-M,M]$.)_ Since $H^M \to H$ pointwise and $|H^M-H|^2 \le 4|H|^2$, <b>dominated convergence</b> gives:

$$
\lVert H^M-H\rVert_{L^2} \to 0.
$$

We then extend $H^M_s=0$ for $s<0$ and define the <b>moving average over the recent past</b> (bounded by $M$, continuous, and adapted in $t$):

$$
H^{M,m}_t(\omega) := m\int_{t-1/m}^{t} H^M_s(\omega)\,ds, \qquad t\in[0,T].
$$

Lebesgue differentiation and dominated convergence give $H^{M,m} \to H^M$ in $L^2$ as $m\to\infty$.

We then <b>discretize at left endpoints</b>. For $t_j=jT/N$, we set:

$$
K^{M,m,N}_t := \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} H^{M,m}_{t_j}\mathbf{1}_{(t_j,t_{j+1}]}(t).
$$

For fixed $M$ and $m$, uniform continuity and dominated convergence give $K^{M,m,N} \to H^{M,m}$ in $L^2$ as $N\to\infty$. Therefore, a <b>diagonal sequence</b> gives $K^n=K^{M_n,m_n,N_n}\to H$ in $L^2$.

Then, $(K_t^n)_{0\le t\le T}$ is a <b>Cauchy sequence</b>, i.e. for $n,m\to\infty$:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^T (K_s^m-K_s^n)^2\,ds\right]
=\int_0^T \mathbb{E}[(K_s^m-K_s^n)^2] \,ds \to 0.
$$

By Itô's isometry, for every $t\in[0,T]$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_0^t K_s^n\,dW_s-\int_0^t K_s^m\,dW_s\right)^2\right]
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_0^t (K_s^n-K_s^m)\,dW_s\right)^2\right] \\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^t (K_s^n-K_s^m)^2\,ds\right] \\
&\le\mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^T (K_s^n-K_s^m)^2\,ds\right]
\longrightarrow 0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, $(\int_0^t K_s^n\,dW_s)_{n\ge1}$ is a Cauchy sequence in $L^2(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_t,\mathbb{P})$.

Since $L^2(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_t,\mathbb{P})$ is complete, there exists a random variable $I_t(H)\in L^2(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_t,\mathbb{P})$ such that

$$
I_t(H)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^t K_s^n\,dW_s
\qquad\text{in }L^2(\mathbb{P}).
$$

Therefore, for $H\in L^2(\Omega\times[0,T],\mathcal{P},d\mathbb{P}\times dt)$, we define the <b>stochastic integral</b> by:

$$
\int_0^t H_s\,dW_s := I_t(H) := \lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^t K_s^n\,dW_s
\qquad\text{in }L^2(\mathbb{P}),
$$

where $(K_t^n)_{n\ge1}$ is a sequence of simple processes satisfying:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^T (H_s-K_s^n)^2\,ds\right]
=\int_0^T \mathbb{E}[(H_s-K_s^n)^2] \,ds \to 0.
$$

_Again_, <b>Itô's isometry</b> holds:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_0^t H_s\,dW_s\right)^2\right]
=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^t H_s^2\,ds\right]
=\int_0^t \mathbb{E}[H_s^2] \,ds.
$$

The process $(\int_0^t H_s\,dW_s)_{0\le t\le T}$ is a <b>square-integrable martingale</b>; as stated above, linearity and continuity also hold.

<b>Doob's Maximal $L^2$ Inequality</b>

<b>Theorem</b>

If $(M_t)_{0\le t\le T}$ is a right-continuous, square-integrable $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-martingale, then:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\le t\le T}|M_t|^2\right] \le 4\mathbb{E}[|M_T|^2].
$$

In particular, for a simple process, Doob's inequality and Itô's isometry give:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\le t\le T}|I_t(K)|^2\right]
\le 4\mathbb{E}[|I_T(K)|^2]
=4\mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^T K_s^2\,ds\right].
$$

Furthermore,

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\le t\le T}|I_t(K^n)-I_t(K^m)|^2\right]
\le 4\mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^T (K_s^n-K_s^m)^2\,ds\right].
$$

<b>Associativity of Stochastic Integrals</b>

For a predictable process $H\in L^2$ and $0\le a<b\le T$, we have:

$$
\int_a^b H_s\,dW_s
=\int_0^T H_s\mathbf{1}_{(a,b]}(s)\,dW_s
=\int_0^b H_s\,dW_s-\int_0^a H_s\,dW_s.
$$

In general, for predictable processes $H$ and $K$ such that $KH\in L^2$:

$$
\int_0^t K_s\,d\left(\int_0^s H_r\,dW_r\right)
=\int_0^t K_sH_s\,dW_s.
$$

Furthermore, if $K$ is a <b>simple process</b>, then:

$$
\left(\int_0^t K_s\,d\left(\int_0^s H_r\,dW_r\right)\right)(\omega)
=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\bar K_j(\omega)
\int_{t_j\wedge t}^{t_{j+1}\wedge t}H_s(\omega)\,dW_s.
$$

_Intuition: the stochastic integral with respect to the martingale $\int_0^t H_s\,dW_s$ accumulates the values of $K$ against the increments of that martingale._


<b>Stochastic Itô Integrals of Predictable Processes</b>

Finally, we can define the stochastic integral for <b>predictable processes</b> $H_t$ satisfying

$$
\int_0^T H_s(\omega)^2\,ds<\infty
\qquad\text{almost surely}
$$

using the <b>localization technique</b>.

For each $n\in\mathbb{N}$, define the <b>stopping time</b>:

$$
\tau_n(\omega)=\inf\left\{t\in[0,T]:\int_0^t H_s(\omega)^2\,ds>n\right\},
\qquad \inf\varnothing=\infty.
$$

_Intuition: $\int_0^t H_s^2\,ds$ represents the size, or energy, of $H$. The stopping time $\tau_n$ is the first time this size exceeds $n$._

The sequence $(\tau_n)_{n\ge1}$ is a <b>localizing sequence</b> of $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-stopping times.

We define the <b>stopped integrand</b> by

$$
H_s^n := H_s\mathbf{1}_{\{s\le\tau_n\}}.
$$

Then $H^n$ is <b>square-integrable</b>, and

$$
N_t^n:=\int_0^t H_s^n\,dW_s
$$

is well-defined. The family $(N_t^n)$ is <b>consistent</b>, meaning that for $m\ge n$:

$$
N^m_{t\wedge\tau_n}=N^n_{t\wedge\tau_n}.
$$

Therefore, for $0\le t\le\tau_n$:

$$
\left(\int_0^t H_s\,dW_s\right)(\omega)
=\left(\int_0^t H_s^n\,dW_s\right)(\omega).
$$

However, note that, since we use the localization technique, the integral is no longer necessarily an $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-martingale, but only an $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-local martingale.

_(Recall that a stochastic process has a property $P$ locally if there exists a localizing sequence such that the stopped process has property $P$.)_

Luckily, we still have the <b>linearity property</b> for two predictable processes $K,H\in L^2$ and $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$:

$$
\int_0^t (aK_s+bH_s)\,dW_s
= a\int_0^t K_s\,dW_s+b\int_0^t H_s\,dW_s.
$$

In particular, the mapping
$$
I_t:L^2(\Omega\times[0,T],\mathcal{P},d\mathbb{P}\times dt)\to L^0(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_t,\mathbb{P})
$$
is a <b>continuous linear operator</b>, where the metric is defined by

$$
d_0(H,K):=\mathbb{E}\left[\min\left\{1,\left(\int_0^T (H_s-K_s)^2\,ds\right)^{1/2}\right\}\right].
$$

<b>Burkholder–Davis–Gundy Inequalities</b>

For $p\in(0,\infty)$, define $\lVert Z\rVert_p:=\mathbb{E}[|Z|^p]^{1/p}$. Then there exists $C_p>0$ such that:

- <b>BDG 1: terminal-time estimate</b>

$$
\left\lVert\int_0^t H_s\,dW_s\right\rVert_p
\le C_p\left\lVert\left(\int_0^t H_s^2\,ds\right)^{1/2}\right\rVert_p.
$$

- <b>BDG 2: maximal estimate</b>

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\le t\le T}\left|\int_0^t H_s\,dW_s\right|^p\right]^{1/p}
\le C_p\left\lVert\left(\int_0^T H_s^2\,ds\right)^{1/2}\right\rVert_p.
$$

Furthermore, for $p\in[2,\infty]$, Minkowski's inequality gives:

$$
\left\lVert\left(\int_0^t H_s^2\,ds\right)^{1/2}\right\rVert_p
\le\left(\int_0^t\lVert H_s\rVert_p^2\,ds\right)^{1/2}.
$$

<b>Quadratic Variation</b>

For $M_t=\int_0^t H_s\,dW_s$, the quadratic variation satisfies:

$$
[M]_t=\lim_{n\to\infty}q_t^n(M) = \int_0^t H_s^2\,ds
\qquad\text{almost surely}.
$$

For the dyadic partition, define

$$
q_t^n(M):=\sum_{k=1}^{2^n}
\left|M_{(Tk/2^n)\wedge t}-M_{(T(k-1)/2^n)\wedge t}\right|^2.
$$

<b>Itô Calculus</b>

<b>Itô processes</b> are given by:

$$
dX_t=a_t\,dt+b_t\,dW_t.
$$

This is shorthand for the integral equation, for $t\ge0$:

$$
X_t=X_0+\int_0^t a_s\,ds+\int_0^t b_s\,dW_s.
$$

Here, $(a_t)$ and $(b_t)$ are <b>predictable processes</b> such that

$$
\int_0^t |a_s|\,ds<\infty,
\qquad
\int_0^t |b_s|^2\,ds<\infty
\quad\text{almost surely}.
$$

Differentials are a tool for computation using the following rules:

- $d[W]_t=(dW_t)^2=dt$.
- $dW_t\,dt=0$.
- $(dt)^2=d[t]=0$.

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
d[X]_t=(dX_t)^2
&=a_t^2(dt)^2+2a_tb_t\,dt\,dW_t+b_t^2(dW_t)^2 \\
&=b_t^2\,dt.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here, $[W]_t$ denotes the <b>quadratic variation</b> of $W$ up to time $t$, defined as:

$$
[W]_t=\lim_{\|\Pi\|\to0}\sum_i(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i})^2.
$$

Recall also that, for a Brownian motion, $[W]_t=t$, usually written as $(dW_t)^2=dt$.

<b>Itô's Formula</b>

Given a $C^{1,2}$ function $f(t,x)$ and an Itô process

$$
dX_t=a_t\,dt+b_t\,dW_t,
$$

the process $F_t=f(t,X_t)$ is also an Itô process satisfying:

$$
\begin{aligned}
df(t,X_t)
&=\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(t,X_t)\,dt
+\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(t,X_t)\,dX_t
+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}(t,X_t)(dX_t)^2 \\
&=\left[
\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(t,X_t)
+a_t\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(t,X_t)
+\frac{1}{2}b_t^2\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}(t,X_t)
\right]dt
+b_t\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(t,X_t)\,dW_t.
\end{aligned}
$$


_For example_, if $X_t=W_t$, then $dX_t=0\,dt+1\,dW_t$, so $a_t=0$ and $b_t=1$. According to Itô's formula:

$$
df(t,W_t)=\left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(t,W_t)+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}(t,W_t)\right]dt+\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(t,W_t)\,dW_t.
$$

<b>Multidimensional Itô Processes</b>

Suppose that $(W_t)_{t\ge0}$ is an $n$-dimensional Brownian motion. Consider the $m$-dimensional Itô process $(X_t^1,\ldots,X_t^m)$ given by:

$$
dX_t^i=a_t^i\,dt+\sum_{\ell=1}^n b_t^{i\ell}\,dW_t^\ell,
\qquad i=1,\ldots,m.
$$

Here, $(a_t^i)$ and $(b_t^{i\ell})$ are predictable stochastic processes such that the integrals are well-defined.

Moreover, the Itô multiplication rules are:

- $(dt)^2=0$;
- $dW_t^i\,dt=0$;
- $dW_t^i\,dW_t^j=\delta_{ij}\,dt$, where $\delta_{ij}=1$ if $i=j$ and $0$ otherwise.

For a $C^{1,2}$ function $f:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^m\to\mathbb{R}$, define
$F_t=f(t,X_t^1,\ldots,X_t^m)$. The multidimensional Itô formula is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
df(t,X_t)
&=\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(t,X_t)\,dt
+\sum_{i=1}^m\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(t,X_t)\,dX_t^i \\
&\quad+\frac12\sum_{i,k=1}^m
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\partial x_k}(t,X_t)\,dX_t^i\,dX_t^k \\
&=\left[
\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(t,X_t)
+\sum_{i=1}^m a_t^i\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(t,X_t)
+\frac12\sum_{i,k=1}^m\sum_{\ell=1}^n
b_t^{i\ell}b_t^{k\ell}
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\partial x_k}(t,X_t)
\right]dt \\
&\quad+\sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{\ell=1}^n
b_t^{i\ell}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(t,X_t)\,dW_t^\ell.
\end{aligned}
$$


<b>Integration by Parts</b>

Suppose we have an $n$-dimensional Brownian motion $(W_t)$ and the pair of Itô processes:

$$
dX_t=a_t\,dt+b_t\,dW_t,
$$

$$
dY_t=c_t\,dt+e_t\,dW_t.
$$

Then the product $(X_tY_t)$ is still an <b>Itô process</b> satisfying:

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(X_tY_t)
&=X_t\,dY_t+Y_t\,dX_t+dX_t\,dY_t \\
&=(Y_ta_t+X_tc_t+b_te_t^{\mathsf T})\,dt
+(Y_tb_t+X_te_t)\,dW_t.
\end{aligned}
$$
