### <a id="" ><b>1. Introduction to Time Series</b></a>

<b>Time Series Definition</b>

A time series is a <b>stochastic process </b> indexed by time $t \in T \subseteq \R$.

Examples _(they are all continuous!):_
- $T = [0,1]$: Brownian bridge from $(0,0)$ to $(1,0)$, where the pair notation denotes $(\text{time}, \text{value}) = (t, X_t)$
- $T = [0, + \infty]$: Brownian motion starting at $(0,0)$
- $T = \R$: two-sided Brownian motion
- $T = \N_0$, or $T = \Z$: discrete random walk.

We use the notation $\{X_t\}_{t \in T}$ to denote a random variable and the notation $\{x_t\}_{t \in T}$ to denote realizations of that variable.

A <b>time series model</b> is either the specification of:
- all joint distributions $f(\{X_t\}_{t \in T})$ (i.e., the model provides $\Pr(X_{t_1} \le x_{t_1}, \ldots, X_{t_k} \le x_{t_k})$ for all $k \in \N$, $t_1, \ldots, t_k$, and $x_1, \ldots, x_k \in \R$)
- all their means and covariances $(\mathbb{E}(X_t), \text{Cov}(X_t, X_p))$ for all $t, p \in T$

<br/>
<b>White noise</b>

The i.i.d. noise $\{X_t\}_{t \in T}$ is a sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables such that $\mathbb{E}(X_t) = 0$ for all $t$. <br/>In general, we also assume that $\mathbb{E}[X_t^2] = \text{Var}(X_t) < \infty$ for all $t$. _Note that in this case $\mathbb{E}[X_t^2]$ does not depend on $t$._

<b>Rademacher Process</b>

Consider $\{X_t\}_{t \in T}$ such that $\Pr(X_t = -1) = \Pr(X_t = 1) = 1/2$ for all $t$.

If $\{B_t\}_t \sim_{\mathrm{iid}} \text{Bernoulli}(1/2)$, then:
- $\{X_t\}_t = 2\{B_t\}_t - 1$
- $\text{Var}(X_t) = 4\text{Var}(B_t) = 1$ _(note that $X_t^2 = 1$ and $\mathbb{E}(X_t) = 0$)_

<b>White noise</b>

$\{X_t\}_t$ is a sequence of centered and uncorrelated random variables. More specifically, $\mathbb{E}(X_t) = 0$, $\mathbb{E}(X_t^2) < \infty$, and $\text{Cov}(X_t, X_s) = 0$ for all $t \neq s$.


- Note that i.i.d. noise such that $\mathbb{E}(X_t^2) < \infty$ is also white noise.
- The term "white" is used by analogy with white light. Indeed, white noise with constant variance $v^2$ has a flat spectral density given by $f(\lambda) = v^2 / 2\pi$, with $\lambda \in [-\pi, \pi]$ (this means that all time-series frequencies are present).

<b>Random walk</b>

Let $\{X_t\}_t$ be i.i.d. noise such that $\mathbb{E}(X_t^2) < \infty$ for all $t \in \N$.

$\{S_t\}_{t \in \N_0}$ is a random walk if $S_0 = 0$ and $S_t = \sum_{i=1}^t X_i$ for all $t \ge 1$.


If $\{X_t\}_{t \in \N_0}$ is a Rademacher process, then $\{S_t\}_{t \in \N_0}$ is called a <b>symmetric random walk</b>, with $\mathbb{E}(S_t) = \sum_{i=1}^t \mathbb{E}(X_i) = 0$.

<b>Time Series with trend</b>

Let $\{Y_t\}_t$ be a zero-mean time series and $\{m_t\}_t$ be a deterministic (non-random) sequence. <br/>Consider $X_t = Y_t + m_t$ for all $t \in T$.

Then $\mathbb{E}[X_t] = m_t$ is called the <b>trend</b> of $\{X_t\}_t$.

<b>Time Series with seasonality</b>

Given a zero-mean time series $\{Y_t\}_t$ and a function $t \mapsto p_t$ such that $p_t$ is periodic with known period $d$ (i.e., $p_{t+d} = p_t$).

Consider the time series $X_t = Y_t + p_t$ for all $t \in T$. Then $\{p_t\}_t$ is called the <b>seasonal component</b> of $\{Y_t\}_t$.

For example, we could have the seasonal component in the following forms:

$$
p_t = a_0 + \sum_{j=1}^k \left(a_j \cos\left(\frac{2 \pi \alpha_j t}{d}\right) + b_j \sin\left(\frac{2 \pi \beta_j t}{d}\right)\right)
$$

with known integers $k \ge 1$, $\alpha_j, \beta_j > 0$, and unknown $a_0, a_j, b_j$ for all $1 \le j \le k$.

<b>Time series with both trend and seasonal component</b>

$$X_t = m_t + p_t + Y_t, \forall t \in T$$

with $\{m_t\}_t$ the trend, $\{p_t\}_t$ the seasonal component, and $\{Y_t\}_t$ a zero-mean time series.


<b>Stationarity</b>

A time series $\{X_t\}_t$ is said to be strictly (strongly) stationary if, for all $n \ge 1$ and $h \in \Z$, it holds that:

$$
(X_1, \ldots, X_n) \overset{d}{=} (X_{1+h}, \ldots, X_{n+h})
$$

i.e., the stochastic behavior of $\{X_t\}_t$ remains unchanged for _any_ time shift.

Let $\{X_t\}_t$ be a time series.

- The mean function of $\{X_t\}_t$ is defined as:

$$
\mu_x(t) = \mathbb{E}(X_t), \forall t \in T
$$

- The covariance function of $\{X_t\}_t$ is defined as:
$$
\begin{align*}
\gamma_x(r,p) &= \text{Cov}(X_r, X_p) = \\
&= \mathbb{E}[(X_r - \mu_x(r))(X_p - \mu_x(p))] \\
&= \mathbb{E}[X_r X_p] - \mu_x(r)\mu_x(p)
\end{align*}
$$ 

provided that $\mathbb{E}[|X_r X_p|] < \infty$ and $\mathbb{E}[X_r] < \infty$ for all $r,p$.

On the other hand, $\{X_t\}_t$ is said to be (weakly) stationary if $\mathbb{E}[X_t^2] < \infty$ for all $t$, and:
- $\mu_x(t)$ is constant in $t$
- $\gamma_x(t, t+h)$ depends only on $h$

<b>Weak Stationarity</b>

A time series $\{X_t\}_{t \in \mathbb{Z}}$ is said to be <b>weakly stationary</b> if:
- the mean function is constant over time: $\mu_x(t) = \mathbb{E}(X_t) = \mu \in \mathbb{R}$
- the covariance $\gamma_X(r,s) = \operatorname{Cov}(X_r, X_s)$ depends only on $h = s - r$

_Intuition:_ Weak stationarity means a series’ average level and dependence pattern stay stable over time.

_Note also that_ the covariance between two time points depends only on their time distance, not on their absolute values _(i.e., not on their calendar dates)_. _On the other hand,_ strict stationarity is a strong concept since the full distribution must be constant over time.

_For example,_ financial prices often show a trend and are not stationary, so we usually work with log returns, such as $r_t = \log P_t - \log P_{t-1}$, which can be closer to weak stationarity and supports tools like ARMA models, autocorrelation analysis, and covariance-based risk estimates.

_However,_ financial returns are not reliable in this sense since volatility clustering, market regimes, and structural breaks can strongly change their distribution.

_Given the definition,_ we can understand why the <b>strict stationarity</b> of $\{X_t\}$ implies that all the $X_t$ have the same marginal distribution.

To show this, set $n = 1$. By the definition of strict stationarity, for any lag $h \in \mathbb{Z}$ we have:

$X_1 \overset{d}{=} X_{1+h}$

Furthermore, since $\{1+h: h \in \mathbb{Z}\} = \mathbb{Z}$, it follows that
$X_1 \overset{d}{=} X_t$ for all $t \in \mathbb{Z}$.

_On the other hand,_ we can now spend some time understanding what a <b>moment</b> means in this context.

In the table below, you can find a summary comparing the first and second moments for single and paired random variables.

$$
\begin{aligned}
W:\quad
&\text{1st moment: } \mathbb{E}(W),\\
&\text{2nd moment: } \mathbb{E}(W^2)\ /\ \operatorname{Var}(W).\\[6pt]
(W_1,W_2):\quad
&\text{1st moments: } \mathbb{E}(W_1),\ \mathbb{E}(W_2),\\
&\text{2nd moments: } \mathbb{E}(W_1^2),\ \mathbb{E}(W_2^2),\ \mathbb{E}(W_1W_2)\\
&\hspace{7.3em}/\ \operatorname{Var}(W_1),\ \operatorname{Var}(W_2),\ \operatorname{Cov}(W_1,W_2).
\end{aligned}
$$

We also recall that moments can be generated by the moment-generating function, defined as:

$$M_W(t) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{tW}\right]$$

$$
M_{W_1,W_2}(s,t)
= \mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{sW_1+tW_2}\right]
$$

<b>Autocovariance and autocorrelation functions</b>

Let $\{X_t\}_t$ be a stationary time series. The <b>Autocovariance Function (ACVF)</b> at lag $h \in \mathbb{Z}$ is defined as:

$$\gamma_X(h) = \operatorname{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+h})$$

_Similarly,_ the <b>Autocorrelation Function (ACF)</b> at lag $h$ is defined as:

$$
\rho_X(h) = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+h})}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X_t)\operatorname{Var}(X_{t+h})}} = \frac{\gamma_X(h)}{\gamma_X(0)}
$$

The second equality holds because, if $\{X_t\}_t$ is stationary, the following hold:

$$\operatorname{Var}(X_t) = \operatorname{Cov}(X_t, X_t) = \gamma_X(0), \quad \forall t \in \mathbb{Z}.$$

$$
\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X_t)}\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X_{t+h})}
= \sqrt{\gamma_X(0)}\sqrt{\gamma_X(0)}
= \gamma_X(0).
$$

<b>Key Properties of ACVF and ACF</b>
- <b>Boundedness</b>: by the _Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,_ we have that:

$$
|\operatorname{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+h})| \le \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X_t)}\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X_{t+h})}
\iff |\gamma_X(h)| \le |\gamma_X(0)|
\iff |\rho_X(h)| \le 1.
$$

_Recall that the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality states that, for random variables $X$ and $Y$ with finite second moments:_

$$
\left|\mathbb{E}[XY]\right|
\leq
\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[X^2]\,\mathbb{E}[Y^2]}.
$$

- <b>Symmetry</b>: since $\gamma_X$ and $\rho_X$ are <b>even functions</b> _(i.e., $f(x) = f(-x)$ for all $x$)_, for any $h \in \mathbb{Z}$ we have:

$$
\gamma_X(h) = \operatorname{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+h}) = \operatorname{Cov}(X_{t+h}, X_t).
$$

If we then set $t' = t+h$, we have

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X_{t'}, X_{t'-h}) = \gamma_X(-h),
\quad \text{so } \gamma_X(-h) = \gamma_X(h).
$$

$$
\rho_X(-h) = \frac{\gamma_X(-h)}{\gamma_X(0)} = \frac{\gamma_X(h)}{\gamma_X(0)} = \rho_X(h).
$$

<b>I.I.D. Noise Process</b>

Let $\{X_t\}_t$ be an i.i.d. noise process such that $\mathbb{E}[X_t] = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[X_t^2] = \operatorname{Var}(X_t) = \sigma^2$. In this case, we use the notation $\{X_t\}_t \sim \operatorname{IID}(0, \sigma^2)$.

For any $h \in \mathbb{Z}$, we also have

$$
\gamma_X(t, t+h) = \operatorname{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+h}) =
\begin{cases}
\operatorname{Var}(X_t) = \sigma^2, & \text{if } h = 0,\\
0, & \text{if } h \neq 0.
\end{cases}
$$

The zero covariance for $h \neq 0$ is implied by independence.

_Furthermore,_ $\mu_X(t) = 0$ for all $t$, so the process $\{X_t\}_t$ is stationary, with ACVF and ACF given by:

$$\gamma_X(h) = \sigma^2 \mathbf{1}_{h=0}, \quad \rho_X(h) = \mathbf{1}_{h=0}.$$

_Also,_ note that an i.i.d. noise process is also <b>strictly stationary</b>.

<b>White Noise Process</b>

_On the other hand,_ let $\{X_t\}_t$ be a white noise process with variance $\sigma^2$. In this case, we say $\{X_t\}_t \sim \operatorname{WN}(0, \sigma^2)$ if the process satisfies the following:

$$\mathbb{E}[X_t] = 0, \quad \mathbb{E}[X_t^2] = \sigma^2 < \infty, \quad \operatorname{Cov}(X_t, X_s) = 0 \quad \forall t \neq s.$$

_Note also_ that the process is <b>stationary</b> with ACVF $\gamma_X(h) = \sigma^2 \mathbf{1}_{h=0}$.

Note that in the case of i.i.d. noise, the $X_t$ are independent and identically distributed, while for white noise we just require <b>uncorrelatedness</b>, zero mean, and finite constant variance _(independence is not required!)_

<b>Example: Random Walk Process</b>

We recall that $\{S_t\}_t$ is a random walk if $S_0 = 0$ and $S_t = \sum_{i=1}^t X_i$, where $\{X_t\}_t \sim \operatorname{IID}(0, \sigma^2)$.

_Also, recall_ that a random walk has mean zero, $\operatorname{Var}(S_0) = 0$, and, for all $t \ge 1$, we have:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(S_t) = \operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{i=1}^t X_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^t \operatorname{Var}(X_i) = t\sigma^2.
$$

_We immediately notice that_, since $\operatorname{Var}(S_t)$ depends on time $t$, the process is <b>not stationary</b>.

We now spend some time computing the autocovariance for a random walk. Consider $t \ge 1$ and $h \ge 1$, so we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\gamma_S(t, t+h) &= \operatorname{Cov}(S_t, S_{t+h}) \\
&= \operatorname{Cov}\left(\sum_{i=1}^t X_i, \sum_{i=1}^t X_i + \sum_{i=t+1}^{t+h} X_i\right) \\
&= \operatorname{Cov}\left(\sum_{i=1}^t X_i, \sum_{i=1}^t X_i\right) + \operatorname{Cov}\left(\sum_{i=1}^t X_i, \sum_{i=t+1}^{t+h} X_i\right) \\
&= \operatorname{Var}(S_t) + 0 = t\sigma^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

The last equality holds because

$$
\operatorname{Cov}\left(\sum_{i=1}^t X_i, \sum_{j=t+1}^{t+h} X_j\right)
= \sum_{i=1}^t \sum_{j=t+1}^{t+h} \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = 0
$$

by independence.

If we also consider the special case $t=0$, for which $\gamma_S(0,h) = 0$, we can conclude that, for all $t,h \in \mathbb{N}_0$, we have

$$\gamma_S(t, t+h) = t\sigma^2.$$

_Secondly,_ we can do the same for the <b>autocorrelation</b> of a random walk. For all $h \in \mathbb{N}_0$ and $t \ge 1$, we can compute:

$$
\rho_S(t, t+h) = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(S_t, S_{t+h})}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(S_t)}\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(S_{t+h})}} = \frac{t\sigma^2}{\sqrt{t\sigma^2}\sqrt{(t+h)\sigma^2}} = \sqrt{\frac{t}{t+h}}.
$$

We can now characterize the <b>asymptotic behavior of random walk autocorrelation</b>.

- If we fix lag $h$ and let $t \to \infty$, we have _stronger correlation as the length of shared history grows_:

$$\lim_{t \to \infty} \rho_S(t, t+h) = 1.$$

- If we fix $t$ and let $h \to \infty$, we have _weaker correlation for larger time gaps_:

$$\lim_{h \to \infty} \rho_S(t, t+h) = 0.$$

<b>First-Order Moving Average Process | MA(1)</b>

Let $\{Z_t\}_t \sim \operatorname{WN}(0, \sigma^2)$ and $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$. Consider the time series $\{X_t\}_t$ defined as:

$$X_t = Z_t + \theta Z_{t-1}$$

_Intuition:_ An MA(1) process combines today's random shock $Z_t$ with the previous period's shock $Z_{t-1}$, scaled by $\theta$. The sign of $\theta$ determines whether consecutive shocks reinforce or offset each other.

_Given this,_ we define the <b>mean function</b> as:

$$
\mu_X(t) = \mathbb{E}[X_t] = \mathbb{E}[Z_t + \theta Z_{t-1}] = \mathbb{E}[Z_t] + \theta \mathbb{E}[Z_{t-1}] = 0, \quad \forall t \in \mathbb{Z}.
$$

_Secondly,_ we can define the <b>autocovariance function (ACVF)</b> of an MA(1) process for all $h \in \mathbb{Z}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\gamma_X(t, t+h) &= \operatorname{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+h}) \\
&= \operatorname{Cov}(Z_t + \theta Z_{t-1}, Z_{t+h} + \theta Z_{t+h-1}) \\
&= \operatorname{Cov}(Z_t, Z_{t+h}) + \theta\operatorname{Cov}(Z_t, Z_{t+h-1}) \\
&\quad + \theta\operatorname{Cov}(Z_{t-1}, Z_{t+h}) + \theta^2\operatorname{Cov}(Z_{t-1}, Z_{t+h-1}) \\
&= \sigma^2\mathbf{1}_{h=0} + \theta\sigma^2\mathbf{1}_{h=1} + \theta\sigma^2\mathbf{1}_{h=-1} + \theta^2\sigma^2\mathbf{1}_{h=0} \\
&= \sigma^2(1 + \theta^2)\mathbf{1}_{h=0} + \theta\sigma^2\left(\mathbf{1}_{h=1} + \mathbf{1}_{h=-1}\right) \\
&= \sigma^2(1 + \theta^2)\mathbf{1}_{h=0} + \theta\sigma^2\mathbf{1}_{|h|=1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The equalities follow from the fact that white noise has zero covariance across different times ($\operatorname{Cov}(Z_i, Z_j) = 0$) and variance $\sigma^2$ at each time. Each indicator marks when two shocks in the expansion are the same shock.

_So,_ since this depends only on the lag $h$, the MA(1) process is <b>stationary</b>, with the following ACVF:

$$
\gamma_X(h) =
\begin{cases}
\sigma^2(1 + \theta^2), & \text{if } h = 0,\\
\theta\sigma^2, & \text{if } h \in \{-1, 1\},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

_Similarly,_ we can compute the <b>autocorrelation function</b> of MA(1), given by:

$$
\rho_X(h) = \frac{\gamma_X(h)}{\gamma_X(0)} =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } h = 0,\\
\frac{\theta}{1 + \theta^2}, & \text{if } h \in \{-1, 1\},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

<b>First-Order Autoregressive Process | AR(1)</b>

Let $\{Z_t\}_t \sim \operatorname{WN}(0, \sigma^2)$ and consider a stationary time series $\{X_t\}_t$ satisfying:

$$X_t = \Phi X_{t-1} + Z_t, \quad t \in \mathbb{Z}$$

where $\Phi \in (-1,1)$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(X_s, Z_t) = 0$ for all $s < t$.

_Intuition:_ The current value is the previous value, scaled by $\Phi$, plus a shock. $\Phi$ controls how much of the past carries forward. _Note that_ AR(1) is useful when past values help predict future values, while MA(1) is useful when the dependence comes from recent shocks; these processes will soon be combined in ARMA models.

The <b>mean function</b> of $\{X_t\}_t$ is then:

Since the time series is stationary, we have that $\mu_X(t) = \mathbb{E}[X_t] = \mu \in \mathbb{R}$. Taking expectations on both sides yields:

$$
\mathbb{E}[X_t] = \Phi\mathbb{E}[X_{t-1}] + \mathbb{E}[Z_t]
\iff \mu = \Phi\mu + 0
\iff \mu(1 - \Phi) = 0.
$$

But since $\Phi \in (-1,1)$, we cannot have $\Phi = 1$, so it follows that $\mu = 0$.

_As before,_ the <b>autocovariance function</b> for AR(1) can be computed as follows for $h \ge 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\gamma_X(h) &= \operatorname{Cov}(X_{t-h}, X_t) \\
&= \operatorname{Cov}(X_{t-h}, \Phi X_{t-1} + Z_t) \\
&= \Phi\operatorname{Cov}(X_{t-h}, X_{t-1}) + \operatorname{Cov}(X_{t-h}, Z_t).
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $t-h < t$, according to the condition in the AR(1) definition, $\operatorname{Cov}(X_{t-h}, Z_t) = 0$, so we write:

$$
\gamma_X(h) = \Phi\gamma_X(h-1), \quad \implies \quad \gamma_X(h) = \Phi^h\gamma_X(0), \quad \forall h \ge 1.
$$

_However,_ we still have to compute $\gamma_X(0)$, which in this case is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\gamma_X(0) &= \operatorname{Var}(X_t) = \operatorname{Var}(\Phi X_{t-1} + Z_t) \\
&= \Phi^2\operatorname{Var}(X_{t-1}) + 2\Phi\operatorname{Cov}(X_{t-1}, Z_t) + \operatorname{Var}(Z_t).
\end{aligned}
$$

So we can conclude that $\gamma_X(0) = \Phi^2\gamma_X(0) + \sigma^2$, which gives $\gamma_X(0) = \frac{\sigma^2}{1-\Phi^2}$.

And, _since the function $h \to \gamma_X(h)$ is even,_ we finally state:

$$\gamma_X(h) = \frac{\sigma^2}{1-\Phi^2}\Phi^{|h|}, \quad \forall h \in \mathbb{Z}.$$

_Similarly,_ the <b>autocorrelation function</b> of AR(1) is _simply given_ by:

$$\rho_X(h) = \frac{\gamma_X(h)}{\gamma_X(0)} = \Phi^{|h|}, \quad \forall h \in \mathbb{Z}.$$

### <a id="3" ><b>3. Sample Estimation and Testing</b></a>

We now consider $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ to be observations from a stationary time series with:
- <b>sample mean</b> $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n} x_t$
- <b>sample ACVF</b> at lag $h$ given by: $\hat{\gamma}(h) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n-|h|}(x_{t+|h|} - \bar{x})(x_t - \bar{x}), \quad \forall |h| \le n - 1$
- <b>sample ACF</b> at lag $h$ given by: $\hat{\rho}(h) = \frac{\hat{\gamma}(h)}{\hat{\gamma}(0)} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n-|h|}(x_{t+|h|} - \bar{x})(x_t - \bar{x})}{\sum_{t=1}^{n}(x_t - \bar{x})^2}, \quad \forall |h| \le n - 1$

_Note that_ we divide by $n$ and not by $n - |h|$ to obtain a <b>positive semidefinite matrix</b>.

_In particular,_ let $y_t = x_t - \bar{x}$ be the centered observation and define, for $h \ge 0$:

$$\hat{\gamma}(h) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n-h} y_t y_{t+h}.$$

Then build the <b>Toeplitz matrix</b> $\hat{\Gamma}$ by setting $\hat{\Gamma}_{ij}=\hat{\gamma}(|i-j|)$.

_Recall also_ that "positive semidefinite" means that, for all vectors $a$, we have:

$$a^T\hat{\Gamma}a \ge 0.$$

To see the reason, imagine the data $y_t$ outside the observed range _(i.e., we only observed $(y_1,\ldots,y_n)$, so define $y_t=0$ for all other integer times)_. The quadratic form can be written as

$$a^T\hat{\Gamma}a = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i y_{k-i}\right)^2,$$

which is <b>nonnegative</b> because it is a sum of squares.

_Intuition:_ Using $n$ as the denominator preserves the structure and allows the matrix to represent squared combinations of the data.

_Note also that,_ for all time series such that the seasonal component $s_t=0$, we can consider the <b>sample estimation of the trend</b> as

$$x_t = m_t + y_t, \quad t \in \mathbb{Z}, \quad \text{with } \mathbb{E}[Y_t]=0 \text{ for all } t.$$

<br/>
<b>Estimation with Two-Sided Moving Average</b>

We now spend some time presenting two different estimation approaches. _Firstly,_ we begin with the <b>two-sided moving average approach</b>.

Suppose there exist $a,b \in \mathbb{R}$ and an integer $q \ge 1$ such that, near $t$, the following approximation for the trend term $m_t$ holds (i.e., a two-sided moving average), assuming the underlying model is $X_t = m_t + Y_t$:

$$
m_{t'} \approx a + bt'
$$

for all times $t'$ in the interval $[t-q, t+q]$. _Note that_ the trend does not have to be a line globally, just to look linear on a small window of width $2q + 1$ around $t$.

The estimate then averages the $2q + 1$ observations centered on $t$ and is given by:

$$
\hat m_t = \frac{1}{2q+1}\sum_{i=t-q}^{t+q} X_i = \frac{1}{2q+1}\sum_{j=-q}^{q} X_{t-j}
$$

If we substitute $X_{t-j} = m_{t-j} + Y_{t-j}$ and split the sum, we get:

$$
\hat m_t = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2q+1}\sum_{j=-q}^{q} m_{t-j}}_{\text{signal part}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{2q+1}\sum_{j=-q}^{q} Y_{t-j}}_{\text{noise part}}
$$

If we use the local linearity approximation, i.e., $m_{t-j} \approx a + b(t-j)$, we get:

$$
\sum_{j=-q}^{q}\big(a+b(t-j)\big) = \sum_{j=-q}^{q}(a+bt) - b\sum_{j=-q}^{q} j = (2q+1)(a+bt)
$$

_We notice now_ that the offsets cancel, i.e., $\sum_{j=-q}^{q}j = 0$, so averaging preserves the trend's value at the center while averaging the noise.

The condition $q+1 \le t \le n-q$ just says the whole window must fit within the observed data, from time $1$ to $n$.

So we can conclude that:

$$
\hat m_t \approx a + bt + \frac{1}{2q+1}\sum_{j=-q}^{q} Y_{t-j} \approx m_t + \frac{1}{2q+1}\sum_{j=-q}^{q} Y_{t-j}
$$

That translates into saying that $\hat m_t$ equals the true trend plus a small, averaged-out noise term.

Consider also that:
- Large $q$ averages out more noise, so the estimate is smoother, but the window should be small enough for the trend to be linear.
- Small $q$, _on the other hand,_ keeps the linearity assumption more plausible but leaves more noise _(and this is the famous bias-variance trade-off!)_

_Furthermore,_ for a large $q$, we can apply the <b>Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN)</b> to conclude that:

$$
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} Y_i \xrightarrow{P} 0 \quad \text{as } N\to\infty
$$

_Intuition:_ for $N = 2q + 1$, as $q$ grows, the noise term is concentrated near $0$.

_Furthermore,_ for uncorrelated noise with variance $\sigma^2$, Chebyshev's inequality allows us to be more precise and state that:

$$
P\left(\left|\frac{1}{2q+1}\sum_{j=-q}^{q} Y_{t-j}\right| > \varepsilon\right) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{(2q+1)\,\varepsilon^2} \to 0
$$

We now spend some more time on the <b>bias-variance trade-off</b>. Let's start by considering:

$$
\{Y_t\}_t \sim \operatorname{WN}(0, \sigma^2), \qquad \Delta_{t'} = m_{t'} - (a + bt'), \qquad t' \in \{t-q, \ldots, t+q\}
$$

_Note that_ we assumed $\{Y_t\}_t$ to be white noise, so it has mean $0$, variance $\sigma^2$, and no correlation between samples. _Also, note that_ $\Delta_{t'}$ is the linearization error _(measuring how far the true trend is from the approximation line $a + bt'$)_

We now calculate the expectation of the estimator. The white-noise term vanishes because it has zero mean:

$$
\mathbb{E}(\hat m_t)=\frac{1}{2q+1}\sum_{j=-q}^{q}\mathbb{E}(X_{t-j}) = \frac{1}{2q+1}\sum_{j=-q}^{q} m_{t-j}
$$

If we subtract and add the line $a + bt'$, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}[\hat m_t] &= \frac{1}{2q+1}\sum_{j=-q}^{q} \big(m_{t-j}-(a+b(t-j))\big)+\frac{1}{2q+1}\sum_{j=-q}^{q} (a+b(t-j)) \\
&= \frac{1}{2q+1}\sum_{j=-q}^{q} \Delta_{t-j} + a+bt
\end{aligned}
$$

where the second equality holds since the average of the line equals $a + bt$, _as shown before_.

We now define the <b>bias of the estimator</b> as:

$$
\text{Bias}(q)=\mathbb{E}(\hat m_t)-m_t=\frac{1}{2q+1}\sum_{j=-q}^{q}\Delta_{t-j}+a+bt-m_t=\frac{1}{2q+1}\sum_{j=-q}^{q}\Delta_{t-j}-\Delta_t
$$

_Intuition:_ the bias is the average linearization error over the window, minus the linearization error at the center. _Therefore,_ if the trend is truly linear, both terms are $0$ and the estimator is <b>unbiased</b>. <br/>_On the other hand,_ if the trend is curved, the bias is non-zero, and it usually grows with $q$ _(window size)_ because a wider window includes points where the line fits worse.

_Furthermore,_ we can compute the <b>mean squared error (MSE)</b> using its standard decomposition:

$$
\text{MSE}(q)=\mathbb{E}[(\hat m_t-m_t)^2]=\text{Bias}(q)^2+\text{Var}(\hat m_t)
$$

_Lastly,_ if we compute its variance, we immediately notice that, since $m_t$ is <b>deterministic</b>, it only comes from the noise, where, since the errors are uncorrelated, the variance _"simply"_ adds up:

$$
\text{Var}\Big(\frac{1}{2q+1}\sum_{j=-q}^{q} Y_{t-j}\Big)=\frac{(2q+1)\,\sigma^2}{(2q+1)^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{2q+1}
$$

It is still interesting to point out that the plain average defined before is a special case of a <b>broader family of estimators</b>. Indeed, _so far_ we used one specific way to average the window, equal weights, but this is not the only choice:

$$
\hat m_t=\sum_{j=-q}^{q} \tfrac{1}{2q+1}\,X_{t-j}
$$

_For example,_ we could put more weight near the center or more at the edges. In general, we can then say that:

$$
\hat m_t=\sum_{j=-q}^{q} w_j X_{t-j},\quad \sum_{j=-q}^{q} w_j=1,\quad \sum_{j=-q}^{q} j\,w_j=0
$$

where each condition can be written as follows:
- $\sum_{j=-q}^{q} w_j = 1$ implies each constant trend passes through unchanged.
- $\sum_{j=-q}^{q} j w_j = 0$ implies a linear trend passes through unchanged.

_Note also that_ any symmetric weights satisfy the second condition, and that $w_j = 1 / (2q + 1)$ is the simplest choice.

<b>Parametric Estimation via Least Squares</b>

We now spend some time with parametric estimation. Consider a model $X_t = m_t + Y_t$, where $Y_t$ is noise, with the <b>parametric assumption</b> that the trend belongs to a finite-dimensional family of functions. _That is,_ we can consider a polynomial of degree $k$ such that $m_t = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 t + \cdots + \alpha_k t^k$, where the unknowns are the $k + 1$ coefficients.

For the <b>least-squares estimator</b>, we want to find the coefficients that make the curve as close as possible to the data _under the squared-error_ sense, i.e., we want to find:

$$
(\hat{\alpha}_0, \hat{\alpha}_1, \ldots, \hat{\alpha}_k) = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\alpha_0,\ldots,\alpha_k} C(\alpha_0,\ldots,\alpha_k),
$$

$$
C(\alpha_0,\ldots,\alpha_k) = \sum_{t=1}^{n} (X_t - \alpha_0 - \alpha_1 t - \cdots - \alpha_k t^k)^2.
$$

_Intuition:_ $C$ is the total squared vertical distance between the data points and the polynomial, and _note that_ we square it since we want a smooth, convex function. We therefore want to minimize it by finding the unique point where the slope _in every direction_ is $0$; this means the least-squares estimator is a <b>stationary point</b> of $C$.

We can then set $\partial C / \partial \alpha_i = 0$, which gives the following linear system of $k + 1$ equations in $k + 1$ unknowns:

$$
\sum_{t=1}^{n} t^i \left(X_t - \sum_{j=0}^{k} \alpha_j t^j\right) = 0, \quad i = 0, \ldots, k.
$$

_Intuition:_ the residuals are orthogonal to every regressor $1, t, t^2, \ldots, t^k$.

_We can proceed with illustrative examples._

If $k = 0$ _(i.e., we have a constant trend)_, the equation is simply $\sum_{t=1}^{n}(X_t - \alpha_0) = 0$, which gives $\alpha_0 = \bar{X}$; the best constant fit is the sample mean.

If $k = 1$ _(i.e., we have a linear trend)_, the two equations are:

$$
\sum_{t=1}^{n} (X_t - \hat{\alpha}_0 - \hat{\alpha}_1 t) = 0, \qquad
\sum_{t=1}^{n} t (X_t - \hat{\alpha}_0 - \hat{\alpha}_1 t) = 0.
$$

If we divide by $n$ and do some algebra, we get:

$$
\begin{cases}
\hat{\alpha}_0 + \frac{n+1}{2}\hat{\alpha}_1 = \bar{X},\\
\frac{n+1}{2}\hat{\alpha}_0 + \frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}\hat{\alpha}_1 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n} t X_t.
\end{cases}
$$

$$
\begin{cases}
\hat{\alpha}_1 = \frac{12}{n(n^2-1)} \sum_{t=1}^{n} t(X_t - \bar{X}),\\
\hat{\alpha}_0 = \bar{X} - \frac{n+1}{2}\hat{\alpha}_1.
\end{cases}
$$

_Note that_ this is the simple-regression formula, with $\hat{\alpha}_1 = \operatorname{Cov}(t, X)/\operatorname{Var}(t)$.

We now consider the <b>matrix formula</b> by stacking the data into $X = (X_1, \ldots, X_n)^T$ and the coefficients into $\alpha = (\alpha_0, \ldots, \alpha_k)^T$.

Let $A$ be the $n \times (k+1)$ matrix whose row $t$ is $(1, t, t^2, \ldots, t^k)$, called the <b>Vandermonde matrix</b>.

Then the model becomes $X = A\alpha + Y$, which gives:

$$
\hat{\alpha} = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T X.
$$

_(The normal equation in matrix form)_ is $\partial C/\partial\alpha = -2A^T(X-A\alpha)=0$, so $A^T A\hat{\alpha}=A^T X$, given that $n \ge k + 1$ _(i.e., we need at least as many data points as parameters, so that $A$ has full column rank and $A^T A$ is invertible)_.

_In this case,_ if the noise has zero mean, $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\alpha}] = \alpha$, i.e., the estimator is unbiased.

_Furthermore,_ in the case of white noise, $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\alpha}) = \sigma^2 (A^T A)^{-1}$.

<b>Estimating trend and seasonality together (nonparametric approach)</b>

We now consider the following model:

$$
X_t = m_t + S_t + Y_t
$$

where $m_t$ is the slowly moving trend, $S_t$ is a seasonal component with known period $d$ such that $S_{t+d} = S_t$ and sums to zero over one period, $\sum_{t=1}^{d} S_t = 0$ _(without it, you could move a constant between $m_t$ and $S_t$ and get the same $X_t$)_.

_Again,_ we assume that $m_t$ is _locally linear_.

The starting idea is that, to estimate the trend, we have to remove the seasonal component; averaging over exactly one full period does exactly this.

So, given that the seasonal period has length $d$, we can set $q$ to be the half-width of the interval, so the simple <b>equal-weight averaging</b> works as follows:

$$
\hat{m}_t = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{j=-q}^{q} X_{t-j} \qquad \text{(}d\text{ odd, } q = (d-1)/2\text{)}
$$

$$
\hat{m}_t = \frac{1}{d} \left( \frac{1}{2}X_{t-q} + \frac{1}{2}X_{t+q} + \sum_{j=-(q-1)}^{q-1} X_{t-j} \right) \qquad \text{(}d\text{ even, } q = d/2\text{)}
$$

We now focus on the case where $d$ is even _(even if the opposite case can be solved similarly)_, so we can say:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{m}_t &= \underbrace{\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{1}{2}m_{t-q} + \frac{1}{2}m_{t+q} + \sum_{j=-(q-1)}^{q-1} m_{t-j}\right)}_{=:A_t} \\
&\quad + \underbrace{\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{1}{2}S_{t-q} + \frac{1}{2}S_{t+q} + \sum_{j=-(q-1)}^{q-1} S_{t-j}\right)}_{=:B_t} \\
&\quad + \underbrace{\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{1}{2}Y_{t-q} + \frac{1}{2}Y_{t+q} + \sum_{j=-(q-1)}^{q-1} Y_{t-j}\right)}_{=:C_t}
\end{aligned}
$$

_(Note that this looks very similar to a moving average...)_

_Indeed,_ the weights $w_{-q} = w_q = \frac{1}{2d}$ and $w_j = \frac{1}{d}$ for $j \in \{-(q-1), \ldots, (q-1)\}$ satisfy

$$
\sum_{j=-q}^{q} w_j = 1 \quad \text{and} \quad \sum_{j=-q}^{q} j\, w_j = 0,
$$

so we can use the same argument as before to show that $A_t \approx a + bt \approx m_t$.

_Furthermore,_ the window covers exactly one period, and $S_{t-q} = S_{t+q}$ since they are $d$ steps apart, so the two half-weights add up to a single term and $B_t$ is the sum of $S$ over one full period, i.e., $B_t = 0$.

_Lastly,_ using the WLLN, we can show that $C_t \approx 0$ with high probability for $q$ large enough, so $\hat{m}_t \approx m_t$.

_Remark_ that $q$ is large enough if and only if $d$ is large enough. _Note also_ that we always take $d$ to be large enough because if $d_0$ is the smallest period of the seasonal component, then $d = k d_0$ is also a period for $k \in \mathbb{N}$.

<b>Nonparametric estimation of the seasonal component</b>

Suppose now that we know the trend $m_t$ exactly, so we can consider it as a _“new observation”_ and consider:

$$Z_t = X_t - m_t = s_t + Y_t$$

Let $r \ge 1$ be the largest integer such that $1 + rd \le n$. Then we have that:

$$s_1 = s_{1+d} = \cdots = s_{1+rd} = a$$

where $a$ is unknown. So we can write:

$$Z_{1+(k-1)d} = a + Y_{1+(k-1)d}$$

To estimate $a$, we then _naturally_ use the <b>empirical sample average</b>, that is:

$$
\hat a = \frac{1}{r+1}\sum_{k=1}^{r+1} Z_{1+(k-1)d}
= \frac{1}{r+1}\sum_{k=1}^{r+1} \left(X_{1+(k-1)d} - m_{1+(k-1)d}\right).
$$

_Instead,_ suppose now the trend $m_t$ is <b>unknown</b>, but we can replace it with $\hat m_t$ for $q+1 \le t \le n-q$ _(since the two-sided window must fit inside the data)_. Then $W_j$ is the average deviation from the trend at seasonal position $j$, defined as:

$$
W_j = \frac{1}{N_j}\sum_{k:\,q+1 \le j+kd \le n-q} \left(X_{j+kd} - \hat m_{j+kd}\right)
$$

$$
N_j = \#\{k : q+1 \le j+kd \le n-q\}
= \#\left\{k : \frac{q+1-j}{d} \le k \le \frac{n-q-j}{d}\right\}
$$

Then, in order to enforce the constraint $\sum_{i=1}^d s_i = 0$, the final estimator is given by:

$$
\hat{s}_j =
\begin{cases}
W_j - \frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^d W_i, & j \in \{1,\ldots,d\},\\
\hat{s}_{j-d}, & j > d.
\end{cases}
$$

_Intuition:_ the estimator averages across periods (to kill noise), subtracts the trend estimate (to isolate the season), and then re-centres the result (to fix the identifiability ambiguity between $m$ and $S$).

<b>Parametric Estimation of the seasonal component</b>

We now introduce the <b>parametric estimation of $s$</b>, with the assumption that the seasonal component follows a harmonic function, _that is:_

$$
s_t = a_0 + \sum_{j=1}^k \left(a_j \cos\left(\frac{2\pi\alpha_j t}{d}\right) + b_j \sin\left(\frac{2\pi\beta_j t}{d}\right)\right)
$$

where $k \in \mathbb{N}$, $\alpha_j, \beta_j \in \mathbb{N}$ are known, and $a_0, a_j$, and $b_j$ are unknown. To avoid trivialities, we assume that $\alpha_j, \beta_j$ are not multiples of $d$ for all $j$.

_Furthermore,_ note that

$$
\sum_{t=1}^d \cos\left(\frac{2\pi\alpha_j t}{d}\right)
= \sum_{t=1}^d \sin\left(\frac{2\pi\beta_j t}{d}\right) = 0.
$$

To prove that, simply set $c = e^{i 2\pi a/d}$, where $a \in \mathbb{N}$ is _not_ a multiple of $d$, so we have $c \neq 1$. Then $c^d = 1$, and we have that:

$$
\sum_{t=1}^d c^t = c \sum_{i=0}^{d-1} c^i = c\frac{c^d - 1}{c-1} = 0.
$$

_Note that_ in order to enforce the constraint $\sum_{i=1}^d s_i = 0$, we must impose $a_0 = 0$.

To estimate $s_t$, we can use the least-squares approach based on the <b>de-trended</b> data $\tilde X_t = X_t - \hat m_t$, with $q + 1 \le t \le n-q$. Then, the LSE estimator of $(a_1,\ldots,a_k,b_1,\ldots,b_k)$ is defined as:

$$
(\hat a_1, \ldots, \hat a_k, \hat b_1, \ldots, \hat b_k)
= \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\tilde a_1,\ldots,\tilde a_k,\tilde b_1,\ldots,\tilde b_k}
\sum_{t=q+1}^{n-q}
\left(\tilde X_t - \sum_{j=1}^k \left(\tilde a_j \cos\left(\frac{2\pi\alpha_j t}{d}\right) + \tilde b_j \sin\left(\frac{2\pi\beta_j t}{d}\right)\right)\right)^2.
$$

<b>Estimating by differencing</b>

Suppose now the model is $X_t = m_t + Y_t$, with $\mathbb{E}[Y_t] = 0$. We want to eliminate $m_t$ _(the trend component)_ instead of estimating it, so we apply an operator that sends a polynomial trend into a constant _(or to zero)_. In particular, we then define:
- $B$, the <b>backward shift</b> operator, which moves the series back by one step, defined as:

    $$BX_t = X_{t-1}$$
- $\nabla$, the <b>lag-1 differencing operator</b>, which compares each value with the previous one _(the discrete analogue of a derivative measuring the change per step)_, defined as:

    $$\nabla X_t = X_t - X_{t-1} = (1-B)X_t$$

where $1 = B^0$ is the <b>identity operator</b>.

For $j \ge 2$, we can define recursively:
- $$B^j = B \circ B^{j-1} \implies B^j X_t = X_{t-j}$$
- $$\nabla^j = \nabla \circ \nabla^{j-1} \implies \nabla^j X_t = (1 - B)^j X_t$$

We can also show that:

$$
\nabla^j = (1-B)^j = \sum_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^k \binom{j}{k}B^k
\implies \nabla^j X_t = \sum_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^k\binom{j}{k}X_{t-k}.
$$

_Intuition:_ each application of $\nabla$ lowers the degree by one, so after $j$ applications, a degree-$j$ polynomial is left as a coefficient, and only the leading coefficient survives, multiplied by $j!$ _(the same factor that appears when we differentiate $t^j$ $j$ times)_.

_Note also that_:
- <b>Stationarity is preserved</b>, i.e., if $Y_t$ is stationary, then $\nabla^j Y_t$ is a linear combination of shifted copies of $Y_t$ and is therefore stationary too. $\implies$ The differenced series can therefore be managed with the usual stationary-friendly tools.
- <b>Zero mean</b>, i.e., since $\mathbb{E}[Y_t] = 0$, we get $\mathbb{E}[\nabla^k X_t] = k!\,a_k$, so $a_k$ can be estimated from the sample mean of the differenced series.


We also have the following properties:

- $\nabla X_t = X_t - X_{t-1} = \frac{X_t - X_{t-1}}{t - (t-1)}$.
- If $m(t) = a_0 + a_1 t + \cdots + a_k t^k$ with $t \in \mathbb{Z}$, then $\nabla^k m(t) = k!\,a_k$.
- If $m_t = a_0 + a_1 t + \cdots + a_k t^k$ and $X_t = m_t + Y_t$ with $t \in \mathbb{Z}$, then $\nabla^k X_t = k!\,a_k + \nabla^k Y_t$.
- If $\{Y_t\}_{t \in \mathbb{Z}}$ is stationary, then $\{\nabla^k Y_t\}_{t \in \mathbb{Z}}$ is also stationary.
