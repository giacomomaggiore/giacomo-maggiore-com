### <b>Introduction to Time Series</b>

<b>Time Series Definition</b>

A time series is a <b>stochastic process </b> indexed by time $t \in T \subseteq \R$.

Examples _(they are all continuous!):_
- $T = [0,1]$: Brownian bridge from $(0,0)$ to $(1,0)$, where the pair notation denotes $(\text{time}, \text{value}) = (t, X_t)$
- $T = [0, + \infty]$: Brownian motion starting at $(0,0)$
- $T = \R$: two-sided Brownian motion
- $T = \N_0$, or $T = \Z$: discrete random walk.

We use the notation $\{X_t\}_{t \in T}$ to denote a random variable and the notation $\{x_t\}_{t \in T}$ to denote realizations of that variable.

A <b>time series model</b> is either the specification of:
- all joint distributions $f(\{X_t\}_{t \in T})$ (i.e., the model provides $\Pr(X_{t_1} \le x_{t_1}, \ldots, X_{t_k} \le x_{t_k})$ for all $k \in \N$, $t_1, \ldots, t_k$, and $x_1, \ldots, x_k \in \R$)
- all their means and covariances $(\mathbb{E}(X_t), \text{Cov}(X_t, X_p))$ for all $t, p \in T$

<br/>
<b>White noise</b>

The i.i.d. noise $\{X_t\}_{t \in T}$ is a sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables such that $\mathbb{E}(X_t) = 0$ for all $t$. <br/>In general, we also assume that $\mathbb{E}[X_t^2] = \text{Var}(X_t) < \infty$ for all $t$. _Note that in this case $\mathbb{E}[X_t^2]$ does not depend on $t$._

<b>Rademacher Process</b>

Consider $\{X_t\}_{t \in T}$ such that $\Pr(X_t = -1) = \Pr(X_t = 1) = 1/2$ for all $t$.

If $\{B_t\}_t \sim_{\mathrm{iid}} \text{Bernoulli}(1/2)$, then:
- $\{X_t\}_t = 2\{B_t\}_t - 1$
- $\text{Var}(X_t) = 4\text{Var}(B_t) = 1$ _(note that $X_t^2 = 1$ and $\mathbb{E}(X_t) = 0$)_

<b>White noise</b>

${X_t}_t$ is a sequence of centered and uncorrelated random variables. More specifically, $\mathbb{E}(X_t) = 0$, $\mathbb{E}(X_t^2) < \infty$, and $\text{Cov}(X_t, X_s) = 0$ for all $t \neq s$.

- Note that i.i.d. noise such that $\mathbb{E}(X_t^2) < \infty$ is also white noise.
- The term "white" is used by analogy with white light. Indeed, white noise with constant variance $v^2$ has a flat spectral density given by $f(\lambda) = v^2 / 2\pi$, with $\lambda \in [-\pi, \pi]$ (this means that all time-series frequencies are present).

<b>Random walk</b>

Let ${X_t}_t$ be i.i.d. noise such that $\mathbb{E}(X_t^2) < \infty$ for all $t \in \N$.

${S_t}_{t \in \N_0}$ is a random walk if $S_0 = 0$ and $S_t = \sum_{i=1}^t X_i$ for all $t \ge 1$.


If ${X_t}_{t \in \N_0}$ is a Rademacher process, then ${S_t}_{t \in \N_0}$ is called a <b>symmetric random walk</b>, with $\mathbb{E}(S_t) = \sum_{i=1}^t \mathbb{E}(X_i) = 0$.

<b>Time Series with trend</b>

Let ${Y_t}_t$ be a zero-mean time series and ${m_t}_t$ be a deterministic (non-random) sequence. <br/>Consider $X_t = Y_t + m_t$ for all $t \in T$.

Then $\mathbb{E}[X_t] = m_t$ is called the <b>trend</b> of ${X_t}_t$.

<b>Time Series with seasonality</b>

Given a zero-mean time series $\{Y_t\}_t$ and a function $t \mapsto p_t$ such that $p_t$ is periodic with known period $d$ (i.e., $p_{t+d} = p_t$).

Consider the time series $X_t = Y_t + p_t$ for all $t \in T$. Then ${p_t}_t$ is called the <b>seasonal component</b> of ${Y_t}_t$.

For example, we could have the seasonal component in the following forms:

$$
p_t = a_0 + \sum_{j=1}^k \left(a_j \cos\left(\frac{2 \pi \alpha_j t}{d}\right) + b_j \sin\left(\frac{2 \pi \beta_j t}{d}\right)\right)
$$

with known integers $k \ge 1$, $\alpha_j, \beta_j > 0$, and unknown $a_0, a_j, b_j$ for all $1 \le j \le k$.

<b>Time series with both trend and seasonal component</b>

$$X_t = m_t + p_t + Y_t, \forall t \in T$$

with ${m_t}_t$ the trend, ${p_t}_t$ the seasonal component, and ${Y_t}_t$ a zero-mean time series.

### <b>Stationarity and auto-covariance function (ACVF)</b>

<b>Stationarity</b>

A time series $\{X_t\}_t$ is said to be strictly (strongly) stationary if, for all $n \ge 1$ and $h \in \Z$, it holds that:

$$
(X_1, \ldots, X_n) \overset{d}{=} (X_{1+h}, \ldots, X_{n+h})
$$

i.e., the stochastic behavior of $\{X_t\}_t$ remains unchanged for _any_ time shift.

Let $\{X_t\}_t$ be a time series.

- The mean function of $\{X_t\}_t$ is defined as:

$$
\mu_x(t) = \mathbb{E}(X_t), \forall t \in T
$$

- The covariance function of $\{X_t\}_t$ is defined as:
$$
\begin{align*}
\gamma_x(r,p) &= \text{Cov}(X_r, X_p) = \\
&= \mathbb{E}[(X_r - \mu_x(r))(X_p - \mu_x(p))] \\
&= \mathbb{E}[X_r X_p] - \mu_x(r)\mu_x(p)
\end{align*}
$$ 

provided that $\mathbb{E}[|X_r X_p|] < \infty$ and $\mathbb{E}[X_r] < \infty$ for all $r,p$.

On the other hand, $\{X_t\}_t$ is said to be (weakly) stationary if $\mathbb{E}[X_t^2] < \infty$ for all $t$, and:
- $\mu_x(t)$ is costant in $t$
- $\gamma_x (t, t+h)$ depends only on $h$
