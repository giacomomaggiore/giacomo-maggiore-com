# Beijing

Beijing has been settled for roughly 3,000 years. Its historic core follows a north-south Central Axis, a UNESCO World Heritage site since 2024. Developed from the Yuan dynasty onward, the axis organizes gates, ceremonial spaces, temples, palaces, and gardens with almost unreasonable confidence in symmetry. The Forbidden City is its imperial centre.

> **Before you go:** ticket rules, security procedures, opening hours, cable-car operation, and university access change. Check official sources shortly before visiting; this guide intentionally avoids fixed booking windows.

## Places

### Forbidden City (Palace Museum, Gugong)

Enter from the south, follow the central sequence of courts northward, then continue to Jingshan Park. Built between 1406 and 1420, it was the centre of imperial power for 505 years and witnessed 24 Ming and Qing emperors. UNESCO inscribed it in 1987.

- The complex has nearly 10,000 rooms. The popular count of 9,999-and-a-half is traditional symbolism: the palace was meant to remain below heaven's mythical 10,000 rooms. Even emperors had a ceiling.
- The Hall of Supreme Harmony is the largest ceremonial hall; its throne was used for major state occasions, not everyday government.
- The shift from vast public courts to residential palaces is political theatre in timber, stone, and yellow roof tiles.
- Use the [Palace Museum official site](https://intl.dpm.org.cn/index.html?l=en) for current real-name ticketing.

### Jingshan Park

Immediately north of the Forbidden City, Jingshan gives the clearest view of the palace within the city. Its artificial hill was made partly from earth excavated for palace moats and waterworks, then used as an auspicious northern backdrop.

- Climb to Wanchun Pavilion for the view straight down the Central Axis over the palace roofs.
- Near sunset is ideal, but allow time for crowds and the descent before closing.
- Curiosity: moat spoil became one of the city's great viewing platforms. Landscaping with consequences.

### Wangfujing and St. Joseph's Church (Dongtang)

Wangfujing is a long-established commercial street, usually liveliest in the evening. It is useful for a central walk, not an archaeological experience.

- St. Joseph's Church traces its origins to 1655. The present Romanesque-revival building is a twentieth-century rebuilding after earlier churches were damaged or destroyed.
- Treat nearby snack streets as changing retail, not as a guaranteed historic night market.

### Tiananmen Square and Tiananmen Gate

Tiananmen Square is one of the world's largest public squares, at about 440,000 square metres. Tiananmen, the Gate of Heavenly Peace, is the ceremonial gateway toward the Forbidden City and displays Mao Zedong's portrait.

- Security and access controls can be substantial. Bring identification and check current official entry requirements.
- The flag-raising ceremony occurs at sunrise, so its time changes through the year. Arrive early; the square does not reward last-minute optimism.
- Mao Zedong Memorial Hall has separate opening arrangements. Verify them on the day.

### Temple of Heaven (Tiantan)

Completed with the Forbidden City in 1420 and rebuilt into its current arrangement by 1749, this 273-hectare complex is the most complete surviving imperial sacrificial complex in China. It became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1998.

- Here the emperor, as the "Son of Heaven," offered sacrifices and prayed for good harvests: crop policy, cosmic legitimacy, and court ceremony sharing one address.
- The Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests is the circular, triple-roofed landmark. Its timber frame uses traditional joinery rather than iron nails.
- Follow the route between the Circular Mound Altar, Imperial Vault of Heaven, and hall. Its geometry contrasts round heaven with square earth.
- Early morning is good for the park atmosphere. Residents may be exercising, dancing, making music, playing cards, or talking; these are everyday activities, not a scheduled performance.

### Summer Palace (Yiheyuan)

The Summer Palace was first built in 1750, largely destroyed in 1860, and reconstructed from 1886 for the Qing court, including Empress Dowager Cixi. It has been UNESCO-listed since 1998.

- Kunming Lake and Longevity Hill form the garden's framework. The lake was shaped from an earlier Yuan-dynasty reservoir, not simply dug as an ornamental pond with imperial branding.
- The Long Corridor is about 728 metres long and carries thousands of painted panels: a very elegant solution to a scenic walk in bad weather.
- Climb Longevity Hill, then leave time for the Seventeen-Arch Bridge and the west side of the garden.

### Lama Temple (Yonghegong)

Yonghegong is Beijing's best-known Tibetan Buddhist monastery. Founded in 1694 as a residence for a Qing prince, it became an imperial palace before its conversion into a lamasery in 1744.

- It remains active. Dress respectfully, do not photograph where prohibited, and give worshippers space.
- The famous Maitreya statue is 18 metres tall and carved from one trunk of white sandalwood. It is enormous, indoors, and somehow still not the temple's only visual surprise.

### Great Wall: Mutianyu and Jiankou

The Great Wall is a system built and rebuilt from the third century BCE to the seventeenth century CE. UNESCO gives the whole system a length of more than 20,000 kilometres and inscribed it in 1987. It is not one continuous wall, and it is not visible from the Moon with the naked eye.

- **Mutianyu:** a restored Ming section in Huairou District, with cable-car access and a well-managed visitor route. It is the sensible choice for most first visits. Check current conditions on the [official Mutianyu site](https://en.mutianyugreatwall.com/).
- **Jiankou:** a largely unrestored, steep, hazardous section. It is not an ordinary self-guided hike; access can be restricted. Use a qualified guide only where access is legal and appropriate.
- A Jiankou-Mutianyu trek is sometimes marketed, but duration, legality, and safety depend on route and conditions. Do not rely on a generic four-to-five-hour estimate.

### 798 Art District

The 798 Art District occupies part of the former 718 Joint Factory complex, a 1950s industrial project built with East German technical assistance. Its sawtooth roofs and huge factory halls are part of the point.

- From the early 2000s, artists, galleries, studios, shops, and cafes adapted the industrial buildings.
- Look at the factory signage and roof trusses as carefully as the exhibitions. The district's best trick is making heavy industry feel suited to contemporary art.
- Programmes and evening activity vary; check individual galleries.

### Nanluoguxiang

Nanluoguxiang is an approximately 800-metre hutong street whose layout dates to the Yuan dynasty. It is popular and heavily commercial, but still a useful introduction to the alley network.

- The main lane is packed with shops and snacks. Side streets are quieter but residential: explore respectfully, not as an open-air set.
- A **siheyuan** is the traditional courtyard-house pattern organized around a central yard.
- Curiosity: its popularity is exactly why it is easy to visit and least likely to feel undiscovered.

### Shichahai: Qianhai, Houhai, and Xihai

Shichahai is a historic water-and-hutong district around three connected lakes: Qianhai, Houhai, and Xihai. Its water links were part of Beijing's wider historic canal and transport landscape.

- Wander by day for courtyard-house streets; visit the lakefront in the evening for restaurants and bars.
- Winter ice activities may operate only in designated, officially managed areas. Never assume a frozen lake is safe to walk or skate on.

### Wudaoying Hutong

Wudaoying is a compact hutong near the Lama Temple with independent cafes, shops, and restaurants. It is more curated than hidden, but usually calmer than Nanluoguxiang. Pair it with the Lama Temple and nearby Guijie for an easy north-east Beijing evening.

### Yangmeizhu Xiejie

Yangmeizhu Xiejie is a historic slanting street in the Dashilar area south-west of Tiananmen, with strong connections to late-Qing and Republican-era publishing, theatre, and commerce.

- Today it mixes restoration projects, design shops, crafts, and cafes. The pleasure is in narrow plots, signboards, and a street that refuses to be perfectly straight. Cities need a little mischief.

### Guijie (Ghost Street)

Guijie is a Dongzhimen restaurant street known for late-night dining. Its name is commonly rendered as "Ghost Street," though it is more illuminated signs and crustaceans than the supernatural.

- Look for Sichuan-style spicy crayfish, hot pot, and malatang. Spice is negotiable; bravado is optional.
- Opening hours and quality vary. Pick a busy restaurant with clear prices and recent reviews.

### Niujie

Niujie is Beijing's historic Hui Muslim neighbourhood, known for northern Chinese Muslim lamb dishes, breads, and sweets.

- The Niujie Mosque dates to 996, though its buildings have changed and been restored over centuries. It is commonly described as Beijing's oldest mosque.
- Respect religious-site access rules. Non-Muslim visitors may be limited to exterior viewing or particular areas.
- Try **chuanr** (seasoned lamb skewers), baked breads, and halal pastries; ask about ingredients if dietary requirements matter.

### Sanyuanli Market

Sanyuanli is a working food market known for local produce, seafood, meat, imported ingredients, and a broad mix of shoppers.

- It is less a polished attraction than a window onto how a large city eats. Visit in the morning and bring a translation app; availability changes with seasons and supply chains.

## Parks, Universities, Sport, and Tech

### Early-morning parks

Temple of Heaven, Jingshan, and Beihai Park are good choices for daily city life early in the day. People may practise tai chi, dancing, calligraphy, music, or exercise; observe without treating participants as tourist entertainment.

### Haidian, Wudaokou, Peking University, and Tsinghua University

Haidian is Beijing's major university and technology district. Wudaokou and Chengfu Road are good bases for student-focused restaurants and cafes.

- Peking University has a lakeside campus and historic architecture; Tsinghua combines historic buildings with a large modern research-university setting.
- Visitor access can be restricted or require advance registration. Confirm through each university's official visitor channel; WeChat booking may be required, but is not universal.

### Olympic Forest Park and Olympic Green

Olympic Forest Park was created for the 2008 Beijing Olympics and covers roughly 680 hectares. It has running and walking routes, landscaped water, and links to the Olympic Green.

- The Bird's Nest and Water Cube are nearby, though views depend on the part of the park and foliage. It is a practical choice for a long run or walk rather than an attraction requiring a tightly choreographed visit.

### Zhongguancun

Zhongguancun is often called China's "Silicon Valley," a useful but imperfect shorthand. It is a large science-and-technology ecosystem near universities and research institutes, with companies, incubators, government-backed parks, and commercial districts.

- Baidu, Xiaomi, and Lenovo have significant Beijing connections, but walking through the area will not reveal an entire innovation system by osmosis.
- Notice the proximity of universities, research institutes, and technology businesses, then look for a current public exhibition or talk.

## Context: Economy, Technology, and Data

Beijing is a service-heavy municipal economy and an important centre for research, higher education, finance, culture, and technology. Exact sector shares, tax incentives, and policy priorities belong to current municipal or national documents, not a timeless travel note.

- **Technology:** AI, robotics, semiconductors, life sciences, 6G, and quantum information feature in Chinese and Beijing policy discussions. They are priorities, not guarantees of commercial success or one unified strategy.
- **Research and commercialization:** universities, private companies, state-owned enterprises, and government programmes can all be involved. A blanket claim that innovation happens mainly through joint ventures is not supportable.
- **PIPL and data:** China's Personal Information Protection Law regulates personal-information processing and cross-border transfers. It does not require every item of data collected in China to remain in China; requirements depend on the data, processor, and legal route.
- **Digital identity:** do not claim that China already has one system aggregating every person's health, financial, social, and professional profile. This broad claim is not established by the sources used here.
- **e-CNY:** the e-CNY is a central-bank digital currency used in pilots and expanding use cases. Claims of universal real-time surveillance, automatic tax enforcement, or bypassing every international payment channel go beyond what this guide can verify.

## China Deep Dive: Society, Technology, and the State

These subjects are connected by one useful question: how do the Party-state, markets, technology, and private life interact? The answer is rarely "the state does everything" or "the market is free." China combines large private firms and consumer markets with unusually strong state direction, regulation, and political control.

### Sport in China

Sport has a more explicit national and strategic dimension in China than it typically does in Europe. It can serve international prestige, public health, national identity, education, local development, and a growing consumer-services economy.

- The traditional elite-sport model is strongly top-down: talent identification, specialist sports schools, provincial teams, and national selection. It helped China build sustained strength in table tennis, badminton, diving, gymnastics, weightlifting, and shooting, where long-term coaching systems can matter enormously.
- This does not mean every athlete is recruited by the state or that leisure sport is insignificant. Mass participation, commercial fitness, outdoor sports, sporting goods, and event tourism have expanded substantially alongside elite sport.
- A better contrast is not "Chinese sport is political; European sport is not." European governments also fund sport and pursue prestige. The difference is the degree of centralized planning and the historic weight of international medals in Chinese state policy.
- For a visitor, public parks, basketball courts, badminton halls, dance groups, and running clubs show the mass-participation side more clearly than an Olympic-medal table.

### Running in China

Road running has become a conspicuous urban lifestyle and event-tourism phenomenon. Major marathons often use lotteries because demand can exceed capacity, but participation figures vary sharply by organizer, year, and whether a count means registrations, finishers, or entries across several distances. Quote a specific number only with its original report and definition.

- **Why it travels well:** entry costs can be relatively low, routes make cities legible, and progress is easy to measure with watches and apps.
- **Status and tourism:** a well-known marathon can be a personal achievement, a social post, and a short city break at once. This is not uniquely Chinese, but the scale of Chinese cities makes the city-branding effect striking.
- **Digital layer:** a run often becomes data, then a post, then a recommendation. WeChat groups, Xiaohongshu posts, Douyin videos, brand communities, and fitness apps can all organize the surrounding social life.
- **Practical caution:** air quality, heat, traffic, track access, and event registration rules can matter more than motivation. In Beijing, choose parks, the Olympic Forest Park, or official routes, and check current local conditions.

### Social Media: China and Europe

The most useful comparison is not that China has "no internet," but that it has a large, heavily regulated, Chinese-language digital ecosystem with different dominant platforms, rules, and habits.

| Function | Typical European/global services | Major services used in mainland China |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Messaging and services | WhatsApp, Messenger, banking apps | WeChat |
| Short video | TikTok, YouTube Shorts | Douyin, Kuaishou |
| Discovery and lifestyle recommendations | Google, Instagram, Reddit, Maps | Xiaohongshu, Douyin, Dianping, Amap |
| Search | Google | Baidu and in-app search |
| Social commerce | Instagram, Amazon, marketplaces | Taobao, JD, Xiaohongshu, Douyin |

- **WeChat is the key difference.** It combines messaging, groups, official accounts, payments, mini-programs, customer service, bookings, and many local services. It is best understood as an operating layer for daily transactions, not merely as a WhatsApp equivalent.
- **Xiaohongshu (Little Red Book)** is influential for experience-led discovery: restaurants, travel, beauty, apartments, products, and fitness. Searching for peer recommendations inside an app can substitute for the European habit of using a web search engine plus reviews.
- **Differences from Europe are structural, not total.** Europeans also use super-app functions, QR payments, platform commerce, and algorithmic feeds. The Chinese version is generally more integrated, mobile-first, local-platform-centric, and subject to different state requirements.
- Do not infer that every user has the same app mix or that platform rankings are permanent. Adoption changes quickly and differs by age, city, occupation, and income.

### The Great Firewall

"Great Firewall" is informal shorthand for a broader system of technical filtering, legal obligations, platform governance, content moderation, real-name rules, and enforcement. It is not one literal wall and it does not operate with perfect consistency.

- Many global services, including Google, YouTube, Facebook, Instagram, X, and numerous foreign news sites, are routinely inaccessible from mainland China without tools that may themselves carry legal or practical risk.
- Technical measures can include DNS interference, IP blocking, connection disruption, and filtering. The broader system also relies on platforms removing prohibited content and users anticipating what is risky to say.
- The state frames these measures in terms of cybersecurity, sovereignty, fraud prevention, public order, and information governance. Rights organizations argue that the system suppresses lawful expression, access to information, privacy, and political organization. Both are descriptions of stated policy and observed effects, not interchangeable claims.
- The domestic-platform outcome matters economically: restrictions on global competitors helped create space for Chinese firms to build scale and network effects. It would be too simple, however, to treat the firewall as the sole cause of Baidu, Tencent, Alibaba, ByteDance, or Xiaohongshu; market size, capital, entrepreneurship, regulation, and product execution also mattered.
- Freedom House rated China **9/100** in its 2025 *Freedom on the Net* assessment and described pervasive state power to censor and manipulate online content. This is an independent assessment, not an official Chinese government measure.

### Being Gay in China: Law, Family, and Public Space

Separate the legal, social, and political questions. They lead to different answers.

- **Legal status:** consensual same-sex sexual activity is not a crime in China. It was decriminalized in 1997 and removed from the Chinese classification of mental disorders in 2001.
- **Equality:** China does not recognize same-sex marriage nationally and does not provide comprehensive, orientation-specific anti-discrimination protection. Same-sex couples therefore do not have the same family-law position as married heterosexual couples.
- **Social life:** experiences vary widely. Large cities have LGBTQ people, friendships, venues, online communities, and supportive families; stigma and pressure also remain real. Avoid treating either acceptance or hostility as universal.
- **Family expectations:** filial responsibility, marriage, children, and care for parents can be powerful expectations. For some people, the pressure is less an explicit rejection of sexuality than a conflict between identity and a family script that expects marriage and descendants. This context helps explain, but does not excuse, coercive expectations or discrimination.
- **Public and political space:** public LGBTQ organizing and visibility have faced significant restrictions, including censorship and closures of campus or online groups reported by rights organizations. Private life, social acceptance, and official tolerance are not the same thing.
- Travel rule: do not ask strangers intrusive questions about sexuality, avoid photographing people at community venues without consent, and do not assume a visible Pride-style public scene will be available.

### State-Owned Enterprises and the Role of the State

China is neither a classic command economy nor a liberal market economy. A useful short description is **a market economy under strong Party-state direction**.

- An **SOE** is an enterprise ultimately owned or controlled by the state. China has central SOEs supervised at the national level as well as provincial and municipal SOEs.
- The state is especially prominent in sectors treated as strategic or foundational: energy, power grids, finance, telecommunications, transport, defence, natural resources, and parts of advanced manufacturing and infrastructure.
- China also has globally important private or nominally private firms, including Huawei, Tencent, Alibaba, BYD, Xiaomi, and ByteDance. "China equals SOEs" is false.
- A better model is:

```text
Private firms + SOEs + state-owned banks + industrial policy + regulation + Party leadership
```

- State direction can coordinate credit, research, procurement, infrastructure, standards, and regulation around national priorities. That can accelerate investment in areas such as batteries, renewables, rail, and digital infrastructure.
- The trade-off is potential misallocation: preferential finance, guarantees, market access, or soft budget constraints can sustain less productive investment and leave private firms on an uneven playing field. This is a recurring concern in OECD and IMF analysis.

### The Party, the State, and Firms

Do not picture the system as a simple line from "government" to "companies." The Chinese Communist Party (CCP), the state administration, financial institutions, SOEs, regulators, local governments, and private firms overlap in different ways.

```text
CCP leadership
	|
State administration, regulation, and policy goals
	|
Banks, SOEs, local governments, and private firms
	|
Investment, employment, technology, and consumer life
```

- The CCP's role extends beyond owning companies: it shapes regulation, senior appointments in state firms, finance, industrial priorities, national-security obligations, and the operating environment for private firms.
- Party organizations exist within many companies, including private ones, but their practical influence varies by firm, sector, locality, and situation. Their existence alone does not show who makes every commercial decision.
- Central SOEs have commercial goals and also explicit strategic, security, and policy roles. The distinction matters when analysing an investment, an industry, or a city's development plan.

### A Working Mental Model

Running, social media, internet controls, LGBTQ public space, and SOEs are not isolated topics. They show different parts of one pattern: economic development, technology, social life, and information are often treated as interconnected questions of national development and security.

Europe is diverse and should not be reduced to one model, but European political culture generally draws firmer conceptual boundaries between state, market, and civil society. In China, these boundaries are more permeable and the Party's coordinating role is more explicit.
